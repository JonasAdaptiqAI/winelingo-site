What Wine Goes With Osso Buco?

A long braise finished with raw lemon and parsley. That last part changes everything.

By Jonas Egeskov · Updated 6 September 2026

Barbera or Nebbiolo — Piedmontese, high in acid. The gremolata is why acidity matters more here than in most braises.

Osso buco is veal shin braised slowly with wine, stock and vegetables until the meat is falling from the bone and the marrow has softened inside it. In the Milanese version it is finished with gremolata — raw lemon zest, garlic and parsley — scattered over at the last moment. That raw finish on top of a long braise is the whole pairing question.

Rich braise, sharp finish

The braise itself is gelatinous and deeply savoury, with rendered marrow adding real richness. That much would take a fairly structured red on its own.

The gremolata pulls the opposite way. Raw lemon zest raises the acidity sharply, and raw garlic flattens a delicate wine and turns an oaked one bitter. The dish is deliberately built this way — the sharpness is there to cut the richness — and the wine has to survive both halves.

The answer is a wine with high acidity to match the lemon, moderate tannin the braise can soften, and no new oak for the garlic to turn bitter.

What to actually open

Barbera. The highest acidity of the everyday Piedmontese reds and naturally light in tannin. It meets the gremolata head-on and does not need the fat to prop it up. The safest answer.

Nebbiolo. More serious and more tannic, but with acidity to match — a Barolo or Barbaresco with some age is a great pairing, and a young one is not, because the tannin has nothing to soften it beyond the marrow.

A white, genuinely. Milanese cooking drinks white as often as red, and a full Soave or a textured Vermentino handles veal and lemon well. Veal is pale meat; this is less eccentric than it sounds.

Risotto alla milanese, and the version without gremolata

Osso buco is traditionally served on saffron risotto, and saffron has a faintly bitter, metallic edge that clashes with oak. One more reason to keep the wine unoaked or barely oaked.

Some versions skip the gremolata, or use a tomato-heavy braise instead. Without the raw lemon the dish is rounder and less sharp, and a slightly fuller red — a Valpolicella Ripasso, or a Montepulciano — sits comfortably.

Common questions

What wine goes with osso buco?

Barbera is the safest answer — high acid, light tannin, Piedmontese. An aged Nebbiolo is the grander option.

Why does gremolata matter?

It is raw lemon zest and garlic scattered on at the end. The lemon raises the acidity the wine must match, and raw garlic turns oaked wine bitter — so the wine needs sharpness and no new oak.

Does white wine work with osso buco?

Yes, more than people expect. Veal is pale meat and Milanese cooking drinks white freely — a full Soave from Garganega or a textured Vermentino handles the lemon well.

Can I use a young Barolo?

Better aged. Young Nebbiolo is very tannic and there is little fat in a veal shin beyond the marrow to soften it. With age the tannin settles and the pairing is excellent.
