What Wine Goes With Coq au Vin?

The sauce is already wine. That makes this the easiest pairing on the site — with one catch.

Red Burgundy, or the same wine you cooked with. When a dish is braised in wine, the sauce and the glass should be relatives.

Coq au vin is chicken braised slowly in red wine with lardons, mushrooms and small onions. Because the sauce is reduced wine, most of the usual pairing arithmetic is already done for you: whatever character the cooking wine had is concentrated on the plate, and a glass of something similar reads as a continuation rather than a contrast.

Cook with what you drink — with limits

The old advice to cook with the wine you intend to drink is sound, but not for the romantic reason usually given. It is simply that reduction concentrates. Simmer a wine for two hours and its acidity, its tannin and any oak all intensify while the alcohol boils off. Whatever was slightly harsh in the bottle becomes distinctly harsh in the sauce.

So the real rule is narrower: cook with a decent, unoaked, moderately tannic red — and then drink one like it. What you must not do is cook with something thin and drink something grand, because the sauce will make the good bottle taste hollow by comparison.

What to actually open

Red Burgundy is the regional answer and the traditional one — the dish is Burgundian, and Pinot Noir has the acidity and the earthy, mushroomy quality that meets the bacon and the mushrooms in the pot. A village wine is plenty.

Beaujolais from one of the ten crus if Burgundy is out of reach. Morgon in particular takes on a savoury, meaty depth with a few years that suits this dish very well.

There is also a legitimate white version — coq au vin jaune, made with the oxidative white wine of the Jura and cream. If that is what you have cooked, drink Jura Savagnin or a nutty older Chardonnay, not a red.

What the lardons do

The bacon is doing more work than it appears. Salt makes wine taste fruitier and softer, so a red that seems a little austere on its own fills out beside this dish. It also means you can drink something with more acidity than you might expect and still find it generous.

Serve the wine cool rather than warm. Coq au vin arrives hot and rich, and a red at proper cellar temperature tastes fresh against it where the same bottle at room temperature tastes heavy and alcoholic.

Common questions

What wine goes with coq au vin?

Red Burgundy is the regional and traditional answer, or a Beaujolais cru. Ideally something close to what you braised the chicken in.

Should I really cook with the wine I drink?

Roughly, yes — but the reason is practical. Reduction concentrates acidity, tannin and oak while the alcohol boils off, so anything harsh in the bottle becomes harsher in the sauce. Cook with a decent unoaked red and drink one like it.

Can I use an expensive bottle for the sauce?

There is no point. Long simmering destroys the delicate aromatics you paid for and concentrates only the structure. Save the good bottle for the glass.

What about coq au vin jaune?

That is the white version, made with the oxidative wine of the Jura and cream. Drink Jura Savagnin or a nutty older Chardonnay with it, not a red.
