What Wine Goes With Lobster and Crab?

Forget the shellfish for a second. The wine has to get past the butter first.

An oaked Chardonnay, and a white Burgundy from Meursault if the occasion justifies it.

The useful way to think about this dish is to stop thinking about the shellfish. Steamed lobster on its own is delicate and faintly sweet, and almost any wine with texture would swamp it. Add drawn butter and the whole plate changes: it becomes rich, slick and coating. You are not pairing lobster. You are pairing butter, and the lobster only has to survive whatever you chose.

You are pairing the butter

Drawn butter asks a wine for two things that pull against each other. It wants body, because a lean wine simply vanishes under that much fat. It also wants acidity, or the fourth mouthful is cloying. Barrel-fermented Chardonnay is the standing answer for a fairly dull reason: few other whites reliably arrive with both. The oak supplies weight; the acidity underneath does the actual clearing.

That also explains the two things not to pour. Razor-sharp, bone-dry whites — Muscadet, Sancerre, Vinho Verde — are magnificent with raw oysters and completely lost against butter, because there is nothing in them to answer the fat. And red wine is a poor idea for a reason that has nothing to do with either: shellfish meat is faintly sweet, sweetness pushes tannin towards bitter and drying, and lobster has none of the oiliness that lets salmon get away with a light red.

The shortlist, in ascending order of nerve

Steamed or butter-poached, the oaked Chardonnay is the benchmark and it earns the cliché. White Burgundy from Meursault is the reference point — a village that has never been awarded a single grand cru, incidentally, despite producing the most famous dry whites in France. Perrières is still, officially, a premier cru.

With a whole lobster, Champagne may quietly be the better bottle: the bubbles scrub butter off the palate in a way no still wine manages, and a vintage or lees-aged cuvée has the toasty depth to keep up. This is the one occasion where spending more on the fizz genuinely shows.

Viognier is the outsider. In Condrieu, in the northern Rhône, the appellation permits no other grape at all, and the wine comes out broad, low in acidity and scented with apricot and honeysuckle. It flatters lobster served with something sweet or aromatic — vanilla butter, saffron, a bisque — and falls apart against plain lemon. The appellation nearly died: fewer than a dozen hectares were still worked in the 1960s, the terraces abandoned for cherries and apricots, and the figure is well over 150 today.

Chenin Blanc is the value answer. A dry Vouvray sec has a waxy, quince-like weight with Loire acidity running underneath it: richness and cut in one bottle, at a fraction of what Meursault costs.

Crab is really two ingredients

The white meat from the claws and legs is lean, flaky and sweet. The brown meat from the body cavity is fatty, salty and almost pâté-like, and it carries most of the flavour. The ratio on your plate, not the word “crab”, decides the wine.

A whole dressed crab, brown meat and all, is a richer dish than a lobster tail and wants the oaked Chardonnay or the Champagne. A pot of picked white meat with mayonnaise is lighter, and a dry Chenin Blanc or a crisp unoaked white suits it far better. Get it the wrong way round and one of the two disappears.

Then serve it warm enough to taste. An oaked white straight from the fridge is mute — the texture you paid for only shows at 10–13°C (50–55°F), so give it fifteen minutes out. Champagne wants 6–10°C (43–50°F), and a serious bottle improves as it warms towards the top of that band. Our serving temperature guide lists the band for everything else.

FAQ

Questions & answers

What wine goes best with lobster?

An oaked Chardonnay — a white Burgundy such as Meursault is the classic. Its creamy weight matches the drawn butter while the acidity underneath stops the dish feeling heavy.

Does Champagne go with lobster?

Very well, and with a whole buttered lobster it can beat the Chardonnay. The bubbles clear the butter between mouthfuls, and a vintage or lees-aged cuvée has the toasty depth to match the sweet meat.

What wine goes with crab?

It depends on the meat. Dressed crab with plenty of rich brown meat needs an oaked Chardonnay or Champagne; picked white claw meat with mayonnaise is lighter and suits a dry Chenin Blanc or a crisp unoaked white.

Is there any red wine that suits lobster?

Almost none. The sweetness of the meat turns tannin bitter and drying, and lobster lacks the oiliness that lets a fish like salmon carry a light red. Stay with white or sparkling.
