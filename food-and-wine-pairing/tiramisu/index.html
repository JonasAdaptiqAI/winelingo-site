What Wine Goes With Tiramisu?

Coffee, cocoa and mascarpone. The wine must out-sweet all three.

By Jonas Egeskov · Updated 6 September 2026

Vin Santo, a sweet Marsala, or Pedro Ximénez sherry. The one absolute rule of dessert pairing applies here more than anywhere.

Tiramisu is savoiardi soaked in coffee, layered with mascarpone beaten with egg and sugar, and dusted with cocoa. It is sweet, bitter and creamy at once, and it breaks more wines than any other common dessert — because the rule that governs all dessert pairing is unusually unforgiving here.

The wine must be sweeter than the pudding

Sugar in food suppresses your perception of sweetness and fruit in whatever follows, while leaving acidity and bitterness completely intact. Drink a dry wine after a sweet dessert and what reaches you is sourness and tannin with the fruit stripped out. The wine does not merely underperform — it tastes actively unpleasant.

So: the wine has to be at least as sweet as the plate. That is the whole rule, and it is the reason so many meals end badly with a fine dry red that was perfect an hour earlier.

Tiramisu adds a second demand. Coffee and cocoa are both bitter, and bitterness in food stacks with bitterness in wine. A tannic sweet red — a young Port, say — can taste harsh here where it would be fine with chocolate alone.

What to actually open

Vin Santo. The Tuscan answer: grapes dried on mats for months, then aged for years in small barrels. Nutty, caramelised, apricot-scented and properly sweet, with the acidity to stop it cloying. The regional match and the best one.

Sweet Marsala. Often the wine already in the mascarpone, which makes it a natural echo — and it costs very little. Look for dolce; the dry version is a cooking wine and will fail the sweetness rule badly.

Pedro Ximénez sherry. Extraordinarily sweet, almost black, tasting of raisin, fig and coffee. It meets the espresso in the dessert directly. A very small glass is plenty.

What to avoid

Dry wine of any colour. This is the failure mode, and it is not subtle — a dry sparkling with tiramisu tastes sour and metallic.

Young Port. It is sweet enough, but its tannin plus the cocoa and coffee produces something hard and drying. An aged Tawny works far better, and its nutty, caramel character suits the dessert.

And if your tiramisu is made with a lot of alcohol already — many are generous with the Marsala or rum — the dessert is stronger than it looks, and a lower-strength sweet wine like Moscato d’Asti is easier to finish a meal on.

Common questions

What wine goes with tiramisu?

Vin Santo, a sweet Marsala, or Pedro Ximénez sherry — all sweeter than the dessert, which is the requirement.

Why does dry wine fail with dessert?

Sugar in food suppresses sweetness and fruit in the wine that follows while leaving acidity and bitterness intact. A dry wine after a sweet dish tastes sour and stripped rather than merely underwhelming.

Does Port work with tiramisu?

An aged Tawny does — nutty and caramelised, which suits the coffee. A young ruby Port does not: its tannin plus the cocoa and espresso turns hard and drying.

What if the tiramisu is very boozy?

Then the dessert is stronger than it looks, and a lower-alcohol sweet wine like Moscato d’Asti is an easier way to finish the meal.
