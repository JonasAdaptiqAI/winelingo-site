What Wine Goes With Indian Food?

One rule covers most of it: aromatic, low in alcohol, and a little off-dry.

Off-dry Riesling or Gewürztraminer. If you remember one thing about wine with Indian food, make it those two.

Indian food is not one cuisine and the heat ranges from a korma that a child could eat to a vindaloo that is genuinely painful. But nearly every dish shares two features — aromatic spice and some chilli — and those two point at the same narrow band of wine, which is why the answer is more consistent than the food is.

Chilli sets the ceiling

Capsaicin heat is amplified by alcohol and sharpened by tannin. That single mechanism explains most bad experiences at an Indian table: a 14.5% oaked red makes the food taste hotter and itself taste bitter and hard. The rule that follows is simple — the hotter the dish, the lower the alcohol and the colder the glass.

Residual sugar helps as well. A little sweetness genuinely damps the burn, which is why off-dry wines work where bone-dry ones fail. This is not about liking sweet wine; it is about a measurable effect on how hot the food tastes.

Warm spice wants aromatics

Cardamom, cumin, coriander, cinnamon and clove are aromatic rather than painful. They do not punish alcohol — they simply need a wine with enough perfume of its own not to be erased by them. A neutral, inexpensive white disappears entirely.

Gewürztraminer is the most aromatic common white there is: lychee and rose, from a compound called cis-rose oxide, usually with a touch of sugar. It is the classic recommendation and it earns it.

Riesling has less perfume but far more acidity and lower alcohol, which makes it the better choice when there is tomato, yoghurt or lemon in the dish — and most of the time there is.

By dish

Creamy and mild — korma, butter chicken — takes a Viognier or a fuller Gewürztraminer. Tomato-based curries want Riesling for the acidity. Biryani is warm-spiced rather than hot and takes the widest range, including a soft Grenache.

Genuinely hot dishes — vindaloo, phaal — are close to unpairable with wine. Drop to the lowest-alcohol off-dry Riesling you can find, serve it very cold, and accept that beer or a lassi does the job better.

For red, keep it fruity and low in tannin: Grenache or a chilled Gamay. Avoid Cabernet Sauvignon and anything oaked — the two most common mistakes.

Common questions

What wine goes with Indian food?

An off-dry Riesling or a Gewürztraminer. Both are aromatic enough for the spice, low enough in alcohol for the chilli, and carry a little sugar that damps the heat.

Why does wine taste worse with spicy food?

Capsaicin heat is amplified by alcohol and sharpened by tannin. A strong, tannic red makes the dish taste hotter and itself taste harder. Lower alcohol, colder serving and a touch of sweetness all reduce the burn.

Can I drink red wine with a curry?

Yes, if it is fruity and low in tannin — a soft Grenache or a chilled Gamay. Cabernet Sauvignon and oaked reds are the usual mistakes.

What wine goes with a very hot curry?

Honestly, the lowest-alcohol off-dry Riesling you can find, served very cold. Beyond a certain heat, beer or a lassi genuinely works better than wine.
