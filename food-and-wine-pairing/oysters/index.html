Wine With Oysters: The Four Classics

Champagne, Chablis, Muscadet, Sancerre — how they differ, and which one suits your oyster.

Bone-dry, high in acidity, no oak. That is the entire brief. Four wines meet it so completely that they have been the answer for a century.

A raw oyster is salt water, iodine and a slippery sweetness, and it is gone in about two seconds. Sugar, oak or body will flatten it. What you want is a wine lean enough to act as seasoning rather than as a drink in its own right.

That sur lie label is worth looking for. It is a legal mention, not marketing: the wine must sit on its fine fermentation lees through the winter without racking, until at least 1 March following the harvest, and then be bottled straight off those lees, in the cellar where it was made, inside a set window later that same year. That window produces one of the odder outcomes in French wine law: Muscadet’s crus communaux — Clisson, Gorges, Le Pallet and the rest, which must spend somewhere between 17 and 24 months on their lees before release — cannot legally print sur lie on the label, having missed the bottling deadline by doing far more of it than the wines that can.

Chablis is quite literally made of oysters

The vineyards of Chablis sit on Kimmeridgian marl: alternating beds of limestone and grey-blue clay laid down under a warm shallow sea between roughly 157 and 152 million years ago. What makes it distinctive is what is in it: fossilised shells of a small comma-shaped oyster called Exogyra virgula, packed densely enough that in the grand cru vineyards you can pick them out of the soil with your fingers.

Whether a vine can transmit any of that to the glass is one of wine’s longest arguments, and nobody has settled it. It does not much matter. Chablis is unoaked Chardonnay grown on cold, chalky ground at the northern limit of Burgundy, and it would suit oysters if it were grown on a car park.

What you put on top changes the answer

Eaten naked, an oyster will take any of the four. A squeeze of lemon adds citrus that Muscadet and Chablis simply absorb. A classic mignonette — shallots steeped in red wine vinegar — is much sharper than people expect, and it is the moment to reach for Sancerre — not because it is the most acidic of the four, which it is not, but because Sauvignon Blanc is the only one of them with a green, oniony-herbal streak that reads as though it belongs with raw shallot.

Tabasco is where the wine stops mattering. Capsaicin scorches a bone-dry, high-acid white, and nothing on this list has the residual sugar to cool it down. Have a beer.

Whatever goes on top, serve everything cold: the wine at 6–10°C (43–50°F), the oysters colder still and straight off ice.

Two mistakes, and one rule you can ignore

The mistakes are oak and sweetness. An oaked Chardonnay buries the oyster under vanilla; anything off-dry makes the brine taste like seawater in the worst sense. Red wine is a third, milder mistake, though rarely for the reason given: it is a wine’s iron content, not its tannin, that provokes the metallic aftertaste with shellfish. The commoner problem is simply scale. An oyster is two seconds of cold salt water, and almost every red has more body than the thing it is meant to be seasoning.

The rule to drop is the one about only eating oysters in months containing an R. It had two real causes. Wild oysters spawn in warm water and turn soft, milky and bland, and summer was genuinely dangerous before refrigeration. Cold chains and testing dealt with the second. The first is being dealt with by triploid oysters, bred with a third set of chromosomes that leaves them largely — though not completely — sterile, so they never pour their substance into spawning. French growers still collect most of their young oysters from the wild, but roughly four-fifths of the spat leaving the hatcheries is triploid, and in Virginia triploids have run at 80–97% of the hatchery oysters planted on farms since surveys began in 2009. A July oyster from a good producer can be every bit as firm as a January one.

FAQ

Questions & answers

What is the best wine to drink with oysters?

Champagne if you want one bottle that never fails, Chablis if you want the classic still white. Both are bone-dry, unoaked and high in acidity, which is the whole requirement.

Is Chablis or Muscadet better with oysters?

Chablis is the finer wine; Muscadet is the better value and often the better match with small, intensely briny oysters. Look for one labelled sur lie for a little extra texture.

Can you drink red wine with oysters?

It rarely works. Most reds simply have too much body for something as fleeting as a raw oyster, and the metallic aftertaste people notice tracks a wine's iron content rather than its colour. A very light, chilled red is the least bad option.

Can you eat oysters in months without an R?

Yes, if they are farmed. The rule reflected summer spawning and the lack of refrigeration. Refrigeration settled the second; triploid oysters, bred to be largely sterile, mostly settle the first, and they now account for about four-fifths of the spat leaving French hatcheries.
