What Wine Goes With Spaghetti Carbonara?

Egg, guanciale, Pecorino, pepper. Four ingredients, no cream, and no room at all for a red.

A crisp, dry, unoaked white. Verdicchio if you want the best version of this pairing, Frascati if you want the Roman one, Soave or a decent Pinot Grigio if that is what the shop has.

Carbonara looks like a cream sauce and contains no cream. It is egg yolk, hard sheep's cheese and rendered pork fat emulsified with starchy pasta water, which means the wine is up against fat and salt at the same time, and a great deal of both.

The dish is younger than the mythology

Carbonara gets talked about as ancient Roman canon. The oldest printed recipe anyone has produced is American: a 1952 guide to eating in Chicago, describing a dish served at a restaurant called Armando's. The first Italian one ran in La Cucina Italiana in August 1954 and called for pancetta, a clove of garlic and Swiss Gruyère. Guanciale — cured pork jowl, the thing every Roman now treats as non-negotiable — only appears in Luigi Carnacina's cookbook in 1960.

That history matters because it explains what the wine is dealing with today. The settled modern version leans on Pecorino Romano, a sheep's-milk DOP cheese that may legally be made only in Lazio, Sardinia and the province of Grosseto. Five months' ageing is the minimum to sell it as a table cheese; the grating version you actually put on carbonara must have had eight. That extra time is why it is so intensely savoury as well as salty — and it is the savour, not the salt, that decides what you are allowed to pour.

Three jobs for the glass

Cut the fat. Guanciale renders down into liquid fat that coats everything, and only acidity clears it. A soft white cannot; it just adds weight to weight.

Match the volume, not the salt. Salt is the component people blame and it is the wrong culprit — salt in food makes wine taste rounder, fruitier and less astringent, which is why cured pork flatters almost anything you open. The real difficulty is intensity: Pecorino Romano is loud, and a thin, neutral white simply gets shouted down.

Stay out of the pepper's way. Coarse black pepper is a genuine ingredient here, not a garnish, and oak's bitterness compounds with it. Every wine on the list is unoaked for that reason.

Verdicchio first, then the other four

Verdicchio dei Castelli di Jesi, without much hesitation. It has the highest acidity of the group, a bit more weight than Pinot Grigio, and a faintly bitter almond note on the finish that does something interesting next to the Pecorino. The Riserva was promoted to DOCG in 2010, but the everyday bottling is the one for this.

Frascati is the local pick, from the Castelli Romani hills south of Rome. The blend must be at least 70% Malvasia, and the difference between a good Frascati and the thin, watery stuff the name became a byword for in the 1970s is usually which Malvasia: the aromatic Malvasia del Lazio, or the high-yielding Candia. Frascati Superiore earned its own DOCG in 2011.

Soave — at least 70% Garganega, from Veneto — is the softest option and the easiest to like. Unoaked Chardonnay matches on weight. Pinot Grigio is fine, but buy it from Alto Adige or Friuli, where it has some flavour to lose.

Why a red cannot win this one

It is not the salt, whatever you have read. Egg yolk coats the tongue and blunts fruit, and eight-month Pecorino Romano is one of the most umami-rich things in any kitchen — aged hard cheese is dense in free glutamate, and umami sharpens astringency and bitterness while flattening fruit. Add coarse black pepper and a structured red turns up tasting hard and gritty.

The nearest thing to an exception is a light, unoaked red served cold — a young Gamay at 13°C (55°F) will not offend anyone. It still does less for the dish than a £10 Verdicchio.

FAQ

Questions & answers

What wine goes with carbonara?

A crisp, dry, unoaked white with high acidity. Verdicchio is the best of them, Frascati is the Roman choice, and Soave or a northern Italian Pinot Grigio both work. The wine's job is to cut the pork fat and match the intensity of Pecorino Romano.

Does any red wine work with carbonara?

It is the weakest pairing on the table, though not for the reason usually given. Salt actually softens a wine. The trouble is that egg yolk coats the palate and blunts fruit while the umami of aged Pecorino sharpens tannin, so a structured red tastes hard and gritty. If you want red, choose something light, unoaked and served cold, such as a young Gamay.

What is the best Italian white wine for carbonara?

Verdicchio dei Castelli di Jesi. It has the acidity to clear the fat, enough body to survive the cheese, and a bitter almond finish that plays well with Pecorino Romano. Frascati is the traditional Roman answer and a close second.

Does the same wine work for cacio e pepe and amatriciana?

For cacio e pepe, yes — it is the same fat-and-intensity problem without the egg, so a crisp white is ideal. Amatriciana adds tomato, which flips the pairing to a high-acid red such as Chianti or Montepulciano d’Abruzzo.
