What Wine Goes With Dark Chocolate Cake?

Chocolate is wine’s hardest partner at the table, and there is exactly one rule that fixes it.

A sweet fortified red: Ruby or Late Bottled Vintage Port, Banyuls, Maury, or a small glass of Pedro Ximénez sherry. Every one of them carries more sugar than the cake does, and that is the whole trick.

Chocolate has a reputation as wine’s difficult partner and it earns it. A flourless 70% cake delivers bitterness, fat and sugar in one mouthful, and dry red wine — the thing people reach for because chocolate smells like red wine — is exactly what it destroys.

One rule, and it is not negotiable

Sugar in food does the opposite of salt. Next to something sweet, a wine loses its own perceived sweetness and fruit while its acidity, bitterness and tannin all step forward. Pour a dry red with chocolate cake and it arrives tasting sour, thin and stalky: fruit gone, structure exposed.

The fix is arithmetic rather than art. Ruby and Late Bottled Vintage Port sit in the IVDP’s “sweet” band — 90 to 130 grams of sugar per litre — comfortably ahead of a flourless 70% cake. Pedro Ximénez must legally hold at least 212 g/l to use the name, and most bottles land nearer 300–400. The cake never gets in front.

Which makes a 70% cake, counter-intuitively, the friendliest chocolate for wine: bitter rather than sugary, so the bar is lower. Milk chocolate is the harder problem.

Port, Banyuls, Maury or PX

  • Ruby Port — the youngest and most straightforwardly fruity, all blackberry and plum. The cheapest way in, and the one to buy if you are pouring for a table.
  • Late Bottled Vintage Port — a single harvest aged four to six years in wood before bottling. Most of what reaches a supermarket shelf is fined and filtered and needs no decanting; unfiltered bottlings say so on the label and will throw a sediment. More depth than Ruby for very little more money, and my default.
  • Banyuls — the French answer, from terraced schist slopes where the Pyrenees drop into the sea in Roussillon. At least half Grenache Noir, rising to 75% for Banyuls Grand Cru, which must also age at least 30 months before release. Fortified while the juice is still on the skins, it keeps a savoury, tarry edge Port does not have.
  • Maury — Banyuls’s inland neighbour, at least 75% Grenache Noir, darker and more figgy.
  • Pedro Ximénez — barely a wine. Black, viscous and raisined, from grapes laid out on mats in the Andalusian sun until they shrivel to raisins, and only then pressed. Spoon it over the cake rather than pouring a glass beside it.

Why the obvious big red fails

The suggestion you will read most often is a ripe, powerful dry red — Amarone, Zinfandel, a warm-climate Cabernet — on the grounds that chocolate and red wine share a cocoa, coffee and dark-fruit vocabulary. The aromas genuinely do rhyme. It still fails, because aroma was never the problem and no amount of shared flavour language survives the sugar.

The version that does work is a single square of very dark chocolate with a glass of red at the end of an evening: no cake, no cream, no pudding sugar in the way.

The same rule rules out most sweet whites. Sauternes has the sugar but not the weight, and its honeyed apricot register vanishes against cocoa. Keep it for the blue cheese.

Serving, and the bottle worth hunting for

Serve Ruby or LBV at 15–16°C (59–61°F), a shade cooler than a table red — warm fortified wine against a warm pudding is cloying. Pedro Ximénez wants 12–14°C (54–57°F). Pour 50–75 ml; Port runs to 19–22% alcohol and the cake is doing most of the work.

If you want the best version of this pairing rather than the easiest, look for rancio on a Banyuls or Maury label. Those wines spend time outdoors in large glass demijohns — bonbonnes — on sun-exposed terraces, deliberately cooked and oxidised by the Roussillon heat before they ever settle into cask. They taste of walnut, coffee grounds and dried fig, and against a bitter chocolate cake that is the most interesting glass on this page.

FAQ

Questions & answers

What wine goes with dark chocolate cake?

A sweet fortified red — Ruby or LBV Port, Banyuls, Maury, or Pedro Ximénez sherry. Each carries more sugar than the cake, which stops the pudding stripping the fruit out of the wine.

Can you drink dry red wine with chocolate?

Not with a sweet chocolate pudding — the sugar on the plate strips a dry red of its fruit and leaves the tannin and acid standing there unaccompanied. A single square of very dark chocolate alongside a glass of red is a different proposition entirely, and works well.

What is a cheaper alternative to Port with chocolate?

Banyuls and Maury from Roussillon are fortified Grenache wines built on the same principle and often cost less than an equivalent Port. A basic Ruby Port is also inexpensive and does the job perfectly well.

What wine goes with milk chocolate?

Milk chocolate is the tougher assignment, because it is sweeter than dark — go up a rung to Tawny Port or Pedro Ximénez rather than a dry-leaning fortified red. Pitch the wine below the chocolate and it turns sharp.
