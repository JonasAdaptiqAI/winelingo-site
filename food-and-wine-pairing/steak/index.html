What Wine Goes With Steak?

Grilled ribeye, firm tannin, and the part of the pairing that happens in your mouth rather than on the plate.

Cabernet Sauvignon, and you can stop reading there if you like. A Napa Cabernet, a Left Bank Bordeaux, an Argentine Malbec or an Australian Shiraz will all do the job on a ribeye.

What is worth knowing is why it works, because the usual explanation — that the tannin cuts through the fat — has it backwards. The steak is doing more to the wine than the wine is doing to the steak.

What the steak does to the wine

The drying, chalky grip you feel from a young red is astringency, and it happens because tannins bind the proteins in your saliva and pull them out of solution, leaving your mouth briefly short of its own lubrication.

Now put a mouthful of ribeye in first. The meat arrives loaded with protein and fat, and the tannin binds to that instead. Less of your saliva gets stripped, so the same wine tastes rounder, softer and noticeably fruitier than it did on its own. That is the whole trick — and it explains why a bottle that felt punishing at a tasting suddenly makes sense at dinner.

It also explains why ribeye specifically is the steak that flatters the biggest wines. USDA figures put a raw boneless rib eye at about 14–15 g of fat per 100 g, and a heavily marbled Choice-grade cut well above that — several times what a trimmed fillet carries, so there is simply more there for the tannin to work on. Fillet gives the wine much less to bind to, and a big Cabernet can taste harder alongside it than you expected.

The shortlist, and what each one brings

  • Cabernet Sauvignon — firm tannin, blackcurrant, cedar. The default, and deservedly so.
  • Napa Cabernet — riper, plusher, higher in alcohol. Best on a plainly grilled and salted steak; that sweet fruit can turn cloying against a rich sauce.
  • Left Bank Bordeaux — leaner and more savoury, with cedar and graphite instead of jam. Given the choice I would take a ten-year-old claret over a young trophy Cabernet every time: mature, savoury flavours flatter beef in a way primary blackcurrant does not.
  • Malbec — dark, plush, easy. The crowd-pleaser, and the one to pour if you do not know the table.
  • Shiraz — peppery and generous. The pick if there is a peppercorn crust or a well-charred edge.

Three ways people get it wrong

Pouring something delicate. A village red Burgundy or a light Beaujolais will simply be flattened by a ribeye. There is nothing wrong with the wine; it is the wrong job.

Serving it too warm. Aim for 15–18°C (59–64°F). A centrally heated room sits nearer 21°C, and at that temperature a 14.5% Cabernet reads as hot and jammy rather than fragrant. Our serving temperature guide works through the other styles.

Letting the meat pick the wine. The sauce usually decides. Béarnaise wants acidity and cuts badly against heavy oak; peppercorn sauce loves Syrah; a blue cheese sauce adds salt, which softens tannin further and lets you go bigger still.

Malbec's steak reputation is newer than it looks

Malbec reads as Argentine now, but it is a Bordeaux grape and is still permitted in the Bordeaux appellations, where it goes by the local name Pressac around Libourne. The frost of February 1956 — the region's hardest freeze since 1709, with the Médoc dropping towards −20°C — wiped out something like three-quarters of the crop and killed a great deal of the region's Malbec outright. Growers replanted with Merlot and Cabernet Franc, and it is worth being clear about why: not because those vines shrug off a freeze, since Merlot buds early and is one of the more frost-prone grapes in Bordeaux, but because they were the more highly regarded grapes, and Malbec's habit of shedding its flowers in a cold, wet spring had long made it a nuisance to farm.

Cahors in south-west France stuck with it — the appellation still requires a minimum of 70% Malbec — and made the dark, tannic wine known for centuries as the black wine. Argentina, meanwhile, took the same grape, planted it high in Mendoza and turned it into the world's default steak wine. The famous pairing is barely two generations old.

FAQ

Questions & answers

What is the best wine to drink with a ribeye steak?

Cabernet Sauvignon. A Left Bank Bordeaux is the more savoury choice and a Napa Cabernet the riper one. Ribeye carries far more fat than a fillet, so it softens firm tannin better.

Why does red wine taste better with steak?

Tannins bind the proteins in your saliva, which is what makes a young red feel drying. The protein and fat in the steak give the tannin something else to bind to, so the wine tastes softer and fruitier.

What wine goes with steak in a peppercorn sauce?

Syrah or Shiraz. Its own black-pepper character comes from the same aroma compound found in peppercorns, so the wine and the sauce reinforce each other rather than competing.

What temperature should red wine be with steak?

15–18°C (59–64°F), which is cooler than a centrally heated dining room. If the bottle has been standing at 21°C, a few minutes in an ice-water bucket gets it there faster than the fridge will. Above that band a high-alcohol red reads as hot and its fruit drops away.
