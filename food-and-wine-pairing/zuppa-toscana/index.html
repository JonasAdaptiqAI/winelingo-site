What Wine Goes With Zuppa Toscana?

Sausage, kale, potato and cream. The fennel in the sausage is what you pair to.

Chianti Classico for a red, Vermentino for a white. Acidity to cut the cream, savour to meet the sausage.

The soup most people mean by zuppa toscana is the American restaurant version: Italian sausage, kale, potato and cream in a light broth. It is richer and simpler than anything you would find in Tuscany, but the pairing logic is the same — fennel-scented pork, bitter greens and dairy, which is three different demands in one bowl.

Three things in the bowl

The sausage is the loudest flavour and usually carries fennel, which is anise-scented and points towards wines with their own herbal or savoury character rather than pure fruit.

The kale brings a mild bitterness, and bitterness in food stacks with bitterness in wine — so heavy oak and firm tannin both make the bowl taste harsher.

The cream and potato coat the palate and dull it. That is what makes acidity non-negotiable: without it the soup flattens after two spoonfuls and the wine disappears entirely.

What to actually open

Chianti Classico. Sangiovese is high in acidity with a dried-herb savouriness that meets fennel sausage exactly, and its tannin is moderate enough not to be stripped bare by the cream. The regional answer, name notwithstanding.

Vermentino. The white route, and arguably better with a cream-heavy version: textured, faintly bitter on the finish in a way that echoes the kale, and sharp enough to cut dairy.

Barbera if you would rather have red with even less grip — the highest acidity of the everyday Italian reds and very little tannin.

What to avoid

Oak, in either colour. Between the kale’s bitterness and any char on the sausage, there is already a bitter edge on the plate, and oak adds more.

Big tannic reds. Cream coats rather than binds, so tannin has nothing to grip except your saliva, and the kale sharpens it further. A Cabernet Sauvignon is the wrong shape here even though the dish is hearty.

If your version skips the cream — the more Tuscan approach, closer to a ribollita — the bowl becomes leaner and more savoury, and a fuller Sangiovese or even a Montepulciano fits comfortably.

Common questions

What wine goes with zuppa toscana?

Chianti Classico for red or Vermentino for white. Both have the acidity to cut the cream and the savour to meet fennel sausage.

Why does the cream matter?

It coats the palate and dulls it, so without real acidity in the glass the soup flattens after a couple of spoonfuls and the wine disappears. Cream also strips tannin bare, which rules out big reds.

What does the kale change?

It brings a mild bitterness, and bitterness in food stacks with bitterness in wine rather than cancelling it. That is the argument against heavy oak and firm tannin.

What if my version has no cream?

It becomes leaner and more savoury, closer to a Tuscan ribollita, and takes a fuller red comfortably — a richer Sangiovese or a Montepulciano.
