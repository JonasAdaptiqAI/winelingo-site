What Wine Goes With Mussels?

Cooked in wine, eaten with bread. Drink a relative of what went in the pot.

Muscadet from Melon de Bourgogne, Chablis, or Albariño. Bone dry, high in acid, no oak. The classic answer is the right one.

Moules marinière is mussels steamed open with white wine, shallots, garlic and parsley, sometimes finished with cream. The broth left in the bottom of the bowl is the point of the dish, and it is essentially concentrated wine, shellfish liquor and butter — which tells you most of what the glass needs to do.

Salt, iodine and the broth

Mussels are briny. Their own liquor is salty and faintly iodine-like, and that has two consequences. Salt makes wine taste rounder and fruitier, so a very dry, lean white shows better here than it does alone. And iodine clashes badly with tannin — the metallic taste people get from red wine with shellfish is real and worth avoiding.

The broth adds butter and reduced wine on top. Acidity cuts both, which is why every traditional pairing for this dish is a high-acid unoaked white, from Brittany to Galicia.

What to actually open

Muscadet. Melon de Bourgogne from the Atlantic end of the Loire, and the classic partner for moules on its home coast. Look for sur lie on the label — the wine rested on its spent yeast, which adds a faintly bready weight to an otherwise very lean wine. It is also cheap, which suits a dish eaten with your fingers.

Chablis. Unoaked Chardonnay with more texture than Muscadet and the same cutting acidity. The better choice if there is cream in the broth.

Albariño. Saline, stone-fruited and high in acid — the Galician answer, where mussels are farmed on the same rías the wine grows above.

Cook with what you drink, for once literally

The dish takes a full glass of wine, and it reduces. That concentrates whatever the wine had: acidity and any oak intensify while the alcohol boils off. Use something dry, cheap and unoaked — and then drink one like it, because the broth will taste of the family of wine you cooked with.

The variations change little. Mussels with cream want Chablis. With chorizo and tomato, the dish gains fat and spice and a dry rosado works well. Thai-style with coconut and chilli moves to an off-dry Riesling, for the reasons that apply to all spicy food.

What never works: oak, and red wine. Oak plus shellfish is heavy and slightly bitter, and tannin plus iodine is the metallic pairing everyone warns about — accurately, for once.

Common questions

What wine goes with mussels?

Muscadet made from Melon de Bourgogne, Chablis, or Albariño — all bone dry, high in acid and unoaked.

What wine should I cook mussels in?

Something dry, cheap and unoaked, and then drink a wine like it. The broth reduces, which concentrates acidity and oak while the alcohol boils off, so anything harsh in the bottle is harsher in the pot.

Can you drink red wine with mussels?

Best not. Shellfish iodine and tannin together produce a genuinely metallic taste — this is one of the few pairing warnings that is chemically real rather than a matter of taste.

What if the mussels have cream or chorizo?

Cream pushes towards Chablis, which has more texture than Muscadet. Chorizo and tomato add fat and spice, and a dry Spanish rosado handles both.
