What Wine Goes With Wagyu?

The most marbled beef there is — which makes it a lighter pairing than you would guess.

By Jonas Egeskov · Updated 7 September 2026

An aged Nebbiolo, an older Bordeaux, or Champagne. Acidity does more here than tannin, which is the opposite of what people expect.

Wagyu is marbled to a degree no other beef approaches — in the highest grades the fat is distributed so finely that it begins melting near room temperature. That richness is the whole pairing question, and it leads somewhere counter-intuitive: the biggest, most tannic red you own is usually the wrong choice.

Why the huge red misfires

The instinct is straightforward — rich meat, big wine. And tannin does bind to fat, which is why ribeye earns a firm Cabernet.

But wagyu is served in small portions for a reason: it is so rich that a large piece becomes cloying. What you actually need between mouthfuls is something that cuts and refreshes, and that job belongs to acidity rather than tannin. A big, ripe, low-acid red adds richness to richness and the whole thing turns heavy.

The other factor is delicacy. For all its fat, wagyu is not a strongly flavoured beef — it is buttery and subtly sweet rather than iron-rich. A powerful oaked wine simply covers it.

What to actually open

Aged Nebbiolo. Barolo or Barbaresco with a decade on it: very high acidity to cut the fat, tannin that has resolved into something fine rather than grippy, and a savoury dried-rose character that meets the beef without burying it. The best answer.

An older Bordeaux. Cedar and tobacco with softened tannin — the same logic, more familiar.

Champagne. Not a joke: with a few slices of seared A5, carbonation and acidity cut the fat more effectively than any red, and it is what a good deal of Japan actually drinks with it. A Blanc de Noirs has the weight for it.

How it is served

Seared in small slices, as it usually is, the pairing above holds. Wagyu in a steakhouse cut of 200g or more behaves more like a very rich ribeye, and a fuller red becomes reasonable.

Sukiyaki or shabu-shabu put it in a sweet-savoury broth, which changes the dish entirely — that is Japanese food territory, and sake or a light chilled red suits it.

Salt and wasabi rather than sauce is the common serving, and neither adds much for the wine to work against — one more reason acidity and finesse beat power here.

Common questions

What wine goes with wagyu?

An aged Nebbiolo, an older Bordeaux, or Champagne. Acidity matters more than tannin with beef this rich.

Why not a big Cabernet with wagyu?

Because wagyu is served in small, extremely rich portions, and what refreshes between mouthfuls is acidity rather than tannin. A ripe low-acid red adds richness to richness, and its oak covers a subtly flavoured beef.

Does Champagne really work with wagyu?

Yes — carbonation and acidity cut extreme fat more effectively than any red, and it is a common pairing in Japan. A Blanc de Noirs has enough weight.

What if it is a large steakhouse cut?

Then it behaves more like a very rich ribeye and a fuller red becomes reasonable. The small-slice serving is what drives the lighter advice.
