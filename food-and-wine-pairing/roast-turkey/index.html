The Best Wine for Roast Turkey

One bottle, a dozen people, and a plate with nine other things on it.

A light, low-tannin red with bright acidity. Pinot Noir, Beaujolais, Grenache or Cinsault — or a high-acid Riesling if the table leans sweet.

The turkey is the easiest thing on a Christmas or Thanksgiving plate to pair with: lean, delicate, faintly savoury and no trouble at all. Everything sitting around it is the trouble.

You are not pairing with the turkey

Look at what is actually on the plate. Cranberry sauce. Glazed parsnips or candied sweet potato. Chestnut stuffing. Bread sauce. Pigs in blankets. Gravy. The bird is perhaps a third of what your mouth is dealing with, and at least two of those things are sweet.

Sugar in food does something specific and unhelpful to a dry wine: it makes the wine taste more acidic, more bitter and noticeably less fruity. A dry red that seemed generous on its own can turn thin and sharp the moment cranberry sauce joins it. There are two ways out — choose a red with fruit to spare, or choose a wine that carries a little sugar of its own. An off-dry Riesling does the second job better than anything else, and the wine sweetness chart shows where the labels sit.

Why the red has to be light

Turkey breast is about as lean as meat gets. Tannin needs fat and protein to bind to; without them it lingers as a drying, faintly bitter grip and strips moisture from meat that had none to spare. The whole shortlist is chosen for the tannin it does not have.

Cinsault is the one almost nobody buys on purpose, and it is the sleeper here: pale, soft, full of strawberry and red cherry, and heat-tolerant enough that southern France plants it everywhere. It is also half of Pinotage. Abraham Perold crossed it with Pinot Noir at Stellenbosch in 1925, then left the university and abandoned the seedlings in a garden; a successor rescued them, and the vine went unnamed for roughly another decade. Because South Africans of the day called Cinsault “Hermitage”, the name it finally got was Pinot plus Hermitage.

Skip: big, high-alcohol reds. A 15% Zinfandel or a young Napa Cabernet buries lean white meat, and the cranberry sauce then strips out whatever fruit the wine had left.

Three rules for buying the turkey wine

  1. Buy one wine, in quantity. Six interesting bottles for twelve people means half the table never gets to try the one they liked. A single light red plus one aromatic white covers every plate.
  2. Chill the red. 12–14°C (54–57°F) — cellar cool, not cupboard warm. A dining room at 21°C (70°F) makes a light red taste flabby and alcoholic; the serving temperature changes the wine more than the price does.
  3. Settle the cranberry sauce first. Homemade and sharp, and a dry red is fine. The sweet jellied kind, and you want the Riesling on the table too.

The Beaujolais Nouveau coincidence

Beaujolais Nouveau is released at one minute past midnight on the third Thursday of November, a date the INAO fixed in 1985. American Thanksgiving falls on the fourth Thursday. The year’s most famous young red therefore reaches the shops exactly seven days before the biggest turkey dinner on earth. Thanksgiving had nothing to do with it: release day had been a fixed 15 November since 1951, and the INAO pinned it to a Thursday so that it would stop landing at weekends.

It is a perfectly decent match: light, juicy, near enough tannin-free. It is not the best Beaujolais you can buy. For a little more, a cru bottling from Fleurie or Morgon gives you the same low-tannin freshness with actual depth, and it will still be drinking well at New Year.

FAQ

Questions & answers

What wine goes with turkey at Christmas?

A light, low-tannin red: Pinot Noir, cru Beaujolais, Grenache or Cinsault. Serve it cool, around 12–14°C (54–57°F). If the table includes sweet sides such as cranberry sauce or glazed parsnips, add an off-dry Riesling.

Should you drink red or white wine with turkey?

Either, as long as it is high in acidity and low in tannin. Light reds have the edge with gravy and stuffing; a high-acid Riesling has the edge with cranberry sauce and anything glazed or candied.

Why does cranberry sauce ruin wine?

Sweetness in the food subtracts from the wine: it pushes acidity and bitterness forward and pulls the fruit back. A dry red served alongside sweet cranberry sauce can suddenly seem thin and sour. A wine with a touch of residual sugar of its own avoids the problem.

Is Beaujolais Nouveau good with Thanksgiving turkey?

Yes, and the timing is uncanny — it is released on the third Thursday of November, a week before Thanksgiving. A cru Beaujolais such as Fleurie or Morgon is a better wine for very little extra, though.
