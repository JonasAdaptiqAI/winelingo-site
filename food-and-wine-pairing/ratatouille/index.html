What Wine Goes With Ratatouille?

Aubergine, courgette, pepper, tomato and herbs. Five vegetables, one very obvious wine region.

Provence rosé, or a Grenache-based southern French red served cool. The dish grew up in the same place as the wine, and it shows.

Ratatouille is a Provençal stew of summer vegetables — aubergine, courgette, peppers, tomatoes, onion and garlic, cooked slowly in olive oil with thyme and bay. Almost everything in it grows within sight of vineyards that make exactly the right wine for it, and that is not a coincidence. Regional pairings tend to work because both halves evolved to suit the same climate and the same table.

What the dish is actually asking for

Three things. Acidity, because the tomatoes and peppers are sharp. Modest tannin, because there is no fat or protein here to soften a firm red — this is vegetables and olive oil, and a tannic wine will taste hard and metallic against it. And a herbal, sun-warmed character in the wine that echoes the thyme and bay rather than fighting them.

That combination points almost exactly at Provence and the southern Rhône. It also explains why a Bordeaux or a Napa Cabernet feels wrong here in a way that has nothing to do with quality.

What to actually open

Provence rosé is the default and it is genuinely right, not merely convenient. Pale, dry, faintly herbal, and made largely from Grenache and Cinsault — the same grapes as the local reds, without the tannin the dish does not want.

Red, if you want one. A light Grenache or Cinsault, served at cellar temperature rather than room temperature. Chilling a light red slightly firms up its acidity and mutes any alcoholic heat, which is exactly what you want beside olive oil.

Tavel if you can find it — a Rhône rosé deliberately made deeper and more structured than the Provençal style. It is the one rosé that can handle ratatouille served hot alongside something substantial.

The pepper problem

Green peppers, if your version leans on them, contain pyrazines — the same class of compound that gives Cabernet Franc and under-ripe Sauvignon Blanc their grassy, bell-pepper note. Pair a pyrazine-heavy dish with a pyrazine-heavy wine and the green character does not cancel out; it doubles, and the result can taste aggressively stalky.

Red peppers, roasted until sweet, do the opposite and make the dish rounder and friendlier. If your ratatouille is built on sweet roasted peppers, you can afford a riper, fruitier wine than the classic advice suggests.

Common questions

What wine goes with ratatouille?

A dry Provence rosé, or a light Grenache or Cinsault red served cool. The dish and those wines come from the same corner of France.

Can I drink red wine with ratatouille?

Yes, but keep it light and chill it slightly. There is no fat or protein in the dish to soften tannin, so a big structured red tastes hard against it. Cellar temperature suits it better than room temperature.

Why does my wine taste extra green with this dish?

Green peppers contain pyrazines, the compounds behind the bell-pepper note in Cabernet Franc and grassy Sauvignon Blanc. Combine both and the green character doubles rather than cancels.

Is rosé a cop-out here?

No — it is the regional answer. Provence rosé is made from the same Grenache and Cinsault as the local reds, minus the tannin the vegetables do not want. It is the right wine, not the easy one.
