What Wine Goes With Spaghetti and Meatballs?

Tomato sauce with meat in it. The tomato still sets the rules.

By Jonas Egeskov · Updated 6 September 2026

Sangiovese, Montepulciano or Barbera. High acid, medium body, moderate tannin — the standard Italian shape.

Spaghetti and meatballs is Italian-American rather than Italian, and it is more generous than either a plain tomato pasta or a bolognese: a proper tomato sauce, meatballs of beef and pork bound with bread and cheese, and usually a snowfall of Parmesan. The meat lets you go a little fuller than tomato alone would allow — but only a little, because the tomato is still in charge.

Tomato still sets the rules

Tomato is acidic and full of glutamate. The acid means a soft, low-acid wine tastes flabby beside it; the glutamate sharpens tannin and hollows out fruit, so a firm oaked red tastes harder and the sauce tastes flatter.

The meatballs push the other way, but less than people assume. They contain breadcrumbs and cheese as well as meat, and they sit in the sauce rather than beside it — so they soften the dish rather than turning it into a meat course. A little more body is justified; a Cabernet is not.

The Parmesan helps. It is salty, and salt makes wine taste rounder and fruitier, so a sharp inexpensive red performs above its price.

What to actually open

Sangiovese. Sour cherry, dried herbs, high acidity, moderate grippy tannin. A Chianti Classico is the obvious bottle and it is obvious because it works.

Montepulciano d’Abruzzo. Rounder, darker and softer, and the best value here — it suits the slightly sweeter, richer Italian-American style of sauce.

Barbera if your sauce is very tomato-forward: the highest acidity of the everyday Italian reds with the least tannin.

What changes it

A long-simmered sauce with a lot of tomato paste is sweeter and deeper, and takes a Primitivo or a Nero d’Avola comfortably.

Meatballs baked with mozzarella on top move the dish towards baked ziti: the melted cheese gives tannin something to bind to, so a firmer red is fine.

What to avoid: Cabernet Sauvignon and heavy oak. This is the most common mistake at an Italian-American table — the tannin is sharpened by the glutamate and the oak turns faintly bitter against the tomato.

Common questions

What wine goes with spaghetti and meatballs?

Sangiovese — a Chianti Classico — or a Montepulciano d’Abruzzo for something rounder and cheaper.

Do the meatballs mean I can drink a bigger wine?

A little, but less than you would think. They sit in the sauce rather than beside it and contain bread and cheese as well as meat, so the tomato still sets the rules.

Why not Cabernet Sauvignon?

It is the most common mistake here. Tomato glutamate sharpens its tannin and the acid turns its oak faintly bitter, so the wine tastes harder and the sauce tastes flatter.

What if the meatballs are baked with mozzarella?

Then the melted cheese gives tannin something to bind to, and a firmer red becomes comfortable — much the same as baked ziti.
