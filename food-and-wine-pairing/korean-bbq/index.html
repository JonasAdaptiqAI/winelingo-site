What Wine Goes With Korean BBQ?

Sweet marinade, charred meat, fermented chilli paste. Fruity and cold beats big and serious.

A chilled Beaujolais or other Gamay, or an off-dry Riesling. Fruit and freshness, not structure — the marinade and the gochujang both punish a serious red.

Korean barbecue looks like a red-wine occasion and mostly is not. Bulgogi and galbi marinades are built on soy, sugar, garlic, sesame and pear, so the meat arrives sweet as well as savoury and charred at the edges. Add gochujang — fermented chilli paste, which brings heat and umami together — and a plate of sharp fermented vegetables, and the obvious big Cabernet turns bitter and hot.

Three things working against a serious red

The sweetness of the marinade makes a dry red taste sour and hollow, the same mechanism that catches people out with squash soup. The chilli in gochujang is amplified by alcohol and sharpened by tannin. And the fermented sides bring enough acidity that a low-acid wine will taste flabby beside them.

A tannic, high-alcohol, oaked red loses on all three counts at once. That is not a criticism of the wine; it is a mismatch of design.

What to actually open

Beaujolais. Gamay gives you bright red fruit, high acidity, very little tannin and modest alcohol — precisely the profile this meal wants. Serve it cool, around cellar temperature. The ten named crus, Morgon and Fleurie among them, are a considerable step up from basic Beaujolais and still inexpensive.

Off-dry Riesling if the gochujang is doing serious work. The residual sugar damps the heat, the acidity meets the fermented sides, and the low alcohol keeps the burn down.

Zinfandel is the popular recommendation and works only with care. Its jammy fruit genuinely suits a sweet marinade and char, but it is often 15% alcohol or more, which fights the chilli. Fine for bulgogi, poor for anything heavily spiced.

The char, and serving temperature

Meat cooked directly over fire develops a bitter, smoky edge, and bitterness in food stacks with bitterness in wine rather than cancelling it. Another reason to avoid heavy oak and firm tannin — you would be adding bitterness to a plate that already has some.

Serving temperature does more work here than the choice of bottle. A light red served at room temperature tastes alcoholic and soft; the same bottle twenty minutes in the fridge tastes fresh, fruity and considerably better against hot food. If you take one thing from this page, chill the red.

Common questions

What wine goes with Korean BBQ?

A chilled Beaujolais or other Gamay, or an off-dry Riesling if there is a lot of gochujang. Fruit and freshness beat structure here.

Why does a big Cabernet not work?

Three reasons at once. The sweet marinade makes a dry red taste hollow, the chilli is amplified by alcohol and sharpened by tannin, and the fermented sides out-acid a soft wine. A tannic, oaked red loses on all three.

Should I chill the red wine?

Yes, and it matters more than which bottle you pick. Twenty minutes in the fridge turns a light red from soft and alcoholic into fresh and fruity, which is what hot, sweet, charred food needs.

Does Zinfandel work with Korean BBQ?

With care. Its jammy fruit suits the sweet marinade and the char, but Zinfandel is often 15% alcohol or more, which makes chilli heat worse. Good for mild bulgogi, poor for anything heavily spiced.
