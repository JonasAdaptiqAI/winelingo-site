What Wine Goes With Glazed Ham?

Cured salt underneath, sugar on top. The two pull in opposite directions.

By Jonas Egeskov · Updated 6 September 2026

An off-dry Riesling, a dry rosé, or a chilled Beaujolais. Fruit for the glaze, acidity for the cure, no need for tannin.

A glazed ham is salt-cured pork finished with something sweet — honey, brown sugar, maple, marmalade — and baked until the surface caramelises. Salt and sugar in the same mouthful is an unusual combination, and it is the whole pairing problem: the two do opposite things to a glass of wine.

Salt helps, sugar hurts

Salt is the friendliest thing food does to wine. It suppresses bitterness and makes a wine taste rounder, fruitier and less tannic — which is why a sharp, inexpensive bottle shows better with cured meat than it does alone.

Sugar does the opposite. It suppresses fruit and sweetness in the wine while leaving acidity untouched, so a dry wine beside a sweet glaze tastes sour, thin and stripped.

The resolution is a wine with real fruit and preferably a little residual sugar, plus enough acidity to cut the fat of the pork. Tannin is neither needed nor helpful — the meat is not that rich and there is nothing here that wants grip.

What to actually open

Off-dry Riesling. The textbook answer and the correct one: sugar to meet the glaze, high acidity for the cure and the fat, low alcohol. An Alsace or a German Kabinett.

A dry rosé. Enough red fruit to sit with the glaze without being sweet itself, and no tannin. Good for a cold ham at a buffet, where it also looks right.

Chilled Beaujolais. The red answer — Gamay has bright fruit, high acidity and almost no tannin. Dry Lambrusco does the same job with bubbles, and is an underrated match for cold ham.

What is in the glaze

Mustard and cloves are the usual additions, and both matter a little. Clove is a warm baking spice, and warm spice turns falsely sweet against new oak — one more reason to stay unoaked. Mustard brings acidity and pungency, which the Riesling handles best of the three.

A pineapple or marmalade glaze is sweeter and sharper, pushing further towards off-dry. A maple or bourbon glaze is deeper and less acidic, and takes the Beaujolais comfortably.

What to avoid: big tannic reds, and oaked whites. Salt-cured pork with a sweet crust gives tannin nothing useful to do, and the sweetness will make an austere wine taste sour.

Common questions

What wine goes with glazed ham?

An off-dry Riesling, a dry rosé, or a chilled Beaujolais. Fruit for the glaze, acidity for the salt cure, and no need for tannin.

Why is ham tricky to pair?

Salt and sugar do opposite things to wine. Salt makes it taste rounder and fruitier; sugar suppresses its fruit and makes a dry wine taste sour. You need fruit and acidity, not structure.

Can I drink red wine with ham?

Yes, if it is light and fruity — a chilled Beaujolais or a dry Lambrusco. Avoid tannic reds; cured pork with a sweet crust gives tannin nothing useful to do.

Does the type of glaze change things?

A little. Pineapple or marmalade is sweeter and sharper, pushing towards off-dry Riesling. Maple or bourbon is deeper and less acidic, which suits the Beaujolais.
