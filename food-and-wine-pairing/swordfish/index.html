What Wine Goes With Swordfish?

Firm, meaty and usually grilled. Another fish that behaves like meat.

By Jonas Egeskov · Updated 6 September 2026

Vermentino, a dry rosé, or a light Mencía if it is grilled. Meaty enough to take more wine than most fish.

Swordfish is dense, firm and steak-like, usually cut thick and grilled. It has more texture and more flavour than white fish and less oil than salmon, which puts it in the same category as tuna: a fish that can stand up to a wine with some presence, including, carefully, a red.

Dense enough for a real wine

The two reasons red wine usually fails with fish are that tannin flattens delicate flesh, and that fish oils react with tannin and iron to produce a metallic aftertaste. Swordfish is dense enough to avoid the first and lean enough to largely avoid the second.

That does not make it a red-wine dish by default. It makes it one where a light, low-tannin red is a legitimate option alongside the whites — particularly when the fish is grilled, because char and a lightly smoky red sit in the same register.

It is also usually Mediterranean in treatment — lemon, capers, olives, tomato, oregano — and those point at Italian and Spanish whites more than at anything else.

What to actually open

Vermentino. Sicilian swordfish is the classic dish and Vermentino grows all along that coast. Textured, citrus and green-almond, with a faintly bitter finish that meets capers and olives.

A dry rosé. The most flexible answer, particularly for grilled swordfish with a tomato or salsa verde treatment — enough fruit for the char, no tannin.

Mencía or a Beaujolais cru if you want red. Light, peppery, low in tannin; serve it cool.

By treatment

Grilled plainly with lemon: keep it simple and sharp — Vermentino, or an Assyrtiko.

Pesce spada alla ghiotta, the Sicilian braise with tomato, olives, capers and pine nuts, is sweet-sour and substantial. That is where a light red genuinely earns its place, or a fuller Fiano.

With a salsa verde or gremolata — raw herbs, garlic, lemon — the acidity bar rises and the oak bar drops to zero. Raw garlic turns oaked wine bitter, as it does with pesto.

What to avoid: oaked whites throughout, and any red with real tannin. Swordfish is meaty but it is not beef.

Common questions

What wine goes with swordfish?

Vermentino is the classic Sicilian answer, a dry rosé is the most flexible, and a light Mencía works if the fish is grilled.

Can you drink red wine with swordfish?

Carefully, yes — it is dense enough not to be flattened and lean enough to avoid the metallic reaction that oily fish causes. Keep the tannin low and serve it cool.

What about swordfish with capers and olives?

That Sicilian treatment is sweet-sour and substantial, and it is where a light red earns its place — or a fuller white like Fiano.

Should the wine be oaked?

No. Swordfish is usually cooked with lemon, capers or garlic, and oak turns bitter against all three.
