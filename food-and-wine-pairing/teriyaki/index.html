What Wine Goes With Chicken Teriyaki?

Salty, sweet and loaded with umami — the glaze picks the wine, not the chicken.

A Riesling with some sugar left in it. The glaze is sweet, so the wine cannot be bone dry; it is salty, so the wine needs sharp acidity; and it is thick with umami, so the wine must bring no tannin at all.

Teriyaki is one of those dishes where the protein barely matters to the pairing. Teri means shine and yaki means grilled — the name describes the lacquer the sauce leaves behind, not the chicken underneath it. Solve the sauce and you have solved the dish.

What is actually in the glaze

A proper teriyaki tare is four ingredients: soy sauce, mirin, sake and sugar. The mirin and the caramelising sugar give the finished dish its gloss. The soy sauce brings the salt and, far more importantly, the savour.

That savour is the whole pairing problem. Brewed soy sauce carries roughly 980 to 1,680 mg of free glutamate per 100 ml in the ordinary dark koikuchi style, which is an enormous quantity of umami for a couple of spoonfuls of anything — and umami is the taste most hostile to red wine there is.

The bottles that work

  • Off-dry Riesling — the textbook answer and genuinely the best one. A Mosel Kabinett at 8% alcohol has sugar, acid and lightness in exactly the right proportions.
  • Pinot Noir — the red to reach for if you want red. Keep it light and unoaked.
  • Gamay — a Beaujolais, served cool. Juicy, and almost no grip.
  • Zweigelt — the sleeper pick. Austria's most-planted red grape, a 1922 crossing of Blaufränkisch and St Laurent, giving sour-cherry fruit with very little tannin.
  • Dry rosé — not a pale Provence, which the glaze flattens. A Tavel or a Spanish rosado has enough fruit and grip to still be there afterwards.

One thing worth being clear about: only the Riesling actually contains sugar. The reds and the rosé are dry. Their ripe fruit simply reads as juicy against the glaze.

Skip the Chardonnay

Oaked Chardonnay is the wrong answer here, despite being recommended for almost anything with chicken in the name. Toasty oak, caramelised sugar and soy are three sweet-savoury notes stacked on top of one another, and the result is muddy.

Avoid anything big and tannic as well — Cabernet, Barolo, a serious Bordeaux — and avoid bone-dry neutral whites, which the glaze walks straight over.

Salt helps, umami hurts, and umami wins

Two things in that sauce pull the wine in opposite directions. Salt suppresses bitterness, so it makes tannin taste softer and fruit taste riper — that effect is working in your favour. Umami does precisely the reverse: it lifts your perception of bitterness and astringency while flattening fruit, body and sweetness.

With teriyaki, the umami wins. A tannic red poured against a soy glaze arrives tasting metallic and stripped of everything that made it enjoyable. The fix is not to find a red tough enough to fight back, but to choose one with almost nothing for the umami to sharpen — which is why every red above is light and low in tannin.

Temperature finishes the job. Pour the Riesling at 7–10°C (45–50°F) and the light reds at 12–14°C (54–57°F); serve either much warmer and the alcohol starts to read as heat against the salt. The serving temperature guide covers every other style.

FAQ

Questions & answers

What wine goes best with chicken teriyaki?

An off-dry Riesling. Its residual sugar matches the sweetness of the soy glaze, its high acidity handles the salt, and it has no tannin to clash with the umami. A Mosel Kabinett at around 8% alcohol is the ideal shape.

Is there a red that survives teriyaki sauce?

One that is light and low in tannin, yes. Pinot Noir, Gamay and Austrian Zweigelt all hold up served slightly cool. Big tannic reds do not — the umami in the soy sauce makes them taste bitter and metallic.

Why does soy sauce make red wine taste bad?

Soy sauce is extremely high in free glutamate, and umami increases your perception of bitterness and astringency while suppressing fruit and body. A tannic red ends up tasting harsh and hollow, even though the salt in the sauce is working the other way.

Does Chardonnay go with teriyaki?

An oaked Chardonnay is a poor match. Its toasty, buttery character piles onto the caramelised sugar in the glaze and the pairing turns heavy and muddled. If you want a white, choose something with acidity and a touch of sweetness instead.
