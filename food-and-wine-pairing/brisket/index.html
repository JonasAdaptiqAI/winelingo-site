What Wine Goes With Brisket?

Smoked or braised, and the two want different wines.

By Jonas Egeskov · Updated 6 September 2026

Zinfandel or Malbec for smoked brisket; a Syrah or Rhône blend for braised. Ripe fruit for the rub, savour for the braise.

Brisket is a tough, heavily worked cut that only becomes tender after many hours — either smoked low and slow with a spice rub, or braised in liquid. Both give you rendered fat and deep savour, but the smoked version adds two things a braise does not: a sweet, peppery bark and a heavy dose of smoke.

Fat earns tannin, smoke complicates it

Brisket is fatty, and fat binds tannin and carries it away, which means a structured red works here in a way it would not with a lean cut. That much is straightforward.

Smoke is the complication. Smoked food carries a bitter, phenolic edge, and bitterness in food stacks with bitterness in wine rather than cancelling it. Heavy new oak on top of heavy smoke produces something acrid, so the wine wants ripe fruit and moderate oak rather than a big toasty one.

The rub adds sugar as well — most Texas rubs are salt, pepper and often a little brown sugar, which caramelises into the bark. Sugar in food makes a dry wine taste sharper, so generous fruit is doing double duty.

What to actually open

Zinfandel. Brambly, jammy fruit and a peppery edge that meets the bark. The American answer for American barbecue, and correct — with the usual caveat that it often runs 15% and higher, which fights a peppery or spicy rub. Primitivo is the same grape at a lower strength.

Malbec. Plush, dark and forgiving, with softer tannin than Cabernet and enough fruit for a sweet bark. The most reliable choice.

Syrah for braised brisket, where the flavour is savoury rather than smoky. Its cured-meat and black-pepper character meets a long braise better than fruit-led wines do.

The sauce, and the Jewish braise

A sweet barbecue sauce ladled on top raises the sugar considerably; lean further towards ripe fruit and away from anything austere.

Braised brisket in the Ashkenazi tradition — with onions, carrots and often a little wine — is a different dish: no smoke, no sweet bark, deeply savoury. That takes a savoury red comfortably, a Rioja Reserva or a Côtes du Rhône, and it is one of the few beef dishes where an older, gentler bottle is better than a young one.

Serve either version cool rather than warm. Brisket is heavy, hot and fatty, and a red at room temperature tastes alcoholic beside it.

Common questions

What wine goes with brisket?

Zinfandel or Malbec for smoked brisket, and a Syrah or Rhône blend for a braise. Ripe fruit for the rub, savour for the braise.

Why does smoke change the wine choice?

Smoked food carries a bitter, phenolic edge, and bitterness in food stacks with bitterness in wine. Heavy new oak on top of heavy smoke tastes acrid, so keep the oak moderate and the fruit ripe.

Can brisket take a big tannic red?

More than most cuts — it is fatty, and fat binds tannin and carries it off. Just avoid the combination of high tannin, heavy oak and heavy smoke.

What about braised brisket rather than smoked?

No smoke and no sweet bark means a savoury dish, which suits a savoury red — a Rioja Reserva or Côtes du Rhône, and one with some age.
