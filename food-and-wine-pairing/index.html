Food & wine pairing

What to drink with dinner

Simple, reliable wine pairings for everyday food — and the plain-language reason each one works, from cheese and steak to spicy takeaway and dessert.

Cheese

Aged Cheddar or Parmigiano-Reggiano

Tawny PortSauternesVintage ChampagneBaroloAged Bordeaux

Hard aged cheeses are intensely salty, fatty, and savoury, and the easiest wins come from contrast rather than brute strength. A touch of sweetness plays off the salt: Tawny Port and Sauternes wrap the sharp, salty cheese in nutty or honeyed sweetness, the classic sweet-salty match. High acidity and bubbles do the other job, cutting through the fat and refreshing your palate, which is why a crisp Vintage Champagne (its bready, nutty character echoing aged Parmigiano) is a celebrated pairing. If you prefer a dry red, reach for a savoury, umami-friendly one: the salt in the cheese also softens a red’s tannin and makes it taste smoother, so an umami-rich Barolo or a mellow aged Bordeaux works far better than a young, sharply tannic bottle, which can clash and taste hollow against sharp Cheddar.

Why it works: Meet salt and fat with sweetness, acidity, or bubbles (contrast)

Brie or Camembert

ChampagneCremantChardonnaylight low-tannin Pinot Noir

Soft bloomy cheeses coat the palate with butterfat, so you want a wine that either refreshes between bites or echoes the rind. Sparkling Champagne and Cremant work by cutting the fat: their bubbles and bright acidity scrub the richness away and reset the palate, while their toasty, lees-aged notes mirror the mushroomy rind. A crisp Chardonnay does the same job with acidity alone (no bubbles). A light, low-tannin Pinot Noir works differently, by complementing rather than cutting: its earthy, forest-floor and red-fruit notes echo the mushroomy rind. Keep the red light and fruit-forward, though, because the bloomy Penicillium rind can react with firm red tannins to taste metallic and bitter, so a bigger, more tannic red clashes.

Why it works: Bubbles and acid cut through fat (sparkling and Chardonnay); a light, low-tannin Pinot Noir instead complements the earthy rind

Fresh chevre (goat cheese)

SancerrePouilly-FumeNew Zealand Sauvignon BlancVinho Verde

Fresh goat cheese is tangy, bright, and lemony, so a flabby low-acid wine would taste dull beside it. A crisp high-acid white mirrors that zing and the citrus-herb notes, letting the two lift each other rather than clash.

Why it works: Acid loves acid

Roquefort or Stilton blue cheese

SauternesTawny PortLate Harvest RieslingPedro Ximenez Sherry

Blue cheese is intensely salty, pungent, and sharp, which makes a dry wine taste harsh and metallic. A rich sweet wine has enough sugar and concentration to counterbalance the salt and tame the bite, creating the classic sweet-salty contrast.

Why it works: With salty, pungent food, the wine should be at least as sweet as the dish.

Dessert & nibbles

Cured charcuterie board (prosciutto, salami, saucisson)

LambruscoBeaujolaisDry RoseChampagneDry Riesling

Bright acidity cuts through the fatty, salty cured meats and refreshes your palate between bites, while the wine’s own acidity makes it taste lively rather than flat against the salt. Stick to light-bodied, high-acid wines with little to no tannin (juicy reds, rose, and sparkling) so they match the savoury weight without overpowering it. Avoid big tannic reds here: salt makes firm tannin taste harsher and more bitter, which is why low-tannin styles work best.

Why it works: Acidity (plus low tannin and light body) cuts through fat and salt and refreshes the palate

Flourless dark chocolate cake (70% cocoa)

Ruby PortLate Bottled Vintage PortBanyulsMauryPedro Ximénez Sherry

These fortified sweet reds carry more sugar and dark-fruit intensity than the chocolate itself, so the dessert tastes balanced rather than bitter. Their high alcohol and ripe-berry flavours echo the cocoa’s richness instead of fighting it.

Why it works: Sweet must out-sweet the dish

Lemon or mixed-berry fruit tart

Moscato d’AstiRieslingDemi-Sec ChampagneSauternesVouvray Demi-Sec

Each of these wines is genuinely sweet, so it stays at least as sweet as the tart — that’s the key: if the wine is less sweet than the dessert, the tart’s sugar makes it taste sour and thin. Their high acidity keeps the sweetness from feeling heavy or cloying and refreshes the palate against the citrus and berry tang.

Why it works: For dessert, pick a sweet wine that’s at least as sweet as the dish, with fresh acidity to keep it from cloying

Salted Marcona almonds and olives (apéritif nibbles)

Fino SherryManzanilla SherryChampagneDry Sparkling WineBone-dry Provence Rosé

Bone-dry, savoury, often saline wines turn even more crisp and refreshing against salt, which softens any perceived bitterness. The bubbles and high acidity scrub the palate clean between salty bites.

Why it works: Bubbles and salt; dry wine versus salt

Fish & shellfish

Grilled salmon

Pinot NoirGamayDry roséUnoaked ChardonnayGrüner Veltliner

Grilled salmon is an oily, meaty fish, so it needs a wine with two things: enough body to match its weight, and fresh acidity to cut through the richness. The two reds here are deliberately light and low in tannin — Pinot Noir and Gamay (Beaujolais) have bright acidity and soft, gentle tannins, so they refresh each bite without the harsh, drying clash that tannin gives oily fish. The three whites and rosé work the same way from the other direction: a dry Provence-style rosé, an unoaked Chardonnay, and a zesty Grüner Veltliner are all medium-bodied and high in acidity, so they stand up to the salmon’s weight while keeping the palate clean. The thread through every choice is high acidity plus a light-to-medium body and no heavy tannin.

Why it works: For oily fish, pick high-acid wines that match its weight — light, low-tannin reds or crisp medium whites.

Raw oysters on the half shell

ChampagneChablisMuscadetSancerre

These wines are bone-dry, high in acidity, and unoaked, so their crispness lifts the briny salt of the oyster instead of fighting it. Champagne’s bubbles also scrub the palate clean between bites.

Why it works: Bubbles & salt / Acid loves brine

Steamed lobster or crab with butter

Oaked ChardonnayChampagneViognierChenin Blanc

Lobster and crab are sweet, rich, and served with melted butter, so they call for a fuller-bodied white with enough texture and weight to match. An oaked Chardonnay’s creamy body mirrors the buttery richness while its acidity keeps each bite from feeling heavy.

Why it works: Richness loves richness / Match weight to weight

Sushi and sashimi

RieslingSauvignon BlancChampagneAlbariñoSparkling wine

Delicate raw fish needs a light, high-acid wine that won’t bury it, while a touch of sweetness in off-dry Riesling tames wasabi heat and balances soy and pickled ginger. The acidity also keeps the palate fresh.

Why it works: A little sweetness tames spice; acid keeps it fresh

Pasta & pizza

Margherita pizza

BarberaSangioveseChiantiLambrusco

Margherita combines acidic tomato with rich mozzarella, so a medium-bodied, high-acid red matches the tomato’s tang while having enough fruit to handle the cheese. The wine’s weight mirrors the easygoing, casual weight of the pizza.

Why it works: Match weight to weight

Mushroom risotto

Pinot NoirDolcettoGamayOaked Chardonnay

Mushrooms are rich in umami — that deep, savoury, brothy taste you also get from parmesan or a good stock. This pairing works in two ways. For the reds, pick a light- to medium-bodied, gentle red with soft tannins: Pinot Noir, Dolcetto, and Gamay (Beaujolais) all have earthy, savoury notes that echo the mushrooms, plus bright fruit and low tannin so they refresh the creamy risotto without fighting it. (Tannin is the drying, grippy feel in red wine; a delicate risotto has little fat or chew to soften it, and umami actually makes tannin taste harsher — so a big, tannic red like Barolo can taste bitter and overpower the dish. Keep the red light.) The oaked Chardonnay works for a different reason: it’s chosen by weight and texture, its creamy, buttery body matching the creamy rice, while gentle toasty, nutty oak mirrors the savoury dish.

Why it works: Two ways in: match earthy with earthy (a light, low-tannin red), or match richness with richness (a creamy, oaked white)

Spaghetti carbonara (creamy egg-and-cheese sauce)

Pinot GrigioFrascatiVerdicchiounoaked ChardonnaySoave

The rich, fatty cream-and-egg sauce coats your palate, so a crisp, dry white with lively acidity scrubs the richness away between bites. A light, unoaked white keeps the pairing refreshing rather than heavy.

Why it works: Acidity cuts through fat

Spaghetti with tomato sauce (marinara)

ChiantiSangioveseBarberaMontepulciano d’Abruzzo

Tomato sauce is high in acidity, so it needs a wine with equally bright acidity or it will make the wine taste flat and dull. A high-acid red like Sangiovese matches the tang and keeps both the wine and the dish feeling fresh.

Why it works: Acid loves acid

Poultry & pork

Grilled pork chop

Pinot NoirZinfandelGrenacheChenin BlancRiesling

Pork is a mild, lightly fatty meat that pairs with a medium-bodied red whose gentle tannins and acidity refresh the palate between bites. With applesauce or a fruity glaze, an off-dry white like Riesling mirrors that sweetness and balances the dish.

Why it works: Acidity and a touch of sweetness balance pork’s richness

Roast chicken

Oaked ChardonnayChablisPinot NoirBeaujolaisGrenache

Roast chicken is a medium-weight, savoury dish, so a medium-bodied wine matches its richness without overpowering the mild meat. An oaked Chardonnay echoes the golden, buttery roasted skin, while crisp, unoaked Chablis (also Chardonnay, but the opposite style) shows the lighter side and lets its acidity cut the richer parts. For reds, Pinot Noir, Beaujolais (Gamay), and Grenache all stay light-to-medium with soft tannins and bright fruit that keep them in balance with the meat.

Why it works: Match weight to weight

Roast turkey

Pinot NoirBeaujolaisGrenacheCinsaultRiesling

Turkey is lean and delicate, so a light-bodied, low-tannin red with bright acidity and ripe red fruit adds juicy flavour without drying the palate or fighting the meat. That same fresh acidity cuts through buttery, herbed stuffing and gravy. A high-acid white like Riesling works on the same logic and is especially good with cranberry and glazed sides. Avoid big, high-alcohol reds (such as Zinfandel), whose weight and tannin overwhelm the lean meat.

Why it works: Lean white meat loves light, low-tannin reds and high-acid whites

Seared duck breast

Pinot NoirSyrahGrenacheCôtes du RhôneChâteauneuf-du-Pape

Seared duck is rich, fatty, and gamey, so it wants a red with enough acidity and ripe fruit to refresh the palate between bites. Pinot Noir is the classic match: its bright acidity and silky red fruit cut through the richness and echo the meat’s savoury depth, with no need for heavy tannin. The Grenache-led Rhône wines (Grenache, Côtes du Rhône, Châteauneuf-du-Pape) bring warmth, full body, and ripe red fruit that stand up to the duck’s intensity through generosity rather than grip, since Grenache is naturally soft and low in tannin. Syrah adds the firmest structure of the group, its dark fruit, pepper, and tannin gripping the fattier, crispier bits while still complementing the gamey flavour.

Why it works: Acidity (with body and ripe fruit) refreshes rich, fatty meat

Red meat & game

Beef burger

ZinfandelMalbecShirazCôtes du RhôneMerlot

A burger is casual, fatty, and often a little sweet from the bun and toppings, so a ripe, fruit-forward red with soft tannins cleans up the grease without feeling too serious. The juicy fruit complements the char while moderate tannin handles the fat.

Why it works: Tannin cuts through fat

Grilled ribeye steak

Cabernet SauvignonNapa CabernetBordeauxMalbecShiraz

The fat and char of a juicy ribeye coat your palate, and the firm tannins in these bold reds bind to that fat and scrub it away, leaving each bite fresh. The wine’s full body also stands up to the meat’s richness instead of getting flattened.

Why it works: Tannin cuts through fat

Roast leg of lamb with rosemary

BordeauxCabernet SauvignonMerlotRiojaSyrah

Lamb is rich and slightly gamey but more delicate than beef, so a medium-to-full red with ripe tannin matches its weight without overpowering it. Herbal, savoury notes in Bordeaux and Rioja echo the rosemary and roasted flavours.

Why it works: Match weight to weight

Roast venison

SyrahPinot NoirNebbioloChâteauneuf-du-PapeBordeaux blend

Venison is lean but intensely savoury and gamey, so it needs a wine that matches that intensity and brings firm tannin and bright acid to cut the richness while echoing the meat’s earthy, peppery character. Syrah adds smoky black pepper, Pinot Noir and Nebbiolo bring forest-floor and truffle earthiness with grippy structure, and Châteauneuf-du-Pape and a savoury Bordeaux blend offer dark fruit wrapped in herbs, spice, and tannin — each mirrors the meat rather than fighting it.

Why it works: Match intensity and structure to the meat’s earthy, savoury flavours

Spicy & Asian

Chicken teriyaki (soy-glazed)

Off-dry RieslingPinot NoirGamayDry roséZweigelt

Teriyaki is medium-weight and salty-sweet with lots of umami, so the goal is a light, juicy, low-tannin wine that won’t fight the glaze. The off-dry Riesling is the textbook match: its touch of residual sugar mirrors the sweet soy glaze while its high acidity cuts the salt. The light reds (Pinot Noir, Gamay, Zweigelt) and the dry rosé bring bright, ripe fruit and fresh acidity but almost no tannin, so nothing turns bitter against the salty umami. Note that the reds and rosé are dry, not sweet — their fruitiness just reads as juicy; only the Riesling actually carries sugar. All of them stay light enough to match the dish’s weight rather than overpower it.

Why it works: Match the weight, keep tannin low; sweet or high-acid wines tame salty-sweet umami

Indian butter chicken

Off-dry RieslingGewurztraminerPinot GrisViognierOff-dry rose

Butter chicken is mild in heat but rich, creamy and fragrant with garam masala. A touch of sweetness in off-dry wines like Riesling and Gewurztraminer takes the edge off the warm spices, while their exotic, floral aromas echo the dish’s spice blend instead of fighting it. Fuller, perfumed whites such as Pinot Gris and Viognier match the weight of the creamy sauce and bring peach and apricot notes; on the off-dry Riesling and rose, lively acidity also helps refresh the palate between rich, buttery bites. Pick an off-dry style (look for “off-dry” or Alsace on the label) so the wine has a hint of sweetness.

Why it works: Aromatic, off-dry whites: a hint of sweetness tames the spice, perfume matches the spices

Sichuan mapo tofu (chilli and Sichuan peppercorn)

Off-dry RieslingMoscato d’AstiOff-dry Mosel RieslingAmabile LambruscoDemi-sec sparkling

Residual sugar is the main thing that tames capsaicin, so a wine with a touch of sweetness cools the chilli burn, while keeping the alcohol low matters because alcohol intensifies that heat. The dish’s Sichuan peppercorn adds a tingling, numbing (mala) buzz rather than more heat, and a chilled, lightly sweet wine refreshes the palate between bites; a gentle frizzante or acidity lifts the rich, oily tofu without fighting the spice. Skip dry, high-alcohol or tannic reds, which read hotter and more bitter against fiery food.

Why it works: A little sweetness plus low alcohol cools the chilli burn

Thai green curry

RieslingGewurztraminerChenin BlancPinot Gris

A touch of residual sugar buffers the chilli heat and balances the salty-sweet coconut sauce, while the wine’s acidity keeps things refreshing. Aim for a wine labelled off-dry or demi-sec; a bone-dry wine (such as a standard Italian Pinot Grigio) would taste harsh and make the spice burn hotter.

Why it works: A little sweetness tames the heat

Biryani

GewürztraminerRieslingGrenacheProvence rosé

Warm spice is not the same problem as chilli heat — cardamom and cloves are aromatic rather than painful, and do not punish alcohol. Only the chilli level should push you towards low alcohol and a colder glass.

Why it works: Aromatic wine for an aromatic dish

Dim sum

ChampagneCavaProseccoRiesling

You are not pairing a dish, you are pairing a table — steamed, fried, savoury and sweet arrive together. Sparkling wine cuts the fried items, refreshes between very different mouthfuls, and keeps its shape against the sweet ones.

Why it works: Good with everything beats perfect with one

Korean BBQ

BeaujolaisGamayRieslingZinfandel

A sweet marinade makes a dry red taste hollow, gochujang heat is amplified by alcohol and sharpened by tannin, and the fermented sides out-acid a soft wine. A big oaked red loses on all three counts.

Why it works: Fruit and freshness, served cold

Pad Thai

RieslingMosel RieslingGewürztraminerGamay

Tamarind, palm sugar and fish sauce make this sour, sweet and intensely savoury before the chilli arrives. Off-dry Riesling is the rare style holding sugar, high acidity, low alcohol and no tannin all at once.

Why it works: One wine for four flavours

Pho

RieslingGrüner VeltlinerPinot NoirAlsace Riesling

The broth is clear and aromatic rather than rich, so the danger is overwhelming it rather than clashing. Star anise and cinnamon want a wine with its own aromatics — but not a herbaceous one, which doubles up on the raw herbs.

Why it works: Aromatic, not herbaceous; light, not heavy

Ramen

ChampagneCavaBeaujolaisGamay

A tonkotsu broth is fat, salt and umami emulsified together. Carbonation physically lifts the fat off the palate in a way acidity alone cannot, and the salt makes the wine taste softer and fruitier in return.

Why it works: Bubbles scrub fat; salt flatters wine

Vegetarian & salads

Green leafy salad with vinaigrette

Sauvignon BlancGrüner VeltlinerVinho VerdeAssyrtiko

A tart vinaigrette is high in acid, and a low-acid wine tastes flat and dull beside it. Each of these whites is genuinely crisp and high in acid, so it meets the dressing head-on and both stay bright and refreshing instead of one flattening the other.

Why it works: Acid loves acid: match a tart, vinaigrette-dressed dish with an equally high-acid wine.

Grilled vegetables (zucchini, peppers, eggplant)

Dry Provence roséGrenacheBarberaPinot Noir

Grilling adds smoky char and a touch of bitterness, so the goal is a wine that won’t amplify it. Low-tannin, fruit-forward reds and a dry rosé work because tannin would magnify the char’s bitterness, while their bright acidity and ripe fruit refresh the palate and echo the sweet, caramelized edges of the vegetables.

Why it works: Keep tannin low so char isn’t amplified

Lentil or bean stew

ChiantiCôtes du RhôneTempranilloMontepulciano d’Abruzzo

Earthy, hearty legumes have a savoury, almost meaty richness that calls for a medium-bodied red. The wine’s gentle tannin and earthy notes mirror the dish and keep each spoonful feeling lively rather than heavy.

Why it works: Match weight to weight

Tomato bruschetta

ChiantiBarberaVerdicchioDry rosé

Fresh tomatoes are surprisingly acidic, so a soft, low-acid wine tastes flabby next to them. A bright, high-acid Italian white or red matches the tomato’s tang and refreshes the palate against the olive oil and garlic.

Why it works: Acid loves acid

Aubergine parmigiana

AglianicoBarberaNero d’AvolaFiano

Tomato normally means keeping tannin down, but melted mozzarella and Parmesan give the tannins protein to bind to before they reach your saliva. That softens a firm red on contact, so parmigiana takes more structure than any other tomato dish.

Why it works: Fat softens tannin

Beetroot salad

SancerrePinot NoirProvence roséSauvignon Blanc

Beetroot’s earthiness comes from geosmin, detectable at a few parts per trillion, so it cannot be masked — meet it with an earthy wine instead. The vinaigrette is the harder problem: vinegar leaves a less acidic wine tasting flat.

Why it works: Meet earth with earth, out-acid the dressing

Butternut squash soup

Vouvray demi-secChenin BlancViognierGewürztraminer

The soup is sweeter than it registers, and sugar in food suppresses fruit and sweetness in the wine while leaving acidity untouched. A bone-dry white therefore tastes thin and sour beside it.

Why it works: The wine must be as sweet as the dish

Falafel

AssyrtikoGrüner VeltlinerProvence roséVermentino

Acidity is doing two jobs: cutting the oil from deep-frying, and keeping up with the lemon and sharp fermented vegetables served alongside. A wine less acidic than the plate tastes hollow.

Why it works: High acid, low alcohol, no oak

Leek and potato soup

ChablisMelon de BourgogneGrüner VeltlinerCrémant

Nothing in this bowl will attack a wine — the risk is the opposite, that a soft low-acid white merges into the cream and disappears. Acidity provides the contrast, and restraint keeps the delicate leek audible.

Why it works: Bring the acidity the dish lacks

Ratatouille

Provence roséGrenacheCinsaultTavel

Vegetables and olive oil give tannin nothing to soften it, so a structured red tastes hard and metallic. The dish and the wines come from the same corner of France, which is not a coincidence — both evolved for the same table.

Why it works: Regional match, low tannin, serve cool

Roasted cauliflower

Fino sherryChardonnayGrüner VeltlinerWhite Burgundy

Roasting drives off the sulphur compounds that make raw brassicas awkward and browns the vegetable instead. What you are pairing with is caramelisation, not cauliflower — so match nuttiness rather than reaching for something citrusy.

Why it works: Match the browning, not the vegetable
