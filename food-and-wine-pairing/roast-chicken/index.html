Roast Chicken: Red or White Wine?

Both are right. The deciding vote belongs to whatever is in the roasting tin.

Pour Chardonnay if the skin is properly golden, and a light red if there is gravy. Oaked Chardonnay, Chablis, Pinot Noir, Beaujolais and Grenache are all genuinely good answers here — four wines that agree with each other about almost nothing.

Roast chicken is the most accommodating main course there is. The meat is mild, medium-weight and faintly sweet, so it will not fight anything you open. That is precisely why the decision gets made somewhere else on the plate — by the fat in the skin, the herbs in the cavity and the gravy in the jug.

Chardonnay can go either way

An oaked Chardonnay — a white Burgundy from Meursault, or a warmer-climate bottle from California or Margaret River — matches the roast rather than cutting it. Barrel ageing and malolactic conversion give it a broad, buttery, faintly toasty character that lines up with crisp golden skin and the browned juices in the tin. This is the bottle for a bird with butter rubbed under the skin.

Chablis is the same grape grown at the cool northern edge of Burgundy, vinified without new oak in all but a handful of premier and grand cru cellars. It does the opposite job: enough acidity to cut clean through the fat and reset your palate between mouthfuls. This is the bottle for a lemon-and-thyme chicken, or for the cold leftovers tomorrow.

If you would rather have red

Keep it light and keep the tannin down. Chicken is lean enough that a grippy red has nothing to bind to, so it stays in your mouth as a dry chalky film and drags the meat down with it — our guide to tannin explains the mechanism.

Pinot Noir is the elegant choice and the one to take when someone else is cooking. Beaujolais — Gamay, from ten named crus including Fleurie, Morgon and Moulin-à-Vent — is better value and the more cheerful drink. Grenache earns its place when the bird has been roasted with paprika, chorizo or a lot of garlic.

Serve the Pinot Noir and the Beaujolais at 12–14°C (54–57°F), which after an afternoon of roasting means a spell in the fridge before anyone sits down. Grenache takes a degree or two more. The serving temperature guide is there if you want the numbers for anything else.

The chicken with an appellation

The Poulet de Bresse is the only chicken in the world with an appellation d’origine contrôlée, granted by a French law of 1 August 1957 — the same class of legal protection that governs Champagne and Roquefort. The rulebook reads like a wine cahier des charges. Ten square metres of grass per bird. No slaughter before the 108th day. A finishing stretch of at least ten days shut in a slatted wooden crate, an épinette, kept in a dark and quiet room. Maize must supply at least 40 per cent of the ration, and from day thirty-six every bird gets dairy on top: half a kilo of milk powder, or four litres of milk.

Bresse sits in eastern France across Ain, Saône-et-Loire and the Jura, a short drive from Beaujolais and from the Chardonnay vineyards of the Mâconnais. Its signature dish is poulet de Bresse à la crème aux morilles — cream, morels, and a bird already fattened on milk. That is a white Burgundy plate by construction, and it makes the case for the oaked Chardonnay better than any tasting note. The Bresse turkey, incidentally, won an appellation of its own in 1976, and it is the only turkey on earth that has one.

What the roasting tin is telling you

Everything that settles this pairing is already in the tin. Lemon, thyme and a light hand with the butter: Chablis, or anything else crisp and unoaked. Butter worked under the skin and a deep golden crust: the oaked Chardonnay stops being an indulgence and becomes the point. Once there is gravy in the jug and roast potatoes soaking it up, the meaty pan juices pull the whole plate towards red — Pinot Noir, or Beaujolais if the table is large and the budget is not. Paprika, harissa or a heavy spice rub sends you to Grenache, or to a dry rosé.

What to avoid: a young, firmly tannic Cabernet Sauvignon, which is the commonest mistake made at this table. There is nothing in mild white meat to soften that structure, so the wine sits on top of the food and the food vanishes underneath it. Heavy oak fails from the other direction — on a plainly roasted bird, with no butter and no gravy, all that toast and vanilla has nothing to hold on to.

FAQ

Questions & answers

Red or white wine with roast chicken?

Both work. Choose an oaked Chardonnay when the skin is buttery and golden, Chablis when the bird is lemony or herby, and a light red such as Pinot Noir or Beaujolais when there is proper gravy on the plate.

What is the best wine for roast chicken?

If you want one bottle that suits almost any roast chicken, make it a good Pinot Noir served slightly cool. It has the acidity for the fat and the weight for the meat, without tannin that would dry it out.

Does Chablis go with roast chicken?

Very well, especially with a lemon-and-herb bird or cold chicken the next day. Chablis is Chardonnay from the cool northern edge of Burgundy, usually made without oak and always high in acidity, so it cuts the fat rather than echoing it — the opposite approach to an oaked Chardonnay.

What wine should you avoid with chicken?

A young, firmly tannic red such as Cabernet Sauvignon. Chicken is lean, so the tannin has nothing to soften against, and the wine ends up tasting bitter while flattening the meat.
