What Wine Goes With Thai Green Curry?

Coconut, lemongrass, fish sauce — and rather more chilli than the name lets on.

Riesling, and it has to have some sweetness in it. Green curry has four awkward corners — the chilli, the coconut fat, the salt of the fish sauce and the palm sugar in the paste — and nothing else covers all four at once.

The trap is the name. Gaeng keow wan translates as "green sweet curry", and English menus have been quietly promising something mild ever since. The wan describes the pale shade of green, not the flavour, and the paste is built on fresh green bird's eye chillies. Green is frequently the hotter of the two curries, not the gentler one.

Match the bottle to the coconut cream

Green curry is not one dish. A lean, chilli-forward version made with fish sauce and barely any palm sugar is a different pairing problem from the thick takeaway kind, and the bottle should move with it.

Off-dry Riesling is the default, and for the leaner end it is the whole answer: sugar, real acidity and low alcohol in one bottle, which very few grapes manage together. Go towards the drier end of off-dry and it will still cope.

As the coconut cream goes up the wine has to gain weight with it. Chenin Blanc is the step nobody takes — a Vouvray demi-sec brings honeyed apple and enough acidity to cut the fat, usually for less money than the Alsace shelf. For the richest versions, Alsace Pinot Gris has the smoke and the texture to stay in the conversation. Gewurztraminer is the divisive one: its perfume runs so close to the galangal and lime leaf in the paste that the pairing either feels uncanny or feels like the same note played twice. Worth trying once to find out which camp you are in.

Sweetness is the lever, acidity is the balance

Capsaicin binds to TRPV1, the same receptor that responds to genuine heat. Sugar does not switch it off, but it competes for attention: in controlled trials a sucrose rinse measurably reduced capsaicin burn compared with water, and it still worked when the sugar was applied to a different part of the tongue from the chilli — so the relief comes from the sensation of sweetness, not from sugar mopping anything up.

Acidity does the second job. Green curry is a coconut-fat dish, and fat needs cutting or the third mouthful arrives tasting like the first, only heavier. Riesling keeps coming up because very few other grapes carry real sugar and searing acidity in the same glass without either cancelling the other. Why the reds lose here

Tannin has nothing to grip in this dish, and that matters more than the chilli does. Green curry has no browned meat, no char, no roasting anywhere in it — just coconut milk, herbs and fish sauce. A serious red is exposed before the heat has even started work on it, which is why good bottles tend to taste hollow with this dish rather than merely clashing.

Then the chilli arrives and turns the tannin bitter. If red has to be on the table, a sweeter Lambrusco served cold is the escape route — but treat it as a rescue rather than a pairing. Skip oak entirely, and skip anything much above 13.5%.

The Pinot Grigio trap

This is where recommendation lists get people into trouble. Alsace Pinot Gris and Italian Pinot Grigio are the same grape and completely different wines. The Alsace version is full-bodied, textured and often carries residual sugar. The standard supermarket Italian bottling is bone-dry, light and deliberately neutral.

Pour the Italian one with green curry and it tastes thin and sour, and the chilli burns harder for it. If the label says Alsace, or the back says demi-sec, you have the right bottle; if it says Delle Venezie, you do not. Knowing how to read the label is worth more here than knowing the grape name.

FAQ

Questions & answers

What wine goes with Thai green curry?

An off-dry Riesling is the best single answer — it has residual sugar to buffer the chilli, high acidity to cut the coconut fat and low alcohol so the heat is not amplified. Gewurztraminer, Vouvray demi-sec and Alsace Pinot Gris also work.

Is Thai green curry milder than red curry?

Usually not. The pastes are near-identical apart from the chillies — red uses dried red chillies, green uses fresh green bird's eye chillies. The name means "green sweet", referring to the pale colour rather than the taste, which is why the dish has a reputation it does not deserve.

Can you drink Pinot Grigio with Thai curry?

Alsace Pinot Gris, yes — it is richer and often off-dry. Standard Italian Pinot Grigio, no: it is bone-dry and neutral, so it tastes sour beside the curry and leaves the chilli burning harder.

Does red wine work with Thai green curry?

Rarely. There is no browned meat or char in the dish, so tannin has nothing to bind to, and the chilli then turns that tannin bitter. A sweeter Lambrusco served cold is the one workable exception.
