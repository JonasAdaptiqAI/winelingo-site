What Wine Goes With Baked Ziti?

Tomato, ricotta and mozzarella, baked until the top catches. A cheese dish as much as a pasta one.

Sangiovese, Montepulciano or Nero d’Avola. Italian, medium-bodied, and more forgiving than a plain tomato pasta would be.

Baked ziti is tomato sauce, ricotta and mozzarella layered through short pasta and baked until the top browns. It is Italian-American rather than Italian, and the cheese is doing more work in the pairing than the tomato — which makes it easier to match than the ingredient list suggests.

The cheese buys you room

Raw tomato is hard on wine: it is acidic, and its glutamate content sharpens tannin and thins fruit. That normally means high acidity and light tannin. But tannin binds to protein and fat, and a generous layer of ricotta and melted mozzarella gives it plenty to bind to before it reaches your saliva.

So baked ziti tolerates more structure than tomato pasta does. Not a great deal more — this is not a steak — but enough that a medium-bodied red with real grip stops being a mistake.

What to actually open

Sangiovese. The default and the right one: sour cherry, dried herbs, high acidity to meet the tomato and moderate tannin that the cheese rounds off.

Montepulciano d’Abruzzo. Softer and darker, inexpensive, and easy to drink with a rich baked dish. The best value on this page.

Nero d’Avola if your sauce is sweet — many American versions carry a little sugar. Riper black fruit meets that sweetness instead of tasting sour beside it.

Sausage, and the browned top

A lot of baked ziti has Italian sausage through it, and that changes things slightly for the better: fennel and pork fat give the wine more to work with and let you move a step up in body — a Primitivo or an Aglianico rather than a light Sangiovese.

The browned cheese on top adds salt and a little bitterness from the char. Salt softens wine and makes it taste fruitier, which is helpful; the char argues mildly against heavy oak, which would add bitterness to bitterness.

Common questions

What wine goes with baked ziti?

Sangiovese, Montepulciano d’Abruzzo, or Nero d’Avola if the sauce is on the sweet side.

Why does baked ziti take more tannin than tomato pasta?

Because tannin binds to protein and fat. The ricotta and melted mozzarella give it plenty to bind to before it reaches your saliva, which softens a firmer red on contact.

What if there is sausage in it?

Move up in body. Fennel and pork fat give the wine more to work with, so a Primitivo or Aglianico suits it better than a light Sangiovese.

Does white wine work with baked ziti?

Less well. There is a lot of tomato and melted cheese here, and most whites lack the fruit weight to answer it. A textured Verdicchio is the best attempt.
