What Wine Goes With Pork Chops?

Pork sits between chicken and beef in weight, so the glaze picks the wine.

Match the glaze, not the meat. A plain grilled chop wants Pinot Noir; apple sauce wants an off-dry Chenin Blanc or Riesling; a sticky barbecue rub wants Zinfandel.

Pork on its own is mild, lightly fatty and sits between chicken and beef in weight — too neutral to dictate much of anything. It is one of very few main courses where the honest answer swings all the way from a delicate red to a faintly sweet white, and the thing doing the swinging is whatever you brushed on it.

The off-dry whites are not a cop-out

The apple-sauce row is where most people go wrong, because pouring a faintly sweet wine with a savoury main course feels like cheating. It is not cheating; it is the only approach that leaves the wine tasting like itself.

Put sugar on the plate and the dry wine on the table loses the argument: its fruit recedes, its acid juts forward, and something that was refreshing on its own arrives stripped and sour. A wine carrying a few grams of its own sugar meets the glaze on level ground, and its acidity still gets through the fat.

Buy a Vouvray demi-sec — Chenin Blanc from the Loire — but buy it with your eyes open, because Vouvray labels are notoriously unhelpful. No rule compels a grower to declare the sweetness, and a fair amount of the region’s classic off-dry wine leaves the cellar with nothing on the front but the word Vouvray. If the bottle does not say sec, demi-sec or tendre, ask the merchant rather than guess: the distance between a bone-dry Vouvray and a properly off-dry one is the entire pairing. A Mosel Riesling at Kabinett level removes the doubt, and at around 8 to 9% alcohol it keeps the meal light. The sweetness chart maps the labels.

Pork is pink now, and that changed the pairing

In 2011 the USDA cut its recommended cooking temperature for whole cuts of pork from 71°C (160°F) to 63°C (145°F) with a three-minute rest. Every piece of pork pairing advice written before that revision is quietly out of date.

A chop cooked to 63°C is juicy, faintly pink and still tastes of pork. A chop cooked to 71°C is grey, dry and crying out for a wine with enough acidity to rescue it. Now that the meat keeps its own moisture, you can pour something lighter and more delicate — which is how Pinot Noir got onto this list at all.

The cut matters too. A lean loin chop suits Pinot Noir. A fattier shoulder or blade chop, with more marbling and connective tissue, will happily take Zinfandel or Grenache.

Two bottles to put back

A big tannic Cabernet Sauvignon. Pork has neither the fat nor the char of a ribeye, so there is nothing for the tannin to soften against and the wine simply flattens the meat. Keep it for the steak.

Zinfandel, if the rub is genuinely hot. Zinfandel ripens unevenly and routinely lands between 14.5 and 16% alcohol, and alcohol amplifies chilli burn rather than cooling it. Sweet-smoky barbecue is Zinfandel’s dish; a chilli-heavy dry rub is not. Switch to the off-dry Riesling and the heat drops away.

FAQ

Questions & answers

What wine goes best with pork chops?

For a plain grilled chop, Pinot Noir. Change the seasoning and the answer changes: off-dry Chenin Blanc or Riesling with apple sauce, Zinfandel with a sweet barbecue glaze, Grenache with garlic and rosemary.

Red or white wine with pork?

Both are correct, which is unusual. Pork is mild and medium-weight, so the sauce decides. Savoury and herby leans red; sweet, fruity or creamy leans towards a white with a little residual sugar.

What wine goes with pork and apple sauce?

An off-dry white — Vouvray demi-sec or a German Riesling Kabinett. The sweetness of the apple would make a bone-dry wine taste sour and thin, while a lightly sweet wine matches it and still cuts the fat.

What wine goes with barbecue pork chops?

Zinfandel, provided the rub is sweet and smoky rather than fiery. Its ripe, jammy fruit matches the caramelised glaze. If there is serious chilli heat in the rub, its high alcohol will make the burn worse — pour an off-dry Riesling instead.
