What Wine Goes With Falafel?

Fried, herby, and served with something sharp. The sharp thing is what you pair against.

Assyrtiko from Greece, Grüner Veltliner, or a dry rosé. High acidity is not optional — it is doing two jobs at once here.

Falafel is deep-fried, and everything deep-fried asks the same thing of wine: acidity, to cut the oil and reset the palate. But falafel is rarely eaten alone. It arrives with tahini, sharp preserved vegetables, chilli sauce and a squeeze of lemon, and those accompaniments are louder than the fritter itself. Pair against the plate, not the fritter.

Two jobs for the acidity

The first is the frying. Fat coats the tongue and dulls everything that follows; acidity strips it away, which is why almost every fried food in the world is served with something sour beside it. The second is what comes with it. Sharply acidic accompaniments raise the bar the wine has to clear — a wine less acidic than the food will taste flabby and hollow, however good it is on its own.

Between them, that rules out a great deal. Soft, low-acid whites are out. Oaked whites are out, because oak plus fried food reads as heavy. Tannic reds are firmly out: there is no fat-and-protein structure here for tannin to bind to, and it will taste bitter beside the chickpeas.

What to actually open

Assyrtiko. The great white grape of Santorini, grown on volcanic soil and famous for holding onto ferocious acidity even in a very hot climate — most grapes lose their acid as they ripen in heat, and this one does not. Bone dry, faintly saline, citrus-driven, and the most interesting answer on this list.

Grüner Veltliner. The safe, widely available one. Its white-pepper edge matches the cumin and coriander in the fritter, and it deals with raw herbs — the parsley in falafel is not a garnish, it is a main ingredient — better than most whites.

A dry rosé, if there is a lot of chilli sauce. Rosé has enough fruit to soften heat without the tannin that would amplify it. Provence rosé or a Spanish rosado.

Tahini and chilli pull in opposite directions

Tahini is rich, nutty and sesame-heavy, and it pushes towards a wine with a little more weight — this is where a Chenin Blanc or a textured Vermentino earns its place over something lean.

Chilli pulls the other way. Capsaicin heat is amplified by alcohol, so a high-alcohol wine makes a hot sauce taste hotter, and tannin sharpens it further. If your falafel arrives under a serious amount of zhoug or harissa, drop to a lower-alcohol wine, keep it well chilled, and accept that this is not the evening for anything expensive.

Common questions

What wine goes with falafel?

Assyrtiko, Grüner Veltliner or a dry rosé. All are high in acidity, which both the frying and the sharp accompaniments demand.

Why does acidity matter so much with falafel?

It is doing two jobs. It cuts the oil from deep-frying, and it keeps up with the lemon and sharp vegetables served alongside. A wine less acidic than the food on the plate will taste flat and hollow.

Can I drink red wine with falafel?

Only a very light, low-tannin one. There is no fat-and-protein structure here for tannin to grip, so it tastes bitter and hard against chickpeas. A chilled Cinsault is about as far as it goes.

What if there is a lot of chilli sauce?

Lower the alcohol and chill the wine. Capsaicin heat is amplified by alcohol and sharpened by tannin, so a big red makes the burn worse. A cold, dry rosé is the practical answer.
