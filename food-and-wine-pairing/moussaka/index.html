What Wine Goes With Moussaka?

Lamb, aubergine, cinnamon and béchamel. The cinnamon is the part people forget.

Greek and medium-bodied: Agiorgitiko or Xinomavro. Enough acidity for the tomato, enough softness for the béchamel, and a savoury edge that meets the warm spice.

Moussaka is three dishes stacked. Underneath, spiced lamb in tomato — usually with cinnamon and sometimes allspice. Above it, layers of fried or roasted aubergine. On top, a thick béchamel enriched with egg and cheese. Each layer wants something different from a wine, and the mistake is to pair with the lamb alone and ignore the other two.

Three layers, three demands

The lamb wants some structure. The tomato beneath it wants acidity, for the reason it always does — tomato is both sharply acidic and full of glutamate, which sharpens tannin. And the béchamel wants the wine not to be too tannic at all, because a thick dairy sauce coats the palate and makes a firm red taste dusty rather than fresh.

Add the cinnamon and you have a fourth consideration. Warm baking spice sits badly with new oak — the two combine into something falsely sweet and slightly medicinal — but it sits beautifully beside a wine with its own savoury, dried-herb character.

What to actually open

Agiorgitiko is the easy Greek answer. From Nemea in the Peloponnese, soft-tannined with ripe red fruit and a gentle spice of its own. It is the least demanding wine on this page and the one most likely to please a table.

Xinomavro is the serious one. The name means "acid-black", which tells you most of it: very high acidity, firm tannin, and a savoury tomato-leaf and olive character that people often compare to Nebbiolo. It is the better match if your moussaka is tomato-heavy, and the riskier one if it is mostly béchamel.

Outside Greece, Nero d’Avola or a Montepulciano do the same job — southern, warm-fruited, moderate in tannin.

The one to avoid

A big oaked Cabernet is the standard wrong answer here, and it fails on three counts at once: the oak collides with the cinnamon, the tannin is stripped bare by the béchamel, and the fruit is too dark and sweet against the tomato. Nothing about it is a disaster, but nothing about it helps either.

If red is not wanted at all, an Assyrtiko with real body is the Greek white answer — its acidity cuts the béchamel cleanly, which is more than most whites manage.

Common questions

What wine goes with moussaka?

Agiorgitiko for something soft and generous, or Xinomavro if the dish is tomato-heavy and you want more structure. Both are Greek, both have the acidity the tomato demands.

Why does cinnamon matter to the wine choice?

Warm baking spice combines badly with new oak — the pair turns falsely sweet and faintly medicinal. It works well beside a savoury, dried-herb red instead, which is why Greek and southern Italian wines suit moussaka so well.

Does the béchamel change things?

Yes. A thick dairy topping coats the palate and makes firm tannin taste dusty. The more béchamel your moussaka carries, the softer and more acidic the wine should be.

What does Xinomavro mean?

Literally "acid-black" — a fair description. It has very high acidity and firm tannin with a savoury, tomato-leaf character often compared to Nebbiolo.
