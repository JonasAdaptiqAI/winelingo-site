What Wine Goes With Prime Rib?

Fatty, beefy and served with its own juices. The occasion for your best red.

Cabernet Sauvignon — Left Bank Bordeaux or Napa. If you are opening something serious, this is the dish for it.

Prime rib is the standing rib roast: heavily marbled, roasted on the bone, served pink with its own jus. It is fatty, beefy and generous in a way few dishes are, and it is one of the few plates that genuinely justifies a structured, tannic, expensive red rather than merely tolerating one.

Why the big red works here

Tannin binds to protein and fat and is carried off with them. Prime rib supplies an unusual amount of both — the marbling melts through the meat as it roasts, and the cut is dense with protein. Give tannin that much to work on and a firm young Cabernet tastes structured and clean rather than drying.

The bone matters too. Meat roasted on the bone develops more savoury depth, which meets a wine with some age and complexity rather than pure fruit.

This is the opposite case to filet mignon, where the leanness of the cut means the same wine tastes austere. Fat is the variable.

What to actually open

Left Bank Bordeaux. Cabernet-dominant, with cassis, cedar and graphite and tannin that has softened if the bottle has a decade on it. The traditional answer and still the best.

Napa Cabernet if you want ripeness and generosity rather than restraint. It suits a well-marbled American roast particularly well.

Barolo or a serious Nebbiolo as the alternative — high tannin and high acidity together, which cuts fat as well as matching structure. Needs age.

Horseradish, Yorkshire pudding and the au jus

Horseradish is the complication. It is pungent and hot in a mustard-oil way rather than a capsaicin way, and it flattens delicate wine. Use it sparingly if you have opened something you care about, or accept that the good bottle should be drunk before the horseradish arrives.

Yorkshire pudding and roast potatoes add starch, which dulls the palate — an argument for concentration over subtlety.

The jus is beef stock and dripping, dense with umami. Umami sharpens tannin, so a very young, firmly tannic wine can taste hard against it even here. Age helps; decanting helps more.

Common questions

What wine goes with prime rib?

Cabernet Sauvignon — Left Bank Bordeaux for savour or Napa for ripeness. An aged Barolo is the fine alternative.

Why does prime rib take a bigger wine than fillet?

Tannin binds to protein and fat. Prime rib is heavily marbled and dense with both, so a firm red tastes structured; filet mignon is the leanest cut and the same wine tastes austere.

Does horseradish ruin the wine?

It flattens a delicate one — it is pungent in a mustard-oil way that overwhelms subtlety. Use it sparingly if you have opened something good, or drink the good bottle before it arrives.

Should I decant?

Worth it. The jus is dense with umami, which sharpens tannin, so a young firm red can taste hard even here. Air softens it.
