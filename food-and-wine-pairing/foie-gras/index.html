What Wine Goes With Foie Gras?

The oldest sweet-with-savoury pairing in French cooking, and it still holds.

Sauternes, Tokaji, or an off-dry Alsace white. Sweetness against fat is the whole mechanism, and it is worth understanding before you break it.

Foie gras is fattened duck or goose liver, served as a terrine or seared. It is among the richest things anyone eats — mostly fat, with a mineral, faintly metallic liver note underneath. The classic pairing is a sweet wine, which sounds odd with a savoury course and is one of the best-founded matches in French cooking.

Why sweet works with fat

Two things happen. Sugar and fat balance each other on the palate in the same way sugar and acid do — the sweetness cuts through the richness and stops it becoming cloying, while the fat stops the wine tasting sugary.

And a botrytised sweet wine like Sauternes is not merely sweet. Noble rot concentrates acidity as well as sugar, and adds a distinctive honeyed, marmalade, faintly mushroomy character. It is that acidity doing half the work; a simply sweet wine without it would be sickly here.

The liver note matters too. Foie gras has a mineral, iron-like undertone that tannin sharpens unpleasantly — which is the main reason red wine is a poor idea however grand the bottle.

What to actually open

Sauternes. The traditional match, from the same corner of Bordeaux as much of the foie gras trade. Honey, apricot and marmalade with cutting acidity. A half bottle is plenty for a starter.

Tokaji Aszú. Hungary’s botrytised wine, and arguably the better choice — higher acidity than most Sauternes, with a distinctive orange-peel sharpness that suits seared foie gras particularly well.

An off-dry Alsace white — Gewürztraminer vendanges tardives, or Pinot Gris. Less sweet, more aromatic, and a lighter way into the dish. This is what much of Alsace actually drinks with it.

Terrine versus seared

A cold terrine is denser, fattier and more uniformly rich; it takes the sweetest wine comfortably and is the classic Sauternes moment.

Seared foie gras is served hot with a caramelised crust and usually something sharp or fruity — fig, apple, a gastrique. That version is less relentlessly fatty and can take a less sweet wine: an Alsace Pinot Gris, or even a Vouvray demi-sec.

The Champagne option is real and less obvious: a properly aged Champagne with some autolytic richness handles the fat through acidity and bubbles rather than sugar. It is the choice if a sweet wine at the start of a meal feels wrong to you.

Common questions

What wine goes with foie gras?

Sauternes is the classic, Tokaji is arguably better for seared, and an off-dry Alsace Gewürztraminer or Pinot Gris is the lighter route.

Why sweet wine with a savoury dish?

Sugar and fat balance each other: the sweetness cuts the richness and the fat stops the wine tasting sugary. Botrytised wines also carry high acidity, which does half the work.

Can I drink red wine with foie gras?

It is a poor idea. Foie gras has a mineral, iron-like undertone that tannin sharpens unpleasantly, and there is nothing in the dish that wants grip.

Does Champagne work?

Yes, and it is the best alternative if a sweet wine at the start of a meal feels wrong. An aged Champagne handles the fat through acidity and bubbles instead of sugar.
