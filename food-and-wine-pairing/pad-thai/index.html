What Wine Goes With Pad Thai?

Sweet, sour, salty and hot in the same mouthful. One wine handles all four.

An off-dry Riesling — a German Kabinett is the classic. It is not a compromise choice; it is the only common style that answers every part of this dish.

Pad Thai is built on a sauce of tamarind, palm sugar and fish sauce, which is to say sour, sweet and intensely savoury all at once, before you add chilli and crushed peanuts. Almost every wine fails against at least one of those. Sugar in the sauce hollows out a dry wine; the sourness demands high acidity; the fish sauce brings enough umami to sharpen tannin; and the chilli punishes alcohol.

Why Riesling and almost nothing else

Off-dry Riesling is unusual in holding four things simultaneously: residual sugar to match the palm sugar, very high acidity to meet the tamarind, low alcohol to avoid inflaming the chilli, and no tannin at all for the fish sauce to sharpen. Take away any one of those and the pairing stops working, which is why so many otherwise excellent wines fall over here.

On a German label, the ripeness ladder runs Kabinett, Spätlese, Auslese — grapes picked progressively riper. Kabinett is the lightest and usually the best fit; a Mosel Riesling at Kabinett level is typically around 8 to 9% alcohol, which is exactly what you want in front of chilli.

The chilli rule

Capsaicin, the compound behind chilli heat, is amplified by alcohol. It is also dissolved by it — which is why a spirit briefly seems to help and then makes everything worse. The practical rule is simple and holds across every spicy cuisine: the hotter the dish, the lower the alcohol and the colder the glass.

Sweetness helps too. A little residual sugar genuinely damps the burn, which is the second reason off-dry works here and bone-dry does not. Tannin does the opposite and sharpens it, which is why red wine with a hot dish so often ends badly.

If there is no Riesling

Gewürztraminer is the obvious alternative — lychee and rose, usually a touch of sugar, plenty of body. Its weakness is low acidity, so it handles the sweetness and the heat but not the tamarind sourness as well.

A dry rosé works if your Pad Thai is on the mild side. Sparkling is better than most people expect: the bubbles clear the peanut oil, though a bone-dry Champagne will taste austere against the sugar. If you go sparkling, pick something with a little dosage rather than a Brut Nature.

Red is genuinely difficult. If you must, a chilled Gamay with almost no tannin.

Common questions

What wine goes with Pad Thai?

An off-dry Riesling, ideally a German Kabinett. It has sugar to match the palm sugar, acidity for the tamarind, low alcohol for the chilli and no tannin for the fish sauce to sharpen.

Why does spicy food make wine taste harsher?

Capsaicin heat is amplified by alcohol and sharpened by tannin. A high-alcohol red therefore makes a hot dish taste hotter and itself taste more bitter. Lower alcohol, colder serving and a little sweetness all reduce the burn.

What does Kabinett mean on a German label?

The lightest rung of the ripeness ladder — Kabinett, then Spätlese, then Auslese, by how ripe the grapes were at harvest. Kabinett is typically low in alcohol, which is what makes it so good with chilli.

Can I drink red wine with Pad Thai?

It is the hardest option. If you want to, chill a low-tannin red like Gamay — tannin sharpens chilli heat, so the less of it the better.
