What Wine Goes With Leek and Potato Soup?

A gentle bowl. The danger is not clashing with it — it is being swallowed by it.

Chablis, Muscadet made from Melon de Bourgogne, or Grüner Veltliner. Unoaked, high in acid, and lean enough to cut a creamy soup rather than sink into it.

Leek and potato soup has no sharp edges. It is mild, starchy, faintly sweet from the softened leeks, and usually enriched with cream or butter. Nothing in it will attack a wine. The risk runs the other way: put a soft, round, low-acid white beside it and the two merge into a single blur of creaminess with nothing to hold your attention.

Acidity is doing the work

What this dish needs from a wine is contrast, and specifically acidity — the thing that cuts through fat and resets the palate between spoonfuls. Without it, each mouthful lands on top of the last and the whole meal flattens out.

It also needs restraint. There is no strong flavour here to stand up to, so an aromatic or heavily oaked wine simply talks over the food. The leeks are the most delicate thing in the bowl and the first thing to be lost.

What to actually open

Chablis. Chardonnay grown at the cool northern edge of Burgundy and, at the classic end, made without new oak. It gives you the texture to sit alongside cream and the acidity to cut it, which is a rare combination. Village Chablis is plenty; the Premier Cru bottles are better spent elsewhere.

Muscadet. Made from Melon de Bourgogne at the Atlantic end of the Loire, and often labelled sur lie, meaning it rested on its spent yeast before bottling. That contact adds a faintly bready weight to what is otherwise a very lean wine — texture without richness, which is exactly the trick this soup wants.

Grüner Veltliner. The white-pepper note gives the bowl something it does not have on its own, and Grüner is unusually good with alliums, which most whites are not.

Vichyssoise is a different bowl

Served cold as vichyssoise, the soup changes character: cold mutes flavour, so it tastes less sweet and more saline, and the cream reads as heavier on the tongue. Push further towards acidity and towards bubbles — a Crémant or a dry Champagne is genuinely better here than a still white, because carbonation scrubs the palate in a way still wine cannot.

If there is smoked bacon or smoked haddock involved, that flavour takes charge, and you want a wine with a little more body: a fuller Chenin Blanc or a white Rhône.

Common questions

What wine goes with leek and potato soup?

Chablis, Muscadet made from Melon de Bourgogne, or Grüner Veltliner — all unoaked, all high in acidity, none loud enough to drown the leeks.

Why not an oaked Chardonnay?

Because there is nothing in the bowl to stand up to it. Oak and cream together produce one very rich flavour with no contrast, and the delicate leek disappears underneath. Unoaked Chardonnay gives you the texture without the noise.

What does sur lie mean on a Muscadet label?

That the wine rested on its spent yeast before bottling. It adds a subtle bready weight to a naturally lean wine — texture without richness, which suits a creamy soup well.

What about cold vichyssoise?

Go for bubbles. Cold mutes flavour and makes the cream feel heavier, so a Crémant or dry Champagne works better than a still white — the carbonation clears the palate between spoonfuls.
