Sweet, low-alcohol fizz from aromatic Moscato grapes in Piedmont — Italy’s joyful party in a glass.

What makes Asti & Moscato d’Asti distinctive

A single fermentation is chilled to a halt before it finishes, locking in natural grape sweetness and keeping alcohol unusually low (~5-7%). The result tastes of fresh grapes and flowers rather than yeast or oak.

Why it matters

Asti and Moscato d’Asti are the same idea at two pressures: sweet, intensely perfumed, low-alcohol sparkling wines from the Moscato grape in Italy’s northwest. They come from the rolling hills around the town of Asti in Piedmont — the same famous region that gives us the mighty dry reds Barolo and Barbaresco.

They matter because they’re one of wine’s friendliest pleasures — and one of its most misunderstood. This is unashamedly sweet, fruity, fizzy wine that tastes like a handful of fresh grapes, made deliberately low in alcohol (often just 5-7%) so a glass at brunch or with dessert never weighs you down.

For a beginner, it’s the perfect proof that sweet and low-alcohol can also be serious, traditional and beautifully made — not a guilty pleasure but a genuine Italian classic.

What’s in the glass

Pour a glass and the first thing you’ll notice is the smell — an explosion of: • ripe peach and fresh table grapes • orange blossom and honeysuckle • elderflower and a touch of honey

Moscato is one of the few grapes whose wine genuinely smells of grapes, and it leaps out of the glass.

Sweetness: clearly sweet, but never heavy or syrupy. A streak of fresh acidity keeps it lively and stops it cloying.

The fizz is the key difference between the two: • Asti — fully sparkling (spumante), with a generous, foaming mousse like Prosecco or Champagne, around 7% alcohol. • Moscato d’Asti — only gently fizzy (frizzante), a soft, delicate prickle rather than a rush of bubbles, and even lighter at around 5% alcohol.

Body is light, tannin is nil (as in any white sparkling), and the finish is clean, floral and refreshing.

Where it comes from

Both wines come from Piedmont in northwest Italy, centred on the hills between the towns of Asti, Alba and Acqui Terme. The growing area straddles three provinces and includes some of Italy’s prettiest, steepest vineyard country.

Quality tier to know: both wines hold DOCG status — Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita, Italy’s highest official quality rank, sitting above the more common DOC. Seeing DOCG on the label tells you the wine met strict rules on where and how it was grown and made.

Label terms: • Asti (or Asti Spumante) = the fully sparkling version. • Moscato d’Asti = the gently frizzante, lower-alcohol version, often the more refined of the two. • Canelli is a prized historic sub-zone — so prized it became its own separate DOCG in 2023 — increasingly seen on top bottles.

What shapes it

The grape: both wines are made from Moscato Bianco, also called Muscat Blanc à Petits Grains (“white Muscat with small berries”) — one of the world’s oldest and most aromatic grape varieties. Its naturally floral, grapey perfume is the whole point of the style.

The defining technique — the tank (Martinotti/Charmat) method, invented in Asti in 1898: instead of a second fermentation, these wines get just one, in a large sealed pressure tank. As the yeast turns grape sugar into alcohol and carbon-dioxide bubbles, the winemaker chills the tank to stop fermentation early, before all the sugar is used up. That single move does three things at once: it keeps the wine sweet (leftover natural grape sugar), keeps the alcohol low (the yeast never finished), and traps the fizz. No oak, no ageing on the yeast — the goal is to bottle that fresh-grape aroma as fast as possible.

Climate & soil: Piedmont’s cool, hilly continental climate and calcareous (chalky) marl soils preserve the grape’s delicate perfume and its crucial backbone of acidity.

Good to know

Food: a natural with dessert — fruit tarts, peach cobbler, light sponge cakes and especially Italian panettone at Christmas. It’s also lovely at brunch, with fresh fruit and soft cheeses, and a clever foil for mildly spicy food.

Serving: serve it very cold (6-8°C) to keep it crisp and refreshing; a small flute or tulip glass shows off the aromatics.

Ageing: don’t cellar it — these wines are all about youthful, fresh fruit. Buy the most recent vintage and drink it within a year or two.

Myth-busters: • Cheap and unserious? The cheapest stuff can be, but well-made Moscato d’Asti is a craftsman’s wine — that gentle, exact fizz is harder to judge than full sparkle. • The same as Prosecco? No — Prosecco is dry and made from the Glera grape; this is sweet, far lower in alcohol, and made from Moscato. • Just for dessert? Its low alcohol makes it a brilliant aperitif and an easy daytime sipper, too.
