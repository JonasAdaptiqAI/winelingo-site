France’s dry, full-bodied take on Riesling — steely, aromatic and minerally, from the sheltered Vosges foothills of Alsace.

What makes Alsace Riesling distinctive

Alsace ferments Riesling bone-dry and fuller-bodied than Germany’s off-dry style, so all that piercing acidity and stony minerality stands exposed, with no sweetness to soften it.

Why it matters

Riesling is widely rated one of the greatest white grapes on earth, and Alsace is the place to meet its dry, grown-up side.

Most of the world knows Riesling as Germany’s lightly sweet, featherweight style. Alsace flips that script: here the wine is usually fermented bone-dry (all the grape sugar turned to alcohol) and comes out fuller and more powerful, with higher alcohol and real presence in the glass.

Why care? Because it’s the clearest window into what Riesling tastes like with nothing to hide behind. No sweetness, no oak — just bright fruit, racing acidity and a stony, mineral core. If you want to understand why wine geeks adore this grape, a good Alsace bottle is the lesson.

What’s in the glass

Expect a wine that is dry, high in acidity and surprisingly full-bodied for a white — crisp and refreshing, yet broad and mouth-filling.

On the nose: lime, green apple and white peach, often with orchard blossom and a wet-stone or flinty edge.

On the palate: zesty citrus and stone fruit carried by a long, mineral, almost salty finish. The acidity is the engine — it makes the wine taste vivid and clean rather than heavy.

• ”Petrol” note: as Alsace Riesling ages it can develop a whiff people call petrol or kerosene (from a natural compound called TDN). It sounds alarming but in moderation it’s a prized hallmark of the grape, not a fault.

There’s effectively no tannin (that drying grip comes from red-grape skins), so the texture stays smooth and juicy.

Where it comes from

Alsace sits in north-east France, hugging the German border along the Rhine. The Vosges mountains to the west block the rain clouds, leaving the vineyards unusually dry and sunny — perfect for ripening Riesling fully while the cool nights lock in acidity.

The best wines come not from the flat plain but from the lower eastern slopes and foothills of the Vosges, where drainage and sun exposure are ideal. Unusually for France, Alsace also labels its wines by grape variety, so the bottle simply says “Riesling.” A few terms worth knowing:

• Grand Cru: 51 named top vineyard sites; these tend to be the most intense, age-worthy and mineral wines.

• Vendanges Tardives (VT): “late harvest” — riper, often off-dry to sweet, not the classic dry style.

• Sélection de Grains Nobles (SGN): lusciously sweet dessert wine from nobly-rotted grapes (rare and pricey).

For the steely dry style described here, look for a straight “Riesling” or a dry Grand Cru.

What shapes it

The grape: Riesling, naturally high in acidity and a brilliant mirror of where it’s grown.

Climate: the rain-shadow of the Vosges gives a dry, sunny, semi-continental climate — warm days to ripen, cold nights to preserve that zippy acidity.

Soils: Alsace is a geological patchwork — granite, limestone, schist, sandstone and volcanic rock — and Riesling telegraphs each one, which is why Grand Cru wines taste so distinct from one another.

The defining technique: simplicity. The wine is usually fermented dry in neutral tanks or old, inert barrels (vessels that add no oak flavour), then rested on its lees — the spent yeast left after fermentation — to build a little extra body and texture. No new oak, no malolactic softening; the goal is to let fruit, acid and minerality speak for themselves.

Good to know

Food: the high acidity makes it a brilliant table wine. Classic Alsace pairings are choucroute (sauerkraut with sausages and pork), roast pork, smoked fish and freshwater trout. It’s also a savvy match for spicy Thai or Indian food, where its crispness cools the heat.

Serving: chill to 8-10°C and use a regular white-wine glass; the tall, slender Alsace flute bottle is a giveaway on the shelf.

Ageing: good dry Rieslings and Grand Crus age beautifully for 10+ years, gaining that honeyed, petrol-tinged complexity.

Value: village-level bottles offer a lot of wine for the money.

Myth-buster: Riesling is not a sweet wine by default. Alsace, most Austrian and many modern German bottles are dry — check the label, and don’t let the grape’s reputation fool you.
