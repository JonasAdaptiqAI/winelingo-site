A skin-contact white from NE Italy, fermented on its grape skins until it turns amber, grippy and savoury.

What makes Friulian Orange distinctive

It’s a white wine made like a red — left to soak on its skins for weeks or months, which pulls out an amber colour, tannic grip and a nutty, dried-fruit savour you’ll never find in a normal crisp white.

Why it matters

Orange wine isn’t made from oranges — it’s a white wine made like a red. Instead of pressing the juice off the grape skins straight away, the winemaker leaves the juice soaking on those skins for weeks or even months. That single choice changes everything: the colour deepens to amber, and the wine gains grip, texture and a savoury, nutty character.

Friuli-Venezia Giulia, tucked into Italy’s far north-east on the Slovenian border, is the spiritual home of the modern style. While the technique is ancient (it’s roughly how wine was made thousands of years ago), it was nearly forgotten until a handful of Friulian growers revived it in the 1990s.

For a learner, it’s the wine that proves “white” and “red” aren’t the only two doors. Tasting one rewires what you expect a white grape to do — and it’s a brilliant bridge wine for red drinkers who think they don’t like white.

What’s in the glass

Pour it and the first surprise is the colour: deep gold to burnished amber, like cold tea or apricot nectar — never clear and pale.

On the nose: dried apricot, orange peel, bruised apple, black tea, dried flowers and a waxy, nutty note like walnut skin or honey.

On the palate: this is the big reveal. There’s a gentle tannin — the same drying, grippy sensation you get from a red wine or strong tea — which is almost unheard of in a white. The fruit is dried and savoury rather than fresh and zingy, the body is fuller and a touch oily in texture, and the finish is long and slightly bitter in a moreish, grown-up way.

• Dry, not sweet — any honeyed note is aroma, not sugar. • Bright acidity keeps it lively despite the weight. • Best served cool but not fridge-cold, so the texture and aromas open up.

Where it comes from

The heartland is Friuli-Venezia Giulia, and within it two zones do most of the heavy lifting:

• Collio — the rolling hills hugging the Slovenian frontier, prized for white grapes. • Oslavia — the tiny hamlet on the edge of Gorizia, inside the Collio, where the modern revival was born.

The style spills across the border into Slovenia’s Brda and Vipava (the same hills, different country), and you’ll now find orange wines made worldwide — but Friuli is the reference point.

Labels to know: there’s no special appellation for orange wine, so it hides under ordinary regional names like Collio DOC or the catch-all Venezia Giulia IGT. Look for clues on the back label: “skin contact,” “macerato,” “sulle bucce” (on the skins), or “anfora.” Many of the benchmark bottles sit in the natural-wine corner of a shop.

What shapes it

The grapes: mainly Ribolla Gialla (Friuli’s signature white, thick-skinned and made for maceration) and Friulano (the regional everyday white), often joined by Pinot Grigio or Malvasia. They’re white grapes — the orange colour comes entirely from the winemaking, not the fruit.

The defining move — extended skin maceration: in a normal white, juice and skins part ways within hours. Here, the juice ferments and then sits on the skins for weeks to months. Skins are where colour, tannin and aroma compounds live, so the longer the soak, the deeper and grippier the wine.

Qvevri / amphora: the pioneers often use large clay vessels (Georgian qvevri or Italian anfora), sometimes buried underground, that breathe gently and let the wine evolve slowly with little intervention.

The pioneers: Josko Gravner and Stanko Radikon, working in Oslavia from the 1990s, championed long skin contact and clay amphorae and effectively launched the modern orange-wine movement.

Good to know

Food is its superpower. That tannin and savoury depth means it eats like a light red — it stands up to dishes that flatten ordinary whites. Try it with aged cheese, cured meats, mushroom risotto, curry, roast poultry or rich pork.

Serving: treat it like a light red, not a crisp white. 12-14°C (cool cellar, not fridge-cold) and a normal wine glass let the aromas and texture show. Many benefit from 20-30 minutes of air.

Ageing & value: the tannin and structure mean good examples can age for years, softening and turning more savoury. Prices range from approachable to collector-level for the original Gravner and Radikon bottles.

Myth-busters: • It is not sweet — “orange” describes the colour, not the flavour. • It is not flawed — the cloudiness and grip are intentional, not a fault. • It is not the same as a rosé — rosé is a red grape briefly tinted pink; orange is a white grape deeply steeped on its skins.
