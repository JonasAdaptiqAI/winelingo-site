Italy’s top traditional-method sparkler — bottle-fermented Chardonnay and Pinot Nero from Lombardy.

What makes Franciacorta distinctive

Made by the same in-bottle second fermentation as Champagne, with long lees-ageing for a bready depth — but a slightly warmer Lombardy climate gives rounder, riper fruit and a touch softer acidity.

Why it matters

Franciacorta (fran-cha-COR-ta) is Italy’s most serious sparkling wine — its proud answer to Champagne. It’s made by the same painstaking method, with a second fermentation inside each bottle and long ageing, so it sits a clear step above easygoing Prosecco in both ambition and price.

If you love the bready, fine-bubbled character of Champagne but want something a little riper, rounder and often friendlier on the wallet, this is the bottle to reach for.

It’s also a point of national pride: Franciacorta was the first Italian still-and-sparkling region to win DOCG status (Italy’s top quality tier) specifically for its bottle-fermented sparkling wine, back in 1995. The name refers to a particular patch of hills in Lombardy — not a method you’ll see copied on labels elsewhere.

What’s in the glass

Expect a dry, light-bodied sparkler with fine, persistent bubbles and bright but not searing acidity.

Nose & palate: green apple, lemon and white peach up front, with a signature toasty layer of brioche, fresh bread and almond that comes from long ageing (more on that below).

Sweetness: almost always bone-dry. Most bottles are labelled Brut (dry) or Extra Brut / Pas Dosé (even drier, little to no added sugar), so don’t expect sweetness unless the label says Demi-Sec.

Body & feel: lighter and crisper than a white Burgundy, but creamier and more textured than Prosecco — the bubbles feel soft rather than frothy. No tannin to speak of, as with nearly all sparkling and white wines.

Where it comes from

Franciacorta comes from a small zone of rolling hills in Lombardy, northern Italy, just south of Lake Iseo near the city of Brescia. The lake moderates the climate, keeping the grapes fresh.

Labels and tiers worth knowing:

• Franciacorta (standard) — the everyday style, aged at least 18 months on the lees.

• Satèn — a Franciacorta speciality: a softer, creamier all-white style (only Chardonnay and Pinot Bianco) bottled at lower pressure for gentler bubbles. A lovely, silky introduction.

• Rosé — pink, made with a good share of Pinot Nero.

• Millesimato (vintage) and Riserva — single-year and top bottlings with much longer ageing (30 and 60 months on the lees respectively), the most complex of the lot.

What shapes it

The grapes: mainly Chardonnay and Pinot Nero (the Italian name for Pinot Noir — a black grape pressed gently for white juice), with Pinot Bianco allowed too, plus a small amount of the local Erbamat in some bottles.

Traditional method: this is the heart of it. After the base wine is made, a small dose of yeast and sugar is added and the bottle is sealed, so a second fermentation happens inside that bottle — trapping carbon dioxide as natural bubbles. The same method makes Champagne.

Lees ageing: “lees” are the spent yeast cells left behind after that second fermentation. The wine rests on them for months or years, which slowly builds the toasty, bready, biscuity flavours. Franciacorta’s minimum ageing meets or beats Champagne’s, and Riserva bottles age for years.

Climate: Lombardy is a touch warmer and sunnier than Champagne, so the fruit ripens a little riper — giving rounder flavour and slightly softer acidity.

Good to know

Food: brilliant as an Italian-style aperitivo, and a natural with the local table — creamy risotto, fried seafood, prosciutto and fresh cheeses. The crisp acidity also cuts through anything fried or rich.

Serving: chill well (8-10°C) and use a tulip-shaped glass rather than a narrow flute, so you can actually smell those brioche aromas.

Ageing: standard bottles are best within a few years of release; Riserva and vintage wines can develop for longer.

Don’t confuse it with Prosecco. Prosecco is made the cheap, fast way in a big tank and stays fruity and frothy; Franciacorta is bottle-fermented and lees-aged, so it’s deeper, drier and a different league of wine. The name on the label is your shortcut to telling them apart.
