Five things to know about Champagne

  1. Two letters tell you who actually made it. Every label carries a code beside its registration number: NM means the house bought its grapes, RM means the grower pressed their own, CM means a co-operative. Most famous names are NM.
  2. You cannot legally buy young Champagne. Every bottle must sit in the cellar for at least 15 months after bottling, 12 of them on the spent yeast, and a vintage must wait three full years. Most houses go well past that.
  3. The famous houses own almost none of the vines. Growers hold roughly 90 per cent of Champagne’s 34,200 hectares, and the average holding is about two hectares. The names on the bottles are mostly buyers and blenders, not farmers.
  4. Champagne is the one wine allowed to mix red into white. EU rules ban blending to make still rosé, which is why Provence relies on skin contact. Rosé Champagne can simply take a measure of red Pinot Noir, and most of it does.
  5. Half a per cent of the world’s vines, nearly a third of the money. Champagne is 0.5 per cent of the global area under vine. It supplies about 8 per cent of the sparkling wine drunk worldwide and takes 31 per cent of what is spent on it.

What makes Champagne distinctive

Years on the lees give that bready, biscuity complexity; the cold climate keeps the acidity racy and the bubbles fine.

Why it matters

Champagne is the benchmark. When people picture celebratory bubbles, they’re picturing Champagne — the sparkling wine from one specific cool, chalky corner of northern France. Nearly every other quality sparkler in the world (Cava, Franciacorta, many English fizzes) is essentially trying to match what Champagne does.

It earned that status the hard way. Champagne perfected the painstaking method of making bubbles by re-fermenting wine inside the sealed bottle — and the long ageing that gives it depth. So the name isn’t just a place; it’s become a quality standard.

Why care as a learner? Understanding Champagne unlocks the whole sparkling category. Once you know why it tastes the way it does, you can spot the same techniques (and the shortcuts) in cheaper bottles — and know when a splurge is actually worth it.

What’s in the glass

Bone-dry, bright, and bracing. Most Champagne is dry, with high, mouth-watering acidity that comes from those cold-climate grapes. The body is light-to-medium, and the bubbles (the ‘mousse’) are fine and persistent rather than aggressive.

On the nose and palate you’ll find green apple and citrus (lemon, grapefruit), alongside the signature brioche and almond — that toasty, bready, biscuity character that develops with ageing.

Sweetness is on the label. From driest to sweetest: • Brut Nature (also ‘Zero Dosage’) — bone-dry, virtually no added sugar (under 3 g/L) • Extra Brut — very dry (0–6 g/L) • Brut — dry (under 12 g/L), and by far the most common style you’ll see • Extra Dry — confusingly, a touch sweeter than Brut (12–17 g/L) • Sec / Demi-Sec / Doux — increasingly sweet, best saved for dessert

Where it comes from

One region, strictly defined. Champagne sits about 130 km east of Paris — one of France’s coolest, most northerly wine zones. By law, only wine made here, in this way, can carry the name Champagne.

Key sub-zones to know: • Montagne de Reims — mostly Pinot Noir, lending body and structure • Côte des Blancs — almost all Chardonnay, prized for finesse and ageing • Vallée de la Marne — Pinot Meunier’s heartland, adding fruit and easy charm • Côte des Bar — the warmer south, a major source of Pinot Noir for blending

Label terms worth learning: • Blanc de Blancs — made only from white grapes (Chardonnay): lean and elegant • Blanc de Noirs — made only from dark grapes (the two Pinots): richer and rounder • NV (Non-Vintage) — a house blend across several years; the everyday style • Vintage — a single standout year, aged longer, more serious (and pricier)

What shapes it

Three grapes do the heavy lifting. Chardonnay brings citrus and elegance; Pinot Noir adds body and backbone; Pinot Meunier offers soft, fruity charm. (Four other grapes — Arbane, Petit Meslier, Pinot Blanc and Pinot Gris — are still legally allowed but make up well under 1% of plantings, so you’ll rarely meet them.) The cool northern climate keeps sugar low and acidity racy — exactly what you want for crisp, age-worthy fizz.

Chalk soils drain well and store water for dry spells, helping grapes ripen in a marginal climate while keeping the wine mineral and precise.

The traditional method is the defining technique. In plain words: a finished still ‘base wine’ is bottled with a little yeast and sugar, then sealed. A second fermentation happens inside that bottle, and the carbon dioxide it produces is trapped as natural bubbles.

Lees ageing is the magic. The ‘lees’ are the spent yeast cells left behind after that second fermentation; resting on them for months or years gives Champagne its bready, biscuity, almond complexity and its fine, lasting bubbles.

Good to know

Serving: Chill well (8–10°C) and reach for a tulip glass rather than a narrow flute — the wider bowl lets those brioche and citrus aromas open up. Serving it too cold, or in a tight flute, mutes everything.

Food: Brilliant with salty, fried, or fatty foods — the acidity and bubbles cut right through. Think oysters, fried chicken, popcorn, soft cheeses, or simply on its own as an apéritif.

Ageing: Non-Vintage is made to drink now; good Vintage Champagne can keep improving for a decade or more, growing nuttier and richer.

Myth-buster: Not all sparkling wine is Champagne. Prosecco (Italy) is made from the Glera grape in big pressurised tanks, not individual bottles, so it’s fruitier and frothier. Cava (Spain) uses the very same bottle method as Champagne but with mostly Spanish grapes (Macabeo, Xarel·lo, Parellada). Champagne’s price reflects the hand-work and years of ageing behind every bottle.

Producers to know for Champagne

1

Louis Roederer

Founded at Reims in 1776 and still family-owned; matures reserve wines in oak foudres and makes Cristal, created in 1876.

2

Bollinger

At Aÿ since 1829 and one of the last houses still fermenting its base wines in old oak barrels; La Grande Année is the vintage.

3

Pierre Péters

A grower at Le Mesnil-sur-Oger bottling Grand Cru Blanc de Blancs since 1919; the single-vineyard Les Chétillons is its benchmark.

4

Nicolas Feuillatte

A Chouilly co-operative founded in 1976 pooling around 5,000 growers; France’s best-selling Champagne and the easiest way into the region.
