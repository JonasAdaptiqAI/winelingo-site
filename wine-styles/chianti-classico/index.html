Tuscany’s classic Sangiovese red — savoury, high-acid and made for the table.

What makes Chianti Classico distinctive

That mouth-watering acidity and tomato-leaf, sour-cherry savour is why it’s the ultimate red-sauce-pasta wine.

Why it matters

Chianti Classico is the beating heart of Tuscan red wine — and one of the best places for a beginner to understand why Italian reds work so brilliantly at the table.

The benchmark Sangiovese. Sangiovese is Italy’s most-planted red grape, and Chianti Classico is its most famous home. If you want to know what Sangiovese tastes like, this is the reference point.

Not the same as plain ‘Chianti’. This is a crucial early lesson. Chianti Classico is its own, stricter appellation covering the historic heartland between Florence and Siena, marked by a black-rooster (Gallo Nero) seal on the neck. Plain Chianti is a much larger surrounding zone, usually simpler and cheaper. Same grape, different rulebook — Classico is the senior, more serious version.

A food wine, by design. Its whole character is built around the meal rather than the spotlight, which is exactly what makes it so useful to know.

What’s in the glass

Pour a glass and you’ll see a medium ruby red, often lightening to a faint orange or brick rim with age. It’s a medium-bodied wine — never heavy or jammy.

The signature is acidity. Expect a bright, mouth-watering tartness that leaves you reaching for the next bite. That freshness is the wine’s calling card.

Tannins — the drying, grippy sensation, like the feeling of strong black tea — are firm and a touch grainy, but kept in check by the fruit.

Flavours lean savoury rather than sweet. Look for: • Sour cherry and red plum — tart red fruit, not ripe jam • Dried herbs and tomato leaf — a green, savoury, sun-warmed-garden note • Earth and leather — an earthy, dried-flower edge that grows with age

The overall impression is dry, lively and gently rustic — refreshing rather than rich.

Where it comes from

Chianti Classico sits in the rolling hills of central Tuscany, between Florence and Siena — the original Chianti zone, mapped out long before the wider region borrowed the name.

Quality tiers to know (they appear right on the label): • Annata — the standard, everyday bottling; aged at least 12 months. Bright and approachable. • Riserva — aged longer before release: a minimum of 24 months, including at least 3 months in bottle. Usually richer and more structured. • Gran Selezione — the top tier, introduced in 2014. Aged at least 30 months and made entirely from a single estate’s own grapes.

Sub-zones (UGA). Since 2023, a wine can name a smaller village or area such as Radda, Gaiole, Castellina or Panzano on the label — a hint that, as in Burgundy, exact location shapes the style. For now this naming is allowed only on Gran Selezione bottles.

The black-rooster seal confirms the wine comes from this heartland.

What shapes it

The grape. Chianti Classico must be at least 80% Sangiovese, and many top wines are 100%. Sangiovese is naturally high in acid and tannin but light in colour — which explains the wine’s translucent ruby hue and zippy freshness. The remaining share can be local grapes such as Canaiolo and Colorino, or international ones like Merlot and Cabernet; white grapes are not permitted in Classico. (From the 2027 vintage, Gran Selezione tightens further: at least 90% Sangiovese, with only native varieties allowed in the rest.)

Climate and soils. These are sunny but breezy hills with cool nights; that day-to-night temperature swing preserves the bright acidity. Classic soils include galestro (a crumbly, flaky marl) and alberese (hard limestone), which give the wine its taut, savoury backbone.

Winemaking. Mostly straightforward dry red fermentation, then ageing — traditionally in botti, large oak casks that let the wine soften and breathe without stamping on it with strong oaky flavour, keeping that pure, food-friendly character.

Good to know

Food is the whole point. That high acidity cuts through fat and matches the acidity in food, making it a dream with tomato-based and grilled dishes — the ultimate red-sauce-pasta wine. Think spaghetti al pomodoro, lasagne, pizza, roast meats and aged pecorino.

Serving. Pour at 16–18°C — a touch below room temperature so the freshness sings. Most bottles need no decanting; a Riserva or Gran Selezione can benefit from 30 minutes of air.

Ageing. Annata is best in its first few years; Riserva and especially Gran Selezione can develop beautifully for a decade or more, gaining leather, dried-cherry and savoury depth.

Myth-busters. • It is *not* the straw-flask cheap Chianti of decades past — that era is gone. • ‘Classico’ means heartland origin, *not* a cheaper basic version — it’s the more prestigious zone. • Don’t expect a big, sweet, oaky red; its charm is freshness and savour.
