Hungary’s legendary golden sweet wine from Tokaj, made mainly from nobly-rotted (Aszú) Furmint berries and graded for sweetness by puttonyos.

What makes Tokaji Aszú distinctive

Shrivelled, noble-rotted Furmint berries are crushed to a paste and steeped in a base wine, so razor-sharp acidity balances the honeyed sweetness — luscious yet never cloying. That acid backbone also lets the best bottles age for decades.

Why it matters

Tokaji Aszú is one of the world’s great sweet wines — and arguably the original of the genre. Made in the Tokaj hills of northeast Hungary, it was prized at royal and imperial courts centuries before Sauternes existed. A 1737 royal decree delimited and regulated its vineyards, making Tokaj one of the first demarcated wine regions on Earth (a 1772 ranking then graded the best vineyards). A famous old toast — often attributed to a French king — called it ”the wine of kings, the king of wines.”

For a learner, it’s the perfect introduction to noble rot (more on that below) and to the idea that sweetness and acidity can coexist in glorious tension. Where many dessert wines feel heavy or one-note, Tokaji stays vivid and lifted — a benchmark for how thrilling a sweet wine can be.

What’s in the glass

Expect a deep gold-to-amber wine that is richly sweet but bracingly fresh — the signature is high sugar held up by mouth-watering acidity.

• Aromas: dried apricot, orange marmalade, honey, quince and dried fig, often with a savoury saffron or marzipan note.

• Palate: unctuous and silky, coating the mouth, then snapping clean on a long, citrus-edged finish. Because it’s a white wine, there is no tannin to speak of.

• Strength: moderate alcohol, usually around 9-12%, because the yeast can’t ferment all that sugar away.

Think honey and apricot jam given a squeeze of lemon — sweet, yes, but it leaves you refreshed rather than sated.

Where it comes from

It all comes from Tokaj (historically “Tokaj-Hegyalja”), in northeast Hungary near where the Bodrog and Tisza rivers meet. Autumn mists rising off the rivers, followed by warm sunny afternoons, create ideal conditions for noble rot to form on the grapes.

Reading the label — puttonyos: sweetness is graded in puttonyos, named after the old hods (puttony) of shrivelled berries once tipped into each barrel. From the 2013 vintage the rules tightened: any wine labelled Aszú must now carry at least 120 g/l residual sugar, so the lower 3 and 4 puttonyos grades were dropped. Today you’ll mainly see:

• 5 puttonyos — from 120 g/l sugar.

• 6 puttonyos — from 150 g/l, the richest.

Above Aszú sits rare Eszencia, the free-run nectar (450 g/l sugar and often far higher — 800 g/l and beyond) that takes years just to ferment to a few percent alcohol.

What shapes it — noble rot & the aszú method

The defining grape is Furmint, a high-acid white whose thin skins welcome botrytis; Hárslevelű (“linden leaf”) adds honeyed perfume and Sárga Muskotály (Yellow Muscat) lends floral lift.

Noble rot (botrytis): a friendly fungus, Botrytis cinerea, that pierces ripe grape skins so water evaporates. The berry shrivels to a sticky raisin, concentrating sugar, acid and flavour — and adding a distinctive saffron-honey character. These shrivelled grapes are the aszú berries.

The aszú method: pickers select aszú berries one by one, then crush them into a paste. This paste is steeped (macerated) in a barrel of fermenting must or dry base wine for roughly a day or two, leaching out sweetness and flavour. The wine is then pressed off and slowly fermented. By law it ages a long time — released no earlier than the third year after harvest, with at least 18 months in oak — building those mellow, nutty layers.

Good to know

Pairing: a classic with blue cheese (the salt-sweet contrast is magic), foie gras, and fruit or nut-based desserts like apricot tart or crème brûlée. It’s also lovely as dessert, on its own.

• Serving: chill well, 8-10°C, and pour small measures — it’s intense. A standard white-wine glass is fine.

• Ageing: that acid backbone lets good bottles last decades; once opened, the high sugar keeps a bottle fresh in the fridge for a couple of weeks.

Myth-busters: don’t confuse Tokaji (Hungary, pronounced “toe-kye”) with Italy’s old dry Tocai — different wines entirely, and Italy was required to drop the Tocai name (now Friulano) after an EU ruling. And not every Tokaji is sweet: the region also makes bone-dry Furmint and the often-drier Szamorodni style. “Aszú” specifically means the sweet, noble-rot wine.
