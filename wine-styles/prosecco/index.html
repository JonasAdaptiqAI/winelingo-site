Italy’s easygoing fizz from Glera, made sparkling in a tank rather than the bottle.

What makes Prosecco distinctive

The tank method keeps it fresh, fruity and frothy rather than bready — light, gently off-dry and made for fun.

Why it matters

Prosecco is the world’s most-loved everyday sparkling wine, and for many people it’s the very first bubbly they ever pour. It’s Italy’s answer to a fun, affordable fizz: light, fruity and gently frothy, made to be opened on a Tuesday rather than saved for a big occasion.

Why a beginner should care: it’s the easiest doorway into sparkling wine. There’s no code to crack, no need to swirl and ponder — it just delivers crisp, fresh refreshment.

The big idea to remember: Prosecco is made sparkling in a big sealed tank, not in each individual bottle. That single choice is why it tastes the way it does — and it’s what sets it apart from Champagne, which it’s constantly (and unfairly) compared to. They’re cousins, not twins.

What’s in the glass

Expect a light, easy wine with lively but soft bubbles — frothier and gentler than the fine, persistent streams of Champagne.

• Body: light and fairly low in alcohol (usually around 11%), so it feels breezy, not heavy. • Sweetness: most Prosecco sits between dry and just off-dry. The most common label is Extra Dry, which — confusingly — is actually a touch sweeter than Brut. (More on those terms below.) • Acidity: fresh and juicy, keeping it clean and thirst-quenching.

On the nose and palate: green apple and pear lead, with white peach and a pretty honeysuckle floral lift. It’s all about bright orchard fruit and flowers — fresh and frothy rather than the toasty, bready notes you find in bottle-aged sparklers.

Where it comes from

Prosecco’s home is northeast Italy, spread across nine provinces of the Veneto and neighbouring Friuli Venezia Giulia regions. “Prosecco” was once the name of the grape itself, but since 2009 it’s been a protected place name — only this corner of Italy may use it (the grape was renamed Glera).

Quality tiers to know (from everyday up): • Prosecco DOC — the large, everyday tier covering the whole zone; most bottles you’ll see. • Prosecco DOCG — the smaller, higher-quality heartland in the hills. There are two DOCG zones: Conegliano Valdobbiadene and Asolo. DOCG on the label signals stricter rules and, usually, finer wine. • Rive & Cartizze — the peak. Within Conegliano Valdobbiadene, Rive wines come from a single steep hillside commune, and Cartizze is a tiny, prized hilltop cru near Valdobbiadene — often called Prosecco’s “grand cru.”

Label terms: DOC and DOCG are Italy’s official quality stamps (DOCG is the stricter of the two). Spumante means fully sparkling, Frizzante means lightly fizzy, and Tranquillo means still (rare).

What shapes it

The grape: Prosecco is made overwhelmingly from Glera (at least 85% by law, with a few local grapes allowed to fill the rest). Glera is a semi-aromatic white that gives those signature apple, pear and honeysuckle notes. Its old name was ‘Prosecco’ too — which is exactly why the 2009 rename happened, so the place could own the name.

Climate and soils: in the DOCG hills, cooler nights and steep, varied slopes lock in freshness and perfume.

The defining technique — the tank method (Charmat / Martinotti): instead of trapping bubbles inside each individual bottle, the second fermentation that creates the fizz happens in a big sealed steel tank, then the wine is bottled under pressure. Because the wine spends little time resting on its spent yeast (the ‘lees’), it stays fresh and fruity rather than developing the bready, biscuity character of Champagne. Fast, clean, and built to preserve Glera’s bright fruit — that’s the whole point. (It’s named for Federico Martinotti, who devised it, and Eugène Charmat, who patented the tank.)

Good to know

Serve it cold: 6–8°C, straight from the fridge. A regular white-wine glass (not a narrow flute) actually lets the aromas shine.

Drink it young: Prosecco is built for freshness, not the cellar. Buy a recent vintage and enjoy it within a year or two — ageing won’t improve it.

Decode the sweetness: counter-intuitively, the dryness ladder runs Brut (driest, up to ~12 g/L sugar) → Extra Dry (a little sweeter, ~12–17 g/L) → Dry (sweeter still). So ‘Extra Dry’ is the gentle middle, not the bone-dry end.

Food: brilliant as an aperitivo, and a natural with light bites — prosciutto, fried snacks, sushi, or a slice of cake. Its freshness and faint sweetness love salty and spicy food.

Value: outstanding. It’s one of wine’s best-value pleasures, so reach for it without guilt.

Myth-buster: Prosecco is not ‘cheap Champagne.’ It’s a different wine made a different way, on purpose — lighter, fruitier and friendlier. It’s also the base of a Spritz and the classic Bellini, so it earns its keep behind the bar too.
