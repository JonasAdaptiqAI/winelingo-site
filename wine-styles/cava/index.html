Spain’s classic bottle-fermented sparkling wine, made by the same painstaking traditional method as Champagne but from native Catalan grapes.

What makes Cava distinctive

It uses the traditional method — a second fermentation inside the very bottle you buy — but built on sun-ripened Catalan grapes, giving Champagne-style toasty depth at a famously friendly price.

Why it matters

Cava is Spain’s answer to Champagne — a serious sparkling wine made by exactly the same labour-intensive process, but usually at a fraction of the price. That makes it one of the best-value bubbles in the world and a brilliant place for a beginner to learn what ‘traditional method’ tastes like without spending a lot.

It’s a category, not a place. Unlike most European wine names, ‘Cava’ refers to how the wine is made rather than a single spot on the map. The overwhelming majority comes from Catalonia in north-east Spain, around the town of Sant Sadurní d’Anoia, but the law also allows it from a handful of other Spanish zones.

Why care? Once you can taste the difference between Cava and cheap tank-made fizz like Prosecco, a huge part of the sparkling-wine world suddenly makes sense.

What’s in the glass

Most Cava is bone dry (look for Brut or Brut Nature on the label) with high, refreshing acidity and a light-to-medium body — it feels lively and crisp rather than rich.

On the nose and palate, expect: • Fruit — green apple, lemon, pear and sometimes a touch of quince. • Bready, nutty notes — almond, toast and brioche, which come from time spent ageing on the lees (more on that below). • A slightly earthy, savoury edge that sets it apart from sweeter, simpler sparklers.

The bubbles are fine and persistent. Younger, cheaper Cava leans fresh and fruity; longer-aged bottles taste deeper, toastier and more complex.

Where it comes from

The vast majority of all Cava is made in Penedès, in Catalonia, with Sant Sadurní d’Anoia as its beating heart and home to the big houses like Freixenet and Codorníu.

Ageing tiers worth knowing (the longer on the lees, the richer and pricier): • Cava de Guarda — the entry level, aged at least 9 months. • Cava de Guarda Superior — a quality umbrella covering the three tiers below, and now required to come from certified-organic vineyards. • Reserva — minimum 18 months. • Gran Reserva — minimum 30 months; noticeably toasty and complex. • Cava de Paraje Calificado — the top rung, a single-vineyard estate wine aged at least 36 months, rivalling vintage Champagne.

What shapes it

The grapes are native Catalan whites, usually blended: Macabeo (apple and freshness), Xarel·lo (body, structure and that earthy character) and Parellada (delicacy and floral lift). Some Cava also adds Chardonnay, and rosado (pink) Cava is made from red grapes like Garnacha, Pinot Noir or Trepat.

The defining technique is the ‘traditional method’ — the same one used for Champagne. In plain words: the wine is first fully made as a still wine, then a little yeast and sugar are added and it’s sealed in the bottle, where a second fermentation creates the bubbles inside that exact bottle.

’Lees’ ageing is the other key. After that second fermentation the dead yeast cells (the lees) are left resting in the bottle for months or years. They slowly break down and give Cava its signature bready, nutty, creamy depth before being removed.

Good to know

Food: Cava is a tapas natural — try it with olives, almonds, jamón, fried seafood or salty cheese. Its acidity also cuts through fried and oily food beautifully.

Serving: chill it properly (6-8°C) and reach for a tulip-shaped glass rather than a wide coupe, which lets the aromas and bubbles last.

Value & myth-busting: • Cava is not ‘cheap Prosecco.’ Prosecco gets its fizz in a big pressurised tank, which is faster and simpler; Cava earns its bubbles bottle by bottle, giving more toasty complexity. • Dryness terms matter: Brut Nature has no added sugar, Brut is dry, while Semi Seco is gently sweet. • Most Cava is meant to be drunk young and fresh, though Gran Reserva and Paraje Calificado bottles can reward a little patience.
