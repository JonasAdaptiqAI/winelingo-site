Five things to know about Georgian Amber (Qvevri)

  1. In 2009 the whole country had five. When Iago Bitarishvili went looking for wineries selling qvevri wine commercially, he found exactly five: Pheasant’s Tears, Ramaz Nikoladze, Our Wine, Vinoterra and Alaverdi Monastery. An 8,000-year tradition had nearly stopped paying its way.
  2. A Russian ban rebuilt Georgian wine. Moscow’s 2006 embargo cost Georgia roughly 80 per cent of its wine exports overnight. Cut off from bulk semi-sweet sales, growers turned back to quality, to rare varieties and to the qvevri.
  3. The dating comes from chemistry, not legend. Researchers found wine acids in pottery from Gadachrili Gora and Shulaveris Gora, about 50 km south of Tbilisi, dated to 6000–5800 BC and published in PNAS in 2017 — the oldest confirmed winemaking anywhere.
  4. UNESCO listed the method, not the wine. The 2013 inscription covers the ancient Georgian craft of making wine in qvevri — the vessel-making, the burial, the technique — as intangible cultural heritage of humanity.
  5. Georgia’s grape list runs to about 500. Roughly 500 native varieties survive, but only 38 are grown commercially. Kakheti, the amber heartland, produces 70 per cent of the country’s grapes, led by Rkatsiteli.

What makes Georgian Amber (Qvevri) distinctive

Fermenting white grapes on their skins and stems in clay vessels buried underground draws out tannin, colour and a savoury, tea-like grip — turning a white grape into something that drinks like a red.

Why it matters

Georgian amber wine is the original ‘orange wine’ — and arguably the oldest style of wine still made anywhere. Georgians have been fermenting grapes this way for around 8,000 years, and UNESCO has recognised the qvevri (kveh-vree) method as part of humanity’s cultural heritage.

The big idea: an ‘orange’ or ‘amber’ wine is a white wine made like a red. Instead of pressing the juice away from the skins straightaway (the usual way to make white wine), the juice ferments in contact with the grape skins — sometimes for weeks or months. Those skins give colour, tannin and a savoury depth you’d normally only expect from red wine.

For a learner, it’s the perfect bottle to stretch your palate. It looks like a white, drinks a bit like a red, and tastes like nothing else — a brilliant way to understand just how much the skins shape a wine.

What’s in the glass

Don’t expect a crisp, fruity white. Amber wine is fuller-bodied, dry and grippy, with a deep amber-to-orange colour from all that skin contact.

• Aromas: dried apricot, orange peel, black tea, walnut, beeswax and a touch of honey — more ‘savoury and nutty’ than ‘fresh and zingy’. • On the palate: properly dry (no sweetness), with bright acidity and — unusually for a white — real tannin, the same drying, mouth-puckering grip you feel in red wine or strong black tea. • Finish: long, earthy and a little oxidative (think the nutty edge of a good sherry or a bruised apple).

The tannin is the surprise. It’s why amber wine feels structured and almost ‘chewy’, and why it pairs with food like a red rather than a white.

Where it comes from

The heartland is Kakheti, in eastern Georgia — a warm, sunny region that grows roughly three-quarters of the country’s grapes.

• Rkatsiteli (rrkah-tsee-TEL-ee) is the classic and most-planted white grape: high in acid, fairly neutral on its own, but a perfect canvas for long skin contact. • Mtsvane, Kisi and Khikhvi are other local whites often used solo or blended in.

Label terms to know: look for the word ’qvevri’ on the bottle — that’s your signal it was made the traditional buried-vessel way. You may also see the ’Kakhetian method’ (long contact with skins and stems, the boldest, most tannic style) versus the gentler ’Imeretian method’ from western Georgia, which uses far less skin contact for a lighter result. ‘Amber’ and ‘orange’ on a label mean the same thing.

What shapes it — the qvevri

Everything hinges on one vessel. A qvevri is a large clay pot with a pointed base — often the size of a person or bigger — that’s lined with beeswax and buried up to its neck in the ground.

Here’s the technique in plain words:

• Skin contact: crushed white grapes go in with their skins (and traditionally their stems), so the fermenting juice steeps on them — exactly what gives red wine its colour and tannin. This is what makes a white wine turn amber and grippy. • Buried in earth: the surrounding ground keeps the qvevri at a steady, cool temperature naturally, no electricity needed — a clever ancient fridge. • Minimal intervention: wild yeasts do the work, little or nothing is added, and the wine often rests on its skins and lees (spent yeast) for months, deepening the savoury, nutty character.

Kakheti’s warm climate ripens the grapes fully, while the high natural acidity of Rkatsiteli keeps the finished wine fresh despite all that power.

Good to know

Food is where it shines. Because it has tannin and body, treat it like a light red: roast chicken, pork, mushrooms, walnut-based Georgian dishes, aged and hard cheeses, even mildly spiced or fermented foods all work beautifully.

Serving: cool but not cold — around 12-14°C — in a normal wine glass, not a tiny white-wine one. Too cold and the tannins turn harsh; let it warm slightly and the aromas open up.

Ageing & value: good examples can age gracefully for years, and Georgia remains one of the wine world’s best-value frontiers.

Myth-busters: • It is not a sweet wine — amber wines are bone dry; the colour just looks like it might be sweet. • ‘Orange wine’ has nothing to do with oranges — it’s named for the colour. • It isn’t ‘natural wine’ by definition, though many are made with very little intervention. The defining feature is the skin contact, not a marketing label.

Producers to know for Georgian Amber (Qvevri)

1

Pheasant’s Tears

Founded in 2007 by grower Gela Patalishvili and American painter John Wurdeman; about ten native varieties, all fermented in qvevri.

2

Iago’s Wine

Iago Bitarishvili’s tiny estate; he convened the 2009 tasting that counted Georgia’s five remaining commercial qvevri producers.

3

Alaverdi Monastery Cellar

Kakhetian monastery whose cellar records begin in 1011; monks still ferment local whites in buried qvevri there.

4

Schuchmann Wines

The Kakheti producer formerly called Vinoterra, one of the five commercial qvevri makers of 2009 and now an export mainstay.
