A bone-dry, lightly fortified white from Andalusia, aged under a veil of flor yeast.

What makes Sherry (Fino) distinctive

The living flor cap protects the wine from oxygen and gives a unique salty, yeasty, nutty tang — utterly dry despite the fortification.

Why it matters

Fino is the gateway to one of the wine world’s great originals. Sherry is a fortified white wine from southern Spain, and Fino is its driest, freshest, most refreshing style. “Fortified” simply means a little neutral grape spirit is added to raise the alcohol — but unlike sweet ports, Fino ends up completely bone-dry.

Why a learner should care: Fino tastes like nothing else in wine. It’s built around a living layer of yeast called flor (Spanish for “flower”) that grows on the surface of the wine as it ages, shielding it from air while feeding on it. The result is a salty, tangy, savoury drink that retrains your palate.

It’s also a confidence-builder. Sherry has a dusty, old-fashioned reputation, but real Fino is electric, low-priced, and one of the most food-friendly wines on earth. Get to know it and you’ll feel like you’re in on a secret.

What’s in the glass

Expect pale, bone-dry and bracingly fresh. Fino is light-bodied with high, mouth-watering acidity, zero sweetness, and no tannin (tannin is the grippy texture you get from red-wine skins — white wines like this have none).

On the nose: the signature is a yeasty, bakery smell — think bread dough and a faintly sour, savoury tang. Around it sit green apple, blanched almond, chamomile and a clean saline note, like a sea breeze.

On the palate: crisp green-apple fruit, a nutty almond edge, and a striking salty-savoury finish that leaves your mouth watering for the next sip. Alcohol sits around 15%, but it drinks light and dry, never hot or heavy.

The takeaway: if you like crisp, mineral whites or the savoury bite of a good dry martini, Fino will click instantly.

Where it comes from

Fino is born in the “Sherry Triangle” in Andalusia, the sun-baked region of south-west Spain. The classic style is tied to three historic towns near the coast.

• Jerez de la Frontera — the inland heart that gives Sherry its name. • El Puerto de Santa María — on the Atlantic, lending an extra saline lift. • Sanlúcar de Barrameda — by the sea at the mouth of the Guadalquivir, home to Fino’s close cousin Manzanilla.

A note on the name: “Sherry” is simply the old English form of *Jerez*. The official appellation spells it out in three languages at once — D.O. Jerez-Xérès-Sherry — and all three names are legally equal, not translations of each other.

Label terms to know: • Fino — the dry, flor-aged style itself. • Manzanilla — Fino’s sibling, made the same biological way but aged only in Sanlúcar’s cool, humid sea air, which keeps the flor thick year-round. That gives it an even saltier, finer edge. It has its own appellation, Manzanilla-Sanlúcar de Barrameda. • En rama — a barely-filtered Fino bottled close to its raw barrel state; richer and more characterful.

What shapes it — Palomino, flor and the solera

One grape, two great techniques. Fino is made almost entirely from Palomino, a fairly neutral white grape that thrives on the region’s dazzling white albariza soil — a chalky earth that reflects the sun and soaks up winter rain to feed the vines through the bone-dry summers.

Flor is the magic. After the dry wine is lightly fortified to about 15%, a film of wild yeast — the flor — grows across the surface in the barrel. This living veil seals the wine off from the air (so it stays pale and fresh) while feeding on it, throwing off those bready, nutty, tangy aromas. Crucially, the alcohol has to land in a narrow window — roughly 15% — or the flor won’t survive: Fino’s whole personality depends on keeping it alive.

The solera ages it. Sherry isn’t a single vintage. Barrels are stacked and fractionally blended: a little is drawn from the oldest level to bottle, topped up from the next-youngest, and so on down the line. This continuous system, the solera, keeps the flor fed and the style consistent year after year (Fino is aged this way for a minimum of two years).

Good to know

Treat Fino like a crisp white, not a sweet sipper. Serve it well-chilled at 7–9°C in a regular wine glass (a tall white-wine glass beats the tiny traditional copita for catching the aromas).

Pairing — this is Fino’s superpower. It’s a tapas natural: olives, salted almonds, jamón, fried fish, prawns, anchovies, even sushi. The salty-savoury wine and salty-savoury food amplify each other beautifully.

Drink it fresh. Because flor keeps Fino delicate, it does not improve in the bottle — buy recent stock, and once opened treat it like a white wine: refrigerate and finish within a few days.

Myth-busters: • “Sherry is sweet.” Fino is bone-dry — sweet styles like Pedro Ximénez are a different animal. • “It’s a dusty old drink.” Fresh Fino is one of the liveliest, best-value wines you can buy. Don’t leave the open bottle in a warm cupboard — chill it.
