Light, juicy, low-tannin Gamay from granite hills just south of Burgundy.

What makes Beaujolais distinctive

Carbonic-style winemaking amplifies the bright, candied fruit; the ‘Cru’ villages (Morgon, Fleurie) make more serious, age-worthy versions.

Why it matters

Beaujolais is the wine that teaches a lot of people to love red. It’s light, bursting with fresh fruit, and so easy to drink that it sidesteps the things beginners often find off-putting — heavy oak, grippy tannin, brooding density.

A gateway red. Made almost entirely from the Gamay grape in a string of hills just south of Burgundy, it delivers the joy of red wine without the weight. Think of it as the friendly cousin of more serious French reds.

A bit of an underdog. For decades Beaujolais was dismissed thanks to the mass-market Beaujolais Nouveau craze (more on that below). But its top villages quietly make some of France’s best-value, most food-friendly reds — wines that over-deliver for the price and reward anyone curious enough to look past the cliché.

What’s in the glass

Pour a glass and the first thing you’ll notice is how pale and bright it looks — translucent ruby, nothing like an inky Cabernet.

On the nose: lifted, fresh aromas of red cherry, raspberry and cranberry, often with a floral lift of violet. In the most carbonic styles you’ll also catch a playful note of banana or bubblegum — that’s a fingerprint of the winemaking, not the grape.

On the palate: • Body — light • Sweetness — dry (no residual sugar) • Acidity — high and refreshing, the source of that mouth-watering snap • Tannin — low, so it feels smooth and soft rather than drying

The overall impression is juicy, vivid and gulpable. The brightest bottlings drink like crushed summer berries, while the top villages add depth, firmer structure and a savoury, earthy edge.

Where it comes from

Beaujolais sits in eastern France, just south of Burgundy proper, running down toward the city of Lyon. There’s a clear quality ladder worth knowing on the label:

• Beaujolais — the basic, everyday tier, mostly from the flatter southern vineyards where richer clay and limestone soils make lighter, fruitier wine. • Beaujolais-Villages — a step up, drawn from better-sited hillside villages; a reliable sweet spot for quality and price. • Cru Beaujolais — the top rung: ten named villages in the granite hills of the north, so distinctive they’re labelled by village name alone, often without the word Beaujolais on the label.

The Crus to know: Morgon and Fleurie are the famous pair — Morgon is darker and firmer, Fleurie is perfumed and silky. The full ten are Brouilly, Côte de Brouilly, Régnié, Morgon, Chiroubles, Fleurie, Moulin-à-Vent (the most powerful and age-worthy), Chénas, Juliénas and Saint-Amour. If a bottle simply carries one of those names, it’s a Cru.

What shapes it

The grape. One grape does nearly all the work here: Gamay (full name Gamay Noir à Jus Blanc, meaning black grape with white juice). It’s naturally high in acid, low in tannin and packed with red fruit — perfectly suited to a fresh, lively style. A tiny proportion of white grapes is technically allowed in the blend, but in practice the wine is essentially all Gamay.

Soil and climate. The best vineyards sit on granite and pink sandy soils in the northern hills. Granite drains well and is thought to give the Crus their finesse and mineral lift, while the richer clay-and-limestone soils to the south make softer, fruitier wine. The climate is moderate, ripening Gamay without baking out its freshness.

Carbonic maceration — the signature trick. This is the technique that defines the style. Instead of crushing the grapes, winemakers pile whole, intact bunches into a sealed tank. As the grapes settle, fermentation fills the tank with carbon dioxide and locks out oxygen, so fermentation begins inside each unbroken berry (most producers use a semi-carbonic version, where the berries at the bottom are crushed by the weight above and kick the process off). This gentle, low-extraction approach pulls out vivid colour and candied fruit while leaving tannin behind — hence the bright, juicy, sometimes bubblegum character. The serious Crus often use only partial carbonic, or none, making sturdier, more structured wines.

Good to know

Serving. Beaujolais is one of the few reds that genuinely loves a light chill. For the lighter styles, pop the bottle in the fridge for 20–30 minutes and serve around 13–14°C — cool enough to sharpen the fruit and acidity. Structured Crus are happiest a touch warmer, 15–17°C. Served too warm, Beaujolais can taste flat.

Food. Wildly versatile and a brilliant table wine: roast chicken, charcuterie, pork, mushroom dishes, soft cheeses — even hard-to-pair foods like turkey. The high acid and low tannin make it a dream at a crowded dinner.

Ageing. Most Beaujolais is made to drink young and fresh. The exception: top Crus like Moulin-à-Vent and Morgon can age 5–10 years (the very best longer), softening and taking on Burgundy-like complexity.

Myth-buster. Don’t judge it by Beaujolais Nouveau — that’s a fun, super-young wine released just weeks after harvest, on the third Thursday of each November. It’s a marketing event, not the real story. The Villages and Crus are where Beaujolais shows its serious, age-worthy side.
