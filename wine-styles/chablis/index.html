Steely, unoaked Chardonnay from the cool, chalky north of Burgundy.

Five things to know about Chablis

  1. The seven Grand Crus are one hillside, not seven vineyards. Chablis Grand Cru is a single appellation of about 100 hectares, fixed by decree on 13 January 1938 and split into seven named climats — Blanchot, Bougros, Les Clos, Grenouilles, Preuses, Valmur and Vaudésir — on one slope above the town.
  2. There is an eighth Grand Cru that officially is not one. La Moutonne runs to 2.35 hectares, sits 95% inside Vaudésir and 5% inside Preuses, and belongs to Domaine Long-Depaquit alone. It is sold as Chablis Grand Cru, yet it is not one of the seven names.
  3. A Premier Cru name on the label may not be where the grapes grew. Forty named Premier Cru climats are grouped under seventeen flag-bearing names, and a grower in one of the smaller ones may legally use the bigger neighbour’s name, such as Fourchaume or Montmains.
  4. Chablis nearly died out within living memory. Phylloxera, two wars and repeated frost cut the vineyard to about 500 hectares by the 1950s. Chablis and Premier Cru alone now cover some 4,500 hectares, defended each spring by sprinklers that seal the buds in ice.
  5. Petit Chablis is different rock, not watered-down Chablis. It grows up on the plateaux on younger Portlandian limestone, which has none of the fossil oyster shells of the Kimmeridgian slopes below. The name tells you altitude and geology, not a lesser grade.

What makes Chablis distinctive

Cold climate and fossil-rich Kimmeridgian limestone give a lean, mineral, almost saline style — the opposite of buttery oaked Chardonnay.

Why it matters

Chablis is the world’s benchmark for unoaked Chardonnay — and the wine that proves Chardonnay isn’t a single flavour but a chameleon shaped by place. Most people meet Chardonnay as a soft, buttery, oak-aged white. Chablis is the opposite: lean, bright and chiselled, with little to no oak in sight.

Why a learner should care: Chablis is the clearest lesson in terroir — the idea that where a grape grows can matter as much as the grape itself. It’s the same variety as a rich Californian Chardonnay, yet a completely different wine, all down to a cold corner of northern France and unusual chalky soil.

It’s also a gateway to the wider region. Chablis sits at the very north of Burgundy, France’s most revered area for both Chardonnay and Pinot Noir. Understand Chablis and you’ve grasped Burgundy’s golden rule: climate and soil first, winemaking second.

What’s in the glass

Expect a wine that feels taut, dry and refreshing rather than soft or sweet.

• Body: light to medium — never heavy or oily. • Sweetness: bone dry; there’s no sugar to round it off. • Acidity: high and zippy — that mouth-watering, lemon-squeeze freshness is the signature. • Tannin: none worth noting (tannin, the grippy, drying sensation, lives mainly in red wines).

On the nose and palate: green apple, lemon and citrus zest, sometimes a hint of pear or white flowers. The famous twist is a stony, chalky character often described as oyster shell, flint, wet stone or a faint saltiness — what tasters loosely call “minerality.”

The takeaway: if you’ve found buttery, vanilla-heavy Chardonnay too rich, Chablis is the bright, clean, food-friendly counterpoint — crisp and almost saline rather than creamy.

Where it comes from

Chablis lies in northern Burgundy, France — but set well apart from the rest of the region. It’s actually closer to the southern edge of Champagne than to Burgundy’s famous heartland, the Côte d’Or. That cool, northerly, slightly isolated position is the whole story behind its lean style.

Quality tiers, lowest to highest — and they sit right on the label: • Petit Chablis — the entry level; lighter and simpler, easy everyday drinking. • Chablis — the classic core, and the wine most people mean when they say Chablis. • Chablis Premier Cru — wine from specific named vineyard plots (called climats) with a higher reputation; look for names like Montée de Tonnerre (often rated the finest), Fourchaume or Vaillons. • Chablis Grand Cru — the pinnacle: seven adjacent, named vineyards clustered on a single sun-facing hill just outside the town (including Les Clos and Vaudésir), making the richest, most ageworthy wines.

Tip: because the tier is printed on every bottle, it’s an easy shortcut to gauge a wine’s ambition and price.

What shapes it

One grape: Chablis is always 100% Chardonnay — required by law (locals even nickname it Beaunois). Everything distinctive about the wine comes from place, not variety.

• Climate: cool, almost cold, and northern. Short, mild summers ripen the grapes slowly, locking in that high, fresh acidity and keeping alcohol modest. • Soil — the secret weapon: much of Chablis sits on Kimmeridgian limestone, a chalky, clay-rich soil packed with tiny fossilised oyster shells from a Jurassic-era seabed. Many growers credit it for the wine’s stony, almost salty “mineral” edge. • Winemaking — why “unoaked” matters: top Burgundy whites are often aged in new oak barrels, which add vanilla, toast and a creamy texture. Most Chablis instead ferments and matures in stainless steel (or older, neutral barrels that lend no flavour), deliberately keeping the fruit pure, crisp and oak-free. The aim is transparency — letting the soil and climate speak rather than the cellar.

Good to know

Food: Chablis and oysters is one of wine’s great classic pairings — that saline snap mirrors the sea. It’s superb with all shellfish, prawns, white fish, sushi and fresh goat’s cheese, and its high acidity slices cleanly through anything briny or buttery.

Serving: chill to 10–12°C (a couple of hours in the fridge, or pull it out of a colder fridge 15 minutes before pouring). Too cold and the aromas go mute; too warm and it loses its edge.

Ageing: drink most Petit Chablis and Chablis young and fresh, within a few years. Premier and Grand Cru can develop beautifully over 5–10 years or more, gaining honeyed, nutty depth.

Myth-buster: ”I don’t like Chardonnay” usually means ”I don’t like oaky Chardonnay.” Chablis is the proof — same grape, none of the butter.

Don’t confuse: real Chablis is always French Chardonnay from this one area. Cheap jug wines once labelled “chablis” elsewhere simply borrowed the name and have nothing to do with it.

Producers to know for Chablis

1

Domaine François Raveneau

Founded in 1948 and farming just 9.29 hectares across three Grand Crus and six Premiers Crus; widely rated the finest address in Chablis.

2

Domaine William Fèvre

The largest owner of Chablis Grand Cru, holding 15.2 of its hundred hectares, and 78 hectares of vines in all.

3

Domaine Louis Michel et Fils

Moved fully to stainless steel by 1980 and never went back, which makes it the clearest lesson in Chablis without oak.

4

La Chablisienne

The co-operative founded in 1923; around 300 grower families supply more than a quarter of all Chablis, at everyday prices.
