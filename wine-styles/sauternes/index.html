A luscious golden dessert wine made from grapes shrivelled by ‘noble rot’ (botrytis).

Five things to know about Sauternes

  1. The rules cap the crop at a fraction of a normal vineyard’s. Sauternes may yield no more than 25 hectolitres per hectare, among the lowest limits in France, and the juice must carry at least 221 grams of sugar per litre before it can use the name.
  2. The same rows are picked three to six times over. Noble rot arrives berry by berry, so pickers go back through the vineyard again and again across several weeks, taking only the shrivelled fruit. Each pass is called a trie.
  3. In ten vintages Château d’Yquem has bottled nothing at all. 1910, 1915, 1930, 1951, 1952, 1964, 1972, 1974, 1992 and 2012 produced no wine. When the rot fails to come, or comes wrong, the entire crop is declassified.
  4. The great sweet estates make dry wine too. Yquem’s dry white is called Y, from the same vineyard in the same year. When the botrytis will not cooperate, the fruit still has somewhere to go.
  5. These are the only sweet wines ever ranked alongside the reds. The 1855 classification covered the Médoc and, in a separate section, Sauternes and Barsac, judged the same way on merchants’ prices between 1815 and 1855. No other sweet region has been classified like it.

What makes Sauternes distinctive

The fungus concentrates the sugars and adds honeyed, apricot-marmalade complexity; bright acidity keeps the sweetness from cloying.

Why it matters

Sauternes is the world’s most famous sweet wine, and the benchmark against which other dessert wines are measured. If you want to understand what ‘great sweet wine’ means, this is the one to know.

A happy accident of nature. Its golden sweetness comes from a fungus that growers learned to welcome rather than fight (more on that below). The result is a wine that can age for decades and fetch high prices, yet it began as something most winemakers would have thrown away.

Why a learner should care. Sauternes teaches one of wine’s most counter-intuitive lessons: that sweetness on its own is boring, and that the truly great sweet wines are the ones balanced by sharp, mouth-watering acidity. Taste it once and the whole category clicks into place.

What’s in the glass

Pour a glass and you’ll see it straight away: a deep gold colour, almost like liquid honey, deepening towards amber with age.

On the nose. Expect apricot, honey and orange marmalade, often with a savoury whiff of saffron and notes of dried fruit, beeswax and a hint of nuttiness in older bottles.

On the palate. It is full-bodied and richly sweet, coating the mouth with candied apricot and honeyed citrus. But here’s the magic: a vein of bright acidity (the tart, juicy freshness you get from a lemon) cuts straight through the sugar, so it never feels heavy or cloying.

The takeaway. Think of it as luscious but lifted, sweet but refreshing, the way a good fruit tart balances sugar with a squeeze of citrus.

Where it comes from

Sauternes is a small district within Bordeaux, in south-west France, sitting where the cool Ciron stream flows into the warmer Garonne river. That meeting of waters is the whole secret (more in the next section).

The five villages. The appellation covers five communes: Sauternes, Bommes, Fargues, Preignac and Barsac. Wine from any of them can be sold as ‘Sauternes’. Barsac is the exception that gets a choice, its growers may label their wine either ‘Sauternes’ or ‘Barsac’.

The famous name. The most legendary estate is Château d’Yquem, widely regarded as the greatest sweet-wine producer in the world.

Label terms to know. • Sauternes / Barsac on the label tells you the region and that it’s the classic noble-rot dessert style. • A 1855 Classification ranks these wines into three tiers. Château d’Yquem sits alone at the very top as ‘Premier Cru Supérieur’ (Superior First Growth), a rank created for it and it alone, above the First Growths and Second Growths below.

What shapes it

The grapes. Sauternes is mostly Sémillon, prized for its thin skin (which the rot can penetrate easily) and its richness, blended with zesty Sauvignon Blanc for freshness, and often a small splash of Muscadelle.

The defining technique, botrytis. The key is ‘noble rot’, the friendly form of a fungus called Botrytis cinerea. Cool morning mists rising off the Ciron let it settle on ripe grapes, and warm, sunny afternoons keep it from turning destructive. The fungus pierces the skins so water evaporates, shrivelling the grapes like raisins and concentrating their sugar, acid and flavour into a tiny amount of intense, honeyed juice.

Why it’s costly. Botrytis spreads unevenly, berry by berry, so pickers go through the vineyard many times over several weeks, selecting only the shrivelled grapes by hand. Yields are tiny, which is why good Sauternes is never cheap.

Good to know

Serving. Chill it well, to around 8-10°C, and pour small measures, this is a wine to sip, not gulp.

Food pairing. The classic match is salty blue cheese such as Roquefort, a sweet-and-salty pairing made in heaven. It also loves foie gras and fruit tarts, or it can simply stand in for dessert itself.

Ageing. Top bottles can improve for 20-50 years, turning more amber and savoury, but everyday Sauternes drinks beautifully young.

Myth-buster. Don’t confuse it with cheap ‘dessert wine’ on a supermarket shelf, real Sauternes is a hand-made, botrytis wine. And good news: an opened bottle keeps in the fridge for a week or more, because the high sugar and acidity act as natural preservatives, so you needn’t finish it in one sitting.

Producers to know for Sauternes

1

Château d’Yquem

The sole Premier Cru Supérieur of 1855; 126 hectares with about 100 in production, and an average yield of nine hectolitres per hectare.

2

Château Climens

Twenty-nine hectares of Barsac chalk planted entirely to Sémillon, farmed biodynamically, making around 3,000 cases a year.

3

Château Coutet

Thirty-eight-hectare Barsac First Growth whose Cuvée Madame, first made in 1922, appears only in exceptional years, about 700 cases at a time.

4

Château Guiraud

Sauternes First Growth blending an unusually high 35% Sauvignon Blanc with its Sémillon, which keeps the wine brisk.
