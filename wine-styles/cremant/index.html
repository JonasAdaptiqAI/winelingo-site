French sparkling wine made the same way as Champagne, but from outside the Champagne region — at a friendlier price.

Five things to know about Crémant

  1. Crémant used to be a Champagne word. Until 1 September 1994 it named a softer, lower-pressure Champagne, around three to four bars against the usual five to six. Champagne gave the term up in a 1985 deal that stopped everyone else printing méthode champenoise.
  2. Only the first pressing counts. The rules allow just 100 litres of juice from every 150 kilograms of hand-picked whole bunches. Anything squeezed after that has to be sold under another name, which is a large part of why Crémant tastes clean.
  3. The category is younger than most of its drinkers. Crémant de Loire and Crémant de Bourgogne were recognised in 1975, Alsace in 1976, and Bordeaux and Limoux only from 1990. What reads as deep French tradition is a 1970s invention.
  4. One region makes most of it. Crémant d’Alsace alone accounts for roughly half of all French crémant and about a quarter of everything Alsace bottles, which makes it France’s second sparkling wine by volume behind Champagne.
  5. Bollinger has been making Crémant since 1973. The Champagne house took a stake in Langlois-Château at Saumur that year and still owns it — a fair signal that the traditional-method skill set travels well outside Champagne.

What makes Crémant distinctive

It uses Champagne’s exact bottle-fermentation method but comes from eight other French regions, each with its own local grapes — so you get genuine fine-bubble complexity for a fraction of the price.

Why it matters

Crémant (pronounced kray-MON) is France’s smartest sparkling-wine bargain. It is made by the exact same painstaking method as Champagne — but anywhere in France except the Champagne region — so it can’t legally use that famous name, which keeps the price down.

The word originally described a gently fizzy, ‘creamy’ style of sparkling wine, made at lower pressure for a softer mouthfeel; today it’s a protected label for traditional-method sparkling wines from eight French regions, from the Loire to Alsace to Burgundy.

Why a learner should care: Crémant is the easiest way to taste real bottle-fermented complexity — fine bubbles, a hint of bready richness — without paying Champagne prices. Once you know the word, a whole tier of value sparkling opens up to you.

What’s in the glass

Most Crémant is dry, light-to-medium bodied and crisp, with a fine, persistent stream of small bubbles.

• Nose & palate: green apple, lemon and pear up front, often with a gentle brioche, toast or almond note from time spent ageing on its yeast. • Acidity is high, which makes it refreshing and food-friendly; tannin is essentially nil, as with almost all sparkling wine. • Sweetness: usually Brut (bone-dry to barely off-dry). You may also see Extra Brut (drier still) or Demi-Sec (noticeably sweeter).

Style shifts by region: a Crémant de Loire (Chenin Blanc-led) leans appley and floral, a Crémant de Bourgogne (Chardonnay/Pinot Noir) is the closest in spirit to Champagne, and there are pink rosé versions almost everywhere.

Where it comes from

Crémant is not one place but eight official French appellations, each tied to a region with its own grapes and feel:

• Crémant de Loire — Chenin Blanc and Cabernet Franc-led; bright, apple-and-quince freshness. • Crémant d’Alsace — France’s best-selling Crémant; Pinot Blanc, Auxerrois, even Riesling. • Crémant de Bourgogne — Chardonnay and Pinot Noir, the most Champagne-like. • Crémant de Limoux — from the Languedoc; locals claim this hilly area made sparkling wine before Champagne. • Also: Crémant de Bordeaux, de Die, de Savoie and du Jura.

The shared word ‘Crémant’ is the quality signal — it guarantees the traditional method, hand-harvesting and a minimum ageing time, whichever region you pick. (Luxembourg is the one place outside France allowed to use the name.)

What shapes it

The defining technique is the traditional method (méthode traditionnelle) — the same process used for Champagne.

• A still base wine is bottled with a little yeast and sugar, then sealed. A second fermentation happens inside that bottle, trapping carbon dioxide as natural bubbles. • The wine then rests on its spent yeast cells, called the lees, for a minimum of nine months (and at least a year from bottling before release). This ‘lees ageing’ is what adds the savoury bready, biscuity, almondy depth. • Crémant rules also require whole-bunch pressing (gentle, for clean juice) and hand-harvesting.

Grapes vary by region rather than being fixed, which is exactly why no two Crémants taste alike. Cool, northern vineyards keep the acidity high — essential for crisp, age-worthy fizz.

Good to know

Value: This is the headline. Because it can’t say ‘Champagne’, Crémant typically costs far less for comparable craftsmanship — the insider’s choice for celebrating on a budget.

Food & serving: Serve well-chilled, 7-9°C. Brut Crémant is a brilliant aperitif and loves fried or salty nibbles, soft cheese, sushi and brunch. Use a tulip-shaped glass rather than a narrow flute so the aromas can show.

Myth-buster: Crémant is not a cheaper shortcut wine like tank-made Prosecco — it uses the very same in-bottle method as Champagne. The main difference is geography (and the price tag).

Ageing: Most is made to drink young and fresh, though richer, longer-aged bottlings can keep a few years.

Producers to know for Crémant

1

Langlois-Château

Founded at Saumur in 1885; its Crémant de Loire leads on Chenin Blanc and rests about two years on the lees.

2

Wolfberger

An Eguisheim co-operative founded in 1902 drawing on some 1,200 hectares; one of the biggest and steadiest names in Crémant d’Alsace.

3

Bailly Lapierre

A Burgundy co-operative near Saint-Bris that ages its Crémant de Bourgogne in a former limestone quarry 50 metres down, at 12°C (54°F).

4

Antech

Family-run at Limoux since 1860 and sparkling-only since 1965; a dependable benchmark for Crémant de Limoux and Blanquette alike.
