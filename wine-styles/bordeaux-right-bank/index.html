Five things to know about Bordeaux (Right Bank)

  1. Three of the four top estates quit the classification in 2022. Saint-Émilion re-ranks itself every ten years. In 2022, Ausone, Cheval Blanc and Angélus withdrew, objecting that marketing and tourism counted for too much, which left Pavie and a newly promoted Figeac as the only A grade.
  2. Pomerol has never been classified at all. Eight hundred hectares, no crus, no growths, no official ranking of any kind. Price and reputation are the only hierarchy, which is why the producer’s name carries the whole weight here.
  3. Merlot took over because of one cold February. The frost of February 1956 destroyed much of the Right Bank, and growers replanting chose early-ripening Merlot over Cabernet Sauvignon. The soft, plummy house style is partly an accident of weather.
  4. Pomerol’s pecking order is younger than the vines. Vieux Château Certan was the appellation’s most expensive wine until 1875, when its neighbour Pétrus overtook it. The legend at the top is only about 150 years old.
  5. The most famous Right Bank wine is barely Merlot. Château Cheval Blanc runs 57% Cabernet Franc to 40% Merlot, because its vineyard is roughly a third Pomerol clay, a third gravel and a third Saint-Émilion limestone.

What makes Bordeaux (Right Bank) distinctive

Cool clay and limestone soils suit Merlot, giving a rounder, softer, earlier-drinking wine than the Cabernet-led Left Bank — supple plummy fruit over gentler tannins.

Why it matters

If the Left Bank is Bordeaux’s stern, tannic side, the Right Bank is its warm, generous one. The two banks sit on opposite sides of the rivers that run through the region, and the soils — not just the grapes — are what split them.

The Right Bank is Merlot country. Merlot is plump, soft and fruity, so these wines feel rounder, plusher and friendlier than the firmer, Cabernet-led reds across the water. That makes the Right Bank one of the easiest ways into serious Bordeaux for a newcomer.

It also matters because two of the most famous names in red wine live here: Saint-Émilion and Pomerol. Pomerol in particular produces some of the most sought-after (and expensive) Merlot on earth — yet plenty of approachable, fairly priced bottles carry the same DNA.

What’s in the glass

Expect a medium-to-full red that feels smooth and welcoming rather than gripping.

• Fruit: ripe plum, black cherry and blackberry — generous, and a little jammy in warm years. • Beyond the fruit: cocoa, violet, fruitcake spice, and with age a savoury note of truffle, forest floor and dried fig. • Tannin (the drying, grippy sensation from grape skins) is present but soft and velvety — gentler than Left Bank Bordeaux. • Acidity is moderate, so the wine tastes mellow and rounded, not tart.

It is fully dry — no sweetness — but the ripe fruit and supple texture can give an impression of richness that beginners sometimes mistake for sweetness.

Where it comes from

The Right Bank lies east of the city of Bordeaux, around the town of Libourne. Two appellations (legally defined wine zones) lead the way:

• Saint-Émilion: the larger and more varied, ranging from everyday reds to grand estates. Its best wines appear in an official quality classification topped by Premier Grand Cru Classé, with Grand Cru Classé on the rung below — but watch out: a plain Saint-Émilion Grand Cru on a label is just the basic appellation, not the same as the classified Grand Cru Classé. • Pomerol: tiny, with no official classification, yet home to legends like Pétrus. Its iron-rich clay gives especially rich, opulent Merlot.

Nearby appellations such as Lalande-de-Pomerol, Montagne-Saint-Émilion, Fronsac and Castillon offer a similar style for far less money — a smart place for value hunters to start.

What shapes it

The grapes: Merlot is the star — soft, dark-fruited and early-ripening — usually blended with Cabernet Franc, an aromatic, lightly herbal grape that adds violet perfume and freshness. A little Cabernet Sauvignon may sneak in, but it stays a minor player here.

The soils — the real story: the Right Bank is built on cool clay and limestone rather than the warm gravel of the Left Bank. Clay holds water and stays cool, which suits early-ripening Merlot perfectly; it would leave late-ripening Cabernet Sauvignon struggling. This is the main reason the two banks grow different grapes.

Winemaking: the wines are typically aged in oak barrels, which adds vanilla, cedar and gentle spice and helps soften the tannins, building a polished, rounded texture.

Good to know

Food: its plush fruit and softer tannins make it hugely food-friendly — roast chicken or duck, pork, lamb, mushroom dishes and medium-hard cheeses all shine.

Serving: 16-18°C in a large-bowled glass; a short 30-60 minute decant opens younger bottles up nicely.

Ageing: because the tannins are gentler, Right Bank reds usually drink well earlier than Left Bank Bordeaux — often lovely at 5-8 years — though the top estates still age for decades.

Myth-buster: people say ‘Bordeaux is harsh and needs 20 years.’ That’s the Left Bank cliché. The Right Bank is the soft, approachable face of Bordeaux — a perfect bridge if you’ve found classic claret too austere.

Producers to know for Bordeaux (Right Bank)

1

Château Cheval Blanc

Forty-one hectares on the Pomerol boundary making about 6,000 cases a year; bought by Bernard Arnault and Albert Frère in 1998.

2

Château Pétrus

Eleven and a half hectares of iron-rich Pomerol clay, planted entirely to Merlot since 2010, owned by the Moueix family.

3

Château Figeac

Fifty-four hectares in a single block on gravel knolls, planted 35% Cabernet Sauvignon, 35% Cabernet Franc and 30% Merlot.

4

Château de La Dauphine

Fronsac estate of 66 hectares dating to 1750, farmed organically and biodynamically; right-bank Merlot at a fraction of Saint-Émilion money.
