Abruzzo Wine Region

A coastal central-Italian region between the Adriatic Sea and the Apennine mountains, best known for juicy, affordable Montepulciano d’Abruzzo reds (made from the Montepulciano grape, not to be confused with the Tuscan town of the same name), the pale-pink Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo rosé, and crisp white Trebbiano d’Abruzzo.

Country

Key grapes
Montepulciano, Trebbiano Abruzzese, Pecorino, Passerina

Montepulciano d’Abruzzo reds are deep and juicy, full of dark cherry and plum with soft, rounded tannins, a warming savoury edge and only gentle oak. The Cerasuolo rosé is crunchy and red-fruited, while the Trebbiano and Pecorino whites are dry, fresh and citrusy.

How Abruzzo wine tastes

Montepulciano is the region’s calling card. In the glass it’s dark and generous, pouring out ripe black cherry, blackberry and plum with a savoury lift of dried herbs and a little pepper. The tannins (the grippy, drying part of a red that you feel on your gums) are soft and mouth-filling rather than harsh, so even a young bottle feels welcoming.

There’s real freshness underneath the fruit, a streak of acidity that keeps these wines lively rather than jammy. Most everyday examples are unoaked or only lightly aged, which keeps the fruit pure; a handful of ambitious producers use oak and long ageing to build something more serious and long-lived.

Alongside the reds, Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo is a deeply coloured rosé (its name comes from cerasa, cherry) that tastes of exactly that, crunchy red cherry with a savoury snap. The whites, from Trebbiano Abruzzese and increasingly Pecorino, are dry and zesty, running from simple and thirst-quenching to genuinely complex.

Signature styles & what to look for

The everyday face of the region is Montepulciano d’Abruzzo: soft, fruity, brilliant value and made for the dinner table. If you want more depth, look for Colline Teramane, the hilly zone near Teramo that earned its own top DOCG status for more structured, age-worthy reds.

On labels you’ll also see Riserva, meaning the wine has had longer ageing before release and usually offers more concentration. For pink, seek out Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo, a proper rosé with more colour and grip than most. For whites, Trebbiano d’Abruzzo is the classic, while Pecorino and Passerina (both grape names, nothing to do with the cheese) give more aromatic, textured styles well worth trying.

The grapes & the land

Montepulciano is the red grape behind almost everything here, a thick-skinned, late-ripening variety that loves sunshine. Confusingly, it shares its name with a Tuscan town, but the town’s famous wine is actually made from Sangiovese, so this is a different thing entirely. Trebbiano Abruzzese leads the whites, joined by the more perfumed Pecorino and Passerina.

The magic is in the region’s position between two extremes. Warm Adriatic sunshine on one side ripens the fruit fully, while cool air tumbling down from the high Apennines and the Gran Sasso massif on the other keeps everything fresh. That daily warm-then-cool rhythm is why the reds stay plush but lively, and the whites finish clean and bright.

Producers to know in Abruzzo

1

Emidio Pepe

Cult family estate working biodynamically, famous for long-lived Montepulciano and Trebbiano aged in glass-lined concrete.

2

Valentini

Historic, fiercely traditional producer whose Trebbiano d’Abruzzo is regarded as one of Italy’s great white wines.

3

Masciarelli

Influential estate that helped modernise the region, making everything from great-value bottlings to serious single-vineyard reds.

4

Cataldi Madonna

Family winery high up at Ofena, known for characterful Montepulciano and benchmark Pecorino whites.

5

Tiberio

Well-regarded estate celebrated for precise, mineral Trebbiano and Pecorino from old high-altitude vines.

Food pairing. Montepulciano is a natural with hearty, tomato-rich food: lamb, grilled sausages, ragù and aged pecorino cheese all sing alongside it. The Pecorino and Trebbiano whites are lovely with the region’s grilled fish and seafood.

FAQ

Questions & answers

How does Abruzzo wine taste?

The red Montepulciano d’Abruzzo is juicy and full, with dark cherry and plum fruit, soft tannins and a warming, savoury edge. It’s generous but stays fresh, which makes it very easy to drink. The whites are dry and zesty, and the Cerasuolo rosé tastes of crunchy red cherry.

Which wineries is Abruzzo known for?

Some of the most respected names are Valentini and Emidio Pepe, both revered for traditional, age-worthy reds and whites, alongside Masciarelli, Cataldi Madonna and Tiberio. There are also excellent-value co-operative wines throughout the region.

Is Montepulciano d’Abruzzo the same as Vino Nobile di Montepulciano?

No, and it’s a common mix-up. Montepulciano d’Abruzzo is made from the Montepulciano grape in Abruzzo. Vino Nobile di Montepulciano is made mostly from Sangiovese in a Tuscan town that happens to be called Montepulciano. Different grape, different place.
