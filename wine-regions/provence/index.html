Provence Wine

Provence, on France’s sun-soaked southeastern coast, is the country’s benchmark and most iconic region for dry rosé, the wine that made pale pink fashionable worldwide. Its rosés are typically very pale, salmon-colored, crisp and delicately fruity, built for sunshine and the table rather than for ageing. Provence is the most famous name in French rosé, though it is not the largest producer by volume.

Provence is France’s benchmark for dry rosé — typically very pale, salmon-pink wines that are crisp, light and delicately fruity, with red-berry, citrus and a subtle herbal note. Fresh and food-friendly rather than sweet, they are built for sunshine and the table.

How Provence wine tastes

Provence is the home of pale, dry rosé — the style that made pink wine fashionable the world over. In the glass these wines are a delicate salmon or onion-skin pink, and on the palate they are crisp, light-bodied and refreshing, with gentle flavours of strawberry, raspberry, peach and citrus.

Crucially, this is dry rosé — a world away from sweet, candy-pink blush wines. Many bottles also carry a subtle savoury, herbal note that locals link to the garrigue, the wild scrub of rosemary, thyme and lavender that covers the surrounding hills.

While rosé is the headline (and the great majority of production), Provence also makes crisp, mineral whites and a smaller amount of sturdy, herb-scented red — most famously the age-worthy reds of Bandol.

Signature styles & what to look for

Most Provence rosé is made for freshness and youth — drink it within a year or two of the vintage, well chilled, rather than cellaring it. The largest and most familiar appellation is Côtes de Provence, alongside Coteaux d’Aix-en-Provence and Coteaux Varois en Provence.

For something with more structure, look beyond rosé to the region’s specialist pockets. Bandol is prized for powerful, long-lived reds built on the Mourvèdre grape (and serious, age-worthy rosé too); Cassis is known for its whites; and tiny Palette is home to the celebrated estate Château Simone.

Styles range from crisp, easy poolside pink to more textured, gastronomic rosés from top estates. The paler the wine, the shorter its contact with the grape skins — which is where rosé gets both its colour and its light, delicate character.

The grapes & the land

Provence lies along France’s sun-drenched south-eastern coast, with a warm Mediterranean climate that ripens grapes fully and reliably. The freshness in the wines comes from two natural air-conditioners: the cool Mistral, a strong, dry wind that funnels down from the north, and the sea breezes off the Mediterranean.

Rosé here is a blend, drawing on Grenache for fruit, Cinsault for delicacy and pale colour, and Syrah and Mourvèdre for a little structure, with local grapes such as Tibouren also in the mix. The white grape Rolle (better known as Vermentino) gives the region’s fresh whites.

The rosés get their pale hue from only the briefest contact with the dark grape skins — just long enough to tint the juice pink and pick up a whisper of red-berry flavour, before it is drained off to ferment like a white wine.

Producers to know in Provence

1

Château d’Esclans

Côtes de Provence estate behind the hugely popular Whispering Angel and a range of more ambitious, barrel-aged rosés.

2

Domaines Ott

Long-established, prestigious producer of characterful, age-worthy rosé across three estates in Provence.

3

Domaine Tempier

Benchmark Bandol estate celebrated for its powerful Mourvèdre-based reds and its serious, structured rosé.

4

Château de Pibarnon

Leading Bandol estate known for concentrated, long-lived reds and elegant rosé grown on high hillside terraces.

5

Château Simone

Historic estate in the tiny Palette appellation, making distinctive, cellar-worthy white, rosé and red.

Food pairing. Provence rosé is a natural with Mediterranean food — grilled fish and seafood, salade niçoise, tapenade, goat’s cheese and anything herb- and olive-oil-rich — while Bandol’s reds suit lamb, game and daube.

FAQ

Questions & answers

How does Provence wine taste?

Its famous rosé is very pale, dry and crisp, with delicate strawberry, peach and citrus fruit and a subtle herbal note. It is fresh and light rather than sweet — the wines also include mineral whites and, in Bandol, sturdy reds.

Is Provence rosé sweet?

No. Despite its pretty pink colour, Provence rosé is made in a dry, crisp style. The gentle red-berry fruit can read as ‘fruity’, but there is very little sweetness.

Which wineries is Provence known for?

Château d’Esclans (of Whispering Angel fame) and Domaines Ott lead the rosé world, while Domaine Tempier and Château de Pibarnon in Bandol and Château Simone in Palette are benchmark names for structured reds and whites.
