Jerez Wine Region

A sun-baked region in Andalusia in Spain’s far south, and the only place that makes true Sherry — a fortified wine, meaning grape spirit is added to boost its strength. Its styles run from bone-dry, tangy Fino to richer, nutty Oloroso and lusciously sweet Pedro Ximénez (which is also the name of the grape).

Sherry spans a huge range, but the through-line is intensity. Bone-dry Fino and Manzanilla are pale, bracing and savoury, tasting of green apple, almond, brine and a distinctive yeasty tang. The darker styles turn nutty, rich and caramelised, from dry Amontillado and Oloroso (walnut, toffee, dried fruit) to intensely sweet Pedro Ximénez (raisin, fig, molasses).

Five things to know about Jerez

  1. Sherry has no vintage, by design. The solera runs on fractional blending: wine is drawn off the oldest barrels, those are topped up from the next-oldest, and so on down a chain of criaderas. Every bottle carries a little of many years.
  2. The Sherry Triangle stopped being a triangle in 2022. Ageing was confined to Jerez de la Frontera, Sanlúcar de Barrameda and El Puerto de Santa María. Since 10 October 2022 all nine towns of the production zone may age sherry, Chipiona, Rota, Trebujena, Chiclana, Puerto Real and Lebrija included.
  3. Fino and Oloroso are separated by about 1.5 per cent alcohol. Fino is fortified to roughly 15.5 per cent, low enough for the flor yeast to live and shield the wine from air. Oloroso goes to 17 per cent or more, which kills the flor and lets oxygen in.
  4. VOS and VORS are Latin, not English. Vinum Optimum Signatum and Vinum Optimum Rare Signatum, for an average age of at least 20 and 30 years. They were chosen to double neatly as Very Old Sherry and Very Old Rare Sherry.
  5. Nine bottles in ten start from a grape that tastes of almost nothing. Palomino is at least 90 per cent of the crop and is deliberately neutral. Everything you recognise as sherry — the brine, the walnut, the toffee — is built by flor, oxygen and time, not by the fruit.

How Jerez wine tastes

Sherry is a fortified wine, meaning a little neutral grape spirit is added to raise its strength, and it comes in a remarkable spread of styles from bone-dry to treacle-sweet.

At the dry, pale end sit Fino and Manzanilla. These are aged under a natural layer of yeast called flor that grows on the wine’s surface, protecting it from air. The result is very pale, light and bracingly dry, with green apple, almond, a salty, briny tang and a savoury yeasty note. They taste closer to a crisp, savoury white than most people expect.

As sherries age with more exposure to air (oxidation), they darken and deepen. Amontillado starts under flor and then oxidises, giving a dry, amber wine with hazelnut and dried-apple notes. Oloroso is aged oxidatively from the start: dark, dry, full-bodied and nutty, with walnut, toffee and dried fig. Palo Cortado sits intriguingly between the two.

At the sweet end, Pedro Ximénez (PX) is almost black, thick and lusciously sweet, tasting of raisin, fig, date and molasses. Sweet blends are sold as Cream and Medium.

Signature styles & what to look for

The core dry styles, from lightest to richest, are Fino, Manzanilla, Amontillado, Palo Cortado and Oloroso. Pedro Ximénez and Moscatel are the naturally sweet ones, and Cream/Medium are sweetened blends.

Two distinctions are worth understanding. First, biological ageing (under flor, giving pale, tangy Fino/Manzanilla) versus oxidative ageing (exposed to air, giving nutty, dark Oloroso). Second, the solera system: barrels are stacked and fractionally blended so that a little of the oldest wine is always carried through, building consistency and complexity over many years.

For the oldest, most concentrated bottlings, look for the age seals VOS (roughly 20 years and over) and VORS (roughly 30 years and over). Manzanilla is simply a Fino-style sherry made in the seaside town of Sanlúcar de Barrameda, where the humid conditions give it an extra saline delicacy. En rama on a label means a Fino or Manzanilla bottled with minimal filtering, for a fuller, more textural style.

The grapes & the land

Jerez lies in sun-baked Andalusia, in Spain’s far south, in a fundamentally hot, dry climate with long arid summers. The prized soil is a brilliant-white, chalky type called albariza, valued because it soaks up the scarce winter rain and reflects the fierce sun, helping the vines through the drought.

The workhorse grape is Palomino, a fairly neutral white variety that is the canvas for almost all the dry styles, its character coming from ageing rather than the grape itself. Pedro Ximénez and Moscatel are dried in the sun after picking to concentrate their sugars for the sweet wines.

The other key ingredient is the local climate itself: humid Atlantic westerly winds keep the flor yeast alive in the barrels near the coast, which is what makes the delicate biological styles possible here in the first place.

Producers to know in Jerez

1

González Byass

Historic Jerez house (est. 1835) behind the world-famous ‘Tío Pepe’ Fino.

2

Lustau

Founded 1896; celebrated for a wide, high-quality range across every sherry style.

3

Valdespino

Long-established bodega known for prestigious single-vineyard Finos and old soleras.

4

Barbadillo

Leading Manzanilla house in Sanlúcar de Barrameda, maker of the classic ‘Solear’.

5

Hidalgo-La Gitana

Sanlúcar producer famed for its ‘La Gitana’ Manzanilla.

Food pairing. Dry Fino and Manzanilla are superb with the local tapas: olives, almonds, cured ham, fried fish and seafood. Nutty Amontillado and Oloroso suit soups, mushrooms, hard cheeses and roast poultry, while sweet Pedro Ximénez is a decadent match for chocolate desserts or simply poured over vanilla ice cream.

FAQ

Questions & answers

How does Jerez (Sherry) wine taste?

It depends on the style. Fino and Manzanilla are pale, bone-dry and savoury with almond and briny notes; Amontillado and Oloroso are dry but nutty and rich; and Pedro Ximénez is intensely sweet, tasting of raisin and molasses.

Which bodegas is Jerez known for?

González Byass (Tío Pepe), Lustau, Valdespino, and, for Manzanilla from Sanlúcar, Barbadillo and Hidalgo-La Gitana are among the most respected houses.

Is all Sherry sweet?

No, and this is the biggest myth about it. Many of the finest sherries, including Fino, Manzanilla, Amontillado and Oloroso, are completely dry. Only the Pedro Ximénez, Moscatel, Cream and Medium styles are sweet.
