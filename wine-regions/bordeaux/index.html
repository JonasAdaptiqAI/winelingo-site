Bordeaux Wine Region

Historic French region famed for structured red blends.

Country
France
World
Old World

Structured, dry red blends with blackcurrant and dark plum fruit, firm tannins and savoury notes of cedar, tobacco, graphite and pencil shavings. Built to age, they soften into something supple and complex over years.

Five things to know about Bordeaux

  1. The ranking has changed once at the top in over 170 years. Napoleon III ordered the 1855 classification for the Paris Exposition Universelle. Since then exactly one estate has moved into the First Growths: Mouton Rothschild, promoted from Second on 21 June 1973.
  2. Merlot is the Bordeaux grape, not Cabernet. Cabernet Sauvignon takes the glory, but Merlot is around two-thirds of Bordeaux’s red plantings and Cabernet Sauvignon just over a fifth. Most red Bordeaux is a Merlot wine with Cabernet in support, not the reverse.
  3. Sauternes was classified in 1855 too. The same exercise ranked the sweet whites of Sauternes and Barsac, and gave Château d’Yquem a tier of its own, Premier Cru Supérieur, held by no other estate then or since.
  4. Bordeaux is pulling vines out of the ground. In 2023 the CIVB and the French state funded the grubbing-up of up to 9,500 hectares at around 6,000 euros a hectare. The region that ranked its wines for an emperor is now shrinking to clear a glut.
  5. Six new grapes were let in for the climate. In January 2021 INAO approved Arinarnoa, Castets, Marselan and Touriga Nacional for red Bordeaux, plus Alvarinho and Liliorila for white. They are capped at five per cent of a vineyard and ten per cent of a blend.

How Bordeaux wine tastes

Classic red Bordeaux tastes of blackcurrant (cassis) and dark plum wrapped in a firm, gently drying grip of tannin. Around the fruit you often find savoury, grown-up aromas: cedar wood, tobacco leaf, graphite and a whiff of pencil shavings, deepened by time in oak barrels.

Style shifts across the region. Wines from the Left Bank (led by Cabernet Sauvignon) tend to be firmer, more structured and slower to open, while those from the Right Bank (led by Merlot) are usually rounder, plusher and more approachable when young.

Young Bordeaux can feel tight and tannic. Its magic is in the ageing: over five, ten or twenty years the tannins soften and the fruit turns into a mellow, layered whole. Bordeaux also makes crisp dry whites and famously sweet ones, so the region is far from reds alone.

Signature styles & what to look for

The heart of Bordeaux is the red blend, typically Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot with supporting roles for Cabernet Franc, Petit Verdot and Malbec. Left Bank wines lean on Cabernet Sauvignon for structure; Right Bank wines lean on Merlot for suppleness.

Bordeaux is famous for its classifications. The historic 1855 Classification ranks the top Médoc estates from First to Fifth Growth (Premier to Cinquième Cru), with the five First Growths at the summit. Saint-Émilion has its own separate ranking, and Cru Bourgeois flags reliable, more affordable Médoc châteaux.

Beyond the reds, look for dry whites from Sauvignon Blanc and Sémillon, and the honeyed sweet wines of Sauternes and Barsac, made from grapes shrivelled by noble rot.

The grapes & the land

Red Bordeaux is a blend, chiefly Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot, supported by Cabernet Franc, Petit Verdot and a little Malbec. The whites are Sauvignon Blanc and Sémillon, the latter also central to the sweet wines.

The region has a maritime climate, moderated by the Atlantic and the Gironde estuary that splits it into Left and Right Banks. This cooler, damper setting ripens Cabernet slowly, building the firm tannin and cassis depth that Bordeaux is known for.

Soil is the reason for the blend. The Left Bank’s warm, well-drained gravel banks suit late-ripening Cabernet Sauvignon, while the Right Bank’s cooler clay and limestone favour earlier-ripening Merlot. Blending across grapes and plots is how Bordeaux balances a wine and evens out the vintage.

Producers to know in Bordeaux

1

Château Margaux

First Growth of the Médoc from the Margaux appellation, celebrated for perfumed, elegant, long-lived reds.

2

Château Latour

Pauillac First Growth known for powerful, structured wines built to age for decades.

3

Château Lafite Rothschild

Pauillac First Growth famed for restrained, refined, aromatically complex Cabernet-based reds.

4

Château Haut-Brion

The oldest of the First Growths, in Pessac-Léognan, known for earthy, savoury reds and fine dry whites.

5

Château Cheval Blanc

Leading Right Bank estate in Saint-Émilion, prized for plush, Cabernet Franc-influenced blends.

Food pairing. A natural partner for red meat: roast lamb, grilled steak, duck and game, whose richness tames the tannins. Right Bank Merlot blends also suit softer dishes, while sweet Sauternes is classic with blue cheese and fruit tarts.

FAQ

Questions & answers

How does Bordeaux wine taste?

Most red Bordeaux is dry and structured, with blackcurrant and dark plum fruit, firm tannins and savoury cedar, tobacco and graphite notes. It typically softens and gains complexity with age.

Which châteaux is Bordeaux known for?

Famous estates include the First Growths Château Margaux, Château Latour, Château Lafite Rothschild and Château Haut-Brion, along with Right Bank stars such as Château Cheval Blanc.

What is the 1855 Classification?

It is a historic ranking of the top Médoc red-wine estates into five tiers, from First to Fifth Growth, with the five First Growths regarded as the pinnacle. It still shapes Bordeaux’s reputation today.
