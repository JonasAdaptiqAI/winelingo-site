Kakheti Wine Region

Georgia’s largest and most important wine region in the east, heartland of an 8,000-year winemaking tradition, famous for amber (orange) wines and robust Saperavi reds.

Country

World

Climate
Warm, semi-continental
Key grapes
Saperavi, Rkatsiteli, Mtsvane, Kisi

Kakheti’s Saperavi reds are deeply coloured, full-bodied and bold, with dark plum, blackberry and blackcurrant fruit, firm tannins and a fresh, tangy acidity. Its traditional amber (orange) wines, whites fermented on their skins in buried clay vessels, are structured and grippy, tasting of dried apricot, walnut, honey and black tea.

Five things to know about Kakheti

  1. Kakheti is not one Georgian region among many — it is most of them. Around 70% of all the grapes grown in Georgia come from here. When somebody hands you a Georgian wine, the odds are strongly that it was made in this valley.
  2. UNESCO listed the qvevri method in 2013. Not as a monument but as living heritage. A qvevri runs from 20 to 10,000 litres, with 800 typical; it is lined with beeswax, buried to the neck and left sealed for at least five to six months.
  3. Saperavi means dye. The Georgian name is literal: the pulp is red as well as the skin, which almost no wine grape manages. That makes it a teinturier variety, and one of the very few anywhere bottled on its own.
  4. Georgia keeps about 500 grape varieties and commercially grows 38. The rest sit in collections, monastery plots and village gardens. It is one of the largest native grape libraries on earth, and Kakheti is where most of the working ones are planted.
  5. 2006 changed the business. Russia banned Georgian wine outright that year, closing the market that had taken the bulk of it. Producers had to win over drinkers who cared what was in the bottle, and the qvevri amber wines went from village custom to export.

How Kakheti wine tastes

Two very different styles define this ancient region.

The red is Saperavi, and it’s a powerhouse. Because the grape has dark flesh as well as dark skin (unusual, and called a teinturier variety), the wines are almost inky in colour and full-bodied and bold, with dark plum, blackberry and blackcurrant fruit, firm tannins and a lively, tangy acidity that keeps them fresh. There’s often a savoury, earthy depth underneath the fruit.

The other signature is amber wine (also called orange wine). White grapes are fermented on their skins, stalks and pips for weeks or months in qvevri, large egg-shaped clay vessels buried underground. This gives an amber-gold wine that is dry, structured and grippy with tannin, tasting of dried apricot, walnut, honey, orange peel and black tea. It’s a world away from a crisp, fruity modern white, more savoury and textural.

You’ll also find lighter, fresher whites made in the modern (steel-tank) way, but the qvevri styles are the region’s heart.

Signature styles & what to look for

The essentials are Saperavi reds (dry, and also a traditional semi-sweet style) and qvevri-fermented amber wines from grapes like Rkatsiteli and Mtsvane.

A useful word on labels is ’qvevri’ itself, which signals the traditional buried-clay method and, for whites, usually the skin-contact amber style. You may also see ’Kakhetian method’, meaning a long skin-and-stalk maceration.

Kakheti also has several named appellations worth recognising: Tsinandali (a dry white blend of Rkatsiteli and Mtsvane), Mukuzani and Napareuli (dry, oak-aged or serious Saperavi reds), and Kindzmarauli and Kvareli (naturally semi-sweet Saperavi reds). Georgia’s semi-sweet reds are a genuine local tradition rather than a modern confection, so don’t be put off by the sweetness on the label.

The grapes & the land

Kakheti, in eastern Georgia, is the heartland of a winemaking tradition stretching back some 8,000 years, which is why the qvevri method here is recognised as living cultural heritage rather than a revival.

The climate is warm and sunny, sheltered by the Caucasus Mountains to the north and cooled by mountain rivers and altitude, so grapes ripen fully while keeping acidity, which is exactly why the Saperavi comes out so deep, ripe and full-bodied.

The key grapes are all indigenous: red Saperavi, and the whites Rkatsiteli, Mtsvane, Kisi and Khikhvi, all well suited to the long qvevri skin-fermentation that gives the amber wines their tannic grip and dried-fruit character.

Producers to know in Kakheti

1

Pheasant’s Tears

Sighnaghi producer dedicated to traditional qvevri wines from indigenous grapes.

2

Schuchmann Wines

Napareuli estate respected for both modern and traditional qvevri amber wines and Saperavi.

3

Alaverdi Monastery

Has made qvevri wine at its cellar for around a thousand years; releases the ‘Since 1011’ label.

4

Teliani Valley

Long-established Kakhetian winery known across the country for its Saperavi and named-appellation wines.

5

Tbilvino

Large, well-regarded producer in Kvareli making both everyday and qvevri Georgian wines.

Food pairing. Bold Saperavi is a fine match for grilled and roast meats, Georgian barbecue and hearty stews. The tannic amber wines love rich, spiced and savoury dishes, walnut-based Georgian recipes, cured meats and strong cheeses.

FAQ

Questions & answers

How does Kakheti wine taste?

The Saperavi reds are deep, full-bodied and bold, with dark plum and blackberry fruit, firm tannins and tangy acidity. The traditional amber wines are grippy and savoury, tasting of dried apricot, walnut, honey and black tea.

Which wineries is Kakheti known for?

Pheasant’s Tears, Schuchmann Wines, Alaverdi Monastery, Teliani Valley and Tbilvino are all well-known producers of Saperavi and traditional qvevri wines.

What is qvevri wine?

It’s wine fermented and aged in a qvevri, a large clay vessel buried in the ground. For white grapes this means long contact with the skins, giving Georgia’s famous amber (orange) wines their colour, tannic grip and dried-fruit flavours. The method is around 8,000 years old.
