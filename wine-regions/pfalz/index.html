Wine region

Pfalz Wine

Germany’s second-largest and one of its sunniest regions, sheltered by the Haardt mountains. It is known for riper, fuller-bodied Rieslings alongside a strong showing of Pinot-family wines.

Country
Germany
World
Old World
Climate
Mild, sunny, relatively warm
Key grapes
Riesling, Spätburgunder, Weissburgunder, Grauburgunder

Pfalz Riesling is riper and rounder than Germany’s cooler regions — ripe orchard and stone fruit, a fuller body and a juicy line of acidity, made in both dry and off-dry styles. The region also turns out serious, savoury Pinot Noir and creamy Pinot Blanc and Pinot Gris.

How Pfalz wine tastes

If your idea of German Riesling is featherlight and racy, the Pfalz will surprise you. This is one of Germany’s warmest, sunniest regions, and the Rieslings show it: riper, fuller and more orchard-fruited, with peach, apricot and yellow apple in place of the piercing lime and green apple of the cooler north.

Most of the best examples today are trocken (fully dry), with the ripeness balanced by a mouth-watering vein of acidity. You will also find gently off-dry and lusciously sweet styles, but the region’s modern reputation rests on its dry whites.

Alongside Riesling, the Pinot family thrives here. Weissburgunder (Pinot Blanc) and Grauburgunder (Pinot Gris) give soft, creamy, food-friendly whites, while Spätburgunder (Pinot Noir) makes genuinely serious reds — red-fruited, earthy and finely structured.

Signature styles & what to look for

Start with the sweetness cue. Trocken means dry; halbtrocken or feinherb means off-dry; and the traditional Prädikat ladder — Kabinett, Spätlese, Auslese and beyond — signals grapes picked progressively riper, often (though not always) making richer, sweeter wines.

For the top dry bottlings, look for Grosses Gewächs (GG) on labels from members of the VDP growers’ association — this marks a dry wine from a classified top vineyard, and it is where the region’s most ambitious Rieslings live.

It also helps to know the two halves of the region. The Mittelhaardt in the north is the classic heartland of grand old Riesling estates, while the Südliche Weinstraße in the south has become a hotbed for excellent dry Riesling and Pinot Noir. For everyday drinking, the deeply coloured Dornfelder grape makes soft, fruity reds.

The grapes & the land

The Pfalz shelters behind the Haardt mountains, which cast a rain shadow that makes the region unusually warm, dry and sunny for Germany — it is effectively the northern continuation of France’s Alsace. That extra warmth is why the grapes ripen so fully and the wines taste rounder.

Riesling is the star, planted across a patchwork of soils — sandstone, loam, limestone and more — that each lend a slightly different accent, from mineral tension to broad, fruity generosity.

The warmth also lets red grapes ripen properly, which is why the Pinot family is such a strength here. Spätburgunder in particular has gone from rustic to refined, giving the Pfalz a reputation for some of Germany’s best reds as well as its most generous Rieslings.

Producers to know in Pfalz

1

Dr. Bürklin-Wolf

Historic Mittelhaardt estate with roots to the 16th century, biodynamically farmed and known for benchmark dry Rieslings from top Forst and Wachenheim sites.

2

Geheimer Rat Dr. von Bassermann-Jordan

Long-established Deidesheim estate (founded 1718) with holdings in many of the region’s finest Riesling vineyards.

3

Reichsrat von Buhl

Historic Deidesheim house holding grand Forst sites such as Kirchenstück and Jesuitengarten, known for precise dry Riesling and traditional-method sparkling.

4

Müller-Catoir

Family estate since 1744, celebrated for intense, characterful Riesling as well as Scheurebe and Rieslaner.

5

Ökonomierat Rebholz

Leading Südliche Weinstraße grower, a pioneer of bone-dry Riesling and fine Spätburgunder.

Food pairing. Dry Pfalz Riesling is superb with pork, schnitzel, spicy Asian dishes and richer fish, the Pinot Blanc and Pinot Gris flatter creamy dishes and poultry, and the Spätburgunder suits duck, mushrooms and roast meats.

FAQ

Questions & answers

How does Pfalz wine taste?

Its Rieslings are riper and fuller than in cooler German regions — think peach, apricot and yellow apple with a juicy acidity — and are mostly made dry. The region also makes savoury Pinot Noir and soft, creamy Pinot Blanc and Pinot Gris.

Is Pfalz Riesling sweet or dry?

Both are made, but the region’s modern reputation rests on dry (trocken) styles. Check the label: trocken is dry, while the Prädikat terms like Spätlese and Auslese often signal sweeter wines.

Which wineries is Pfalz known for?

Historic Mittelhaardt estates such as Dr. Bürklin-Wolf, Bassermann-Jordan and Reichsrat von Buhl, alongside Müller-Catoir and the southern star Ökonomierat Rebholz, are all well-established names.
