Sicily Wine

Italy’s largest wine region by vineyard area, a sun-soaked Mediterranean island known for bold reds from the volcanic slopes of Mount Etna and the value-driven Nero d’Avola, alongside the historic fortified wine Marsala.

Country
Italy
World
Old World
Climate

Key grapes
Nero d’Avola, Nerello Mascalese, Grillo, Carricante

Sicily wears two faces: warm, ripe, mouth-filling Nero d’Avola with dark cherry, plum and soft spice; and, up on Mount Etna, lighter, savoury Nerello Mascalese with red-cherry fruit, bright acidity and a smoky, stony edge. The whites are crisp, citrussy and often sea-fresh.

How Sicily wine tastes

Sicily really offers two very different drinking experiences, and it helps to know which one is in your glass. The island’s warm, sunny climate ripens grapes fully, so a classic Sicilian red like Nero d’Avola tends to be generous and mouth-filling: think ripe dark cherry and plum, a little sweet spice, soft rounded tannin and a warming, full body.

Up on the slopes of Mount Etna, an active volcano in the north-east, everything changes. High-altitude vineyards and cool nights slow the ripening right down, so reds made from Nerello Mascalese come out paler and more savoury, with bright red-cherry fruit, fresh acidity, and a distinctive smoky, stony character from the volcanic soil. Many drinkers compare Etna reds to red Burgundy or Barolo rather than to a big, sunny southern red.

Sicilian whites are refreshing and food-friendly: crisp, citrussy and often with a saline, sea-breeze freshness that reflects the island’s long coastline.

Signature styles & what to look for

For an easy, crowd-pleasing red, look for Nero d’Avola, Sicily’s flagship black grape, often bottled on its own and offering lovely value. Cerasuolo di Vittoria, the island’s only DOCG (its top quality tier), blends Nero d’Avola with the lighter Frappato for a brighter, more perfumed style.

For something more distinctive, seek out Etna Rosso (mainly Nerello Mascalese) and Etna Bianco (from the mineral white grape Carricante). Some producers bottle single ‘contrade’, the individual named vineyard plots on the volcano, much as Burgundy does, and these are worth exploring if you want to taste how the mountain’s different sites vary.

Among whites, Grillo and Catarratto give fresh, everyday wines. And don’t forget Marsala, Sicily’s historic fortified wine from the west of the island: a caramel-and-nut, oxidative style that runs from dry aperitif to sweet pudding wine.

The grapes & the land

The key red grapes are Nero d’Avola (warm, ripe and widely planted across the island) and Nerello Mascalese (the elegant, high-altitude speciality of Etna). Among whites, Carricante gives Etna its crisp, age-worthy wines, while Grillo and Catarratto are the workhorses of the warmer lowlands.

Sicily’s split personality comes straight from the land. Most of the island enjoys a hot, dry Mediterranean climate, tempered by sea breezes and cooler nights that keep the wines from tasting merely jammy. Mount Etna is the exception: here vines grow on black volcanic soils at real altitude, sometimes above 900 metres, where the thin air and big day-to-night temperature swings give the wines their unusual freshness and smoky minerality. Centuries of trade also left their mark, shaping the oxidative winemaking behind Marsala.

Producers to know in Sicily

1

Planeta

Family winery central to Sicily’s modern revival, with estates across the island and well-known Nero d’Avola and Chardonnay.

2

Donnafugata

Family-owned estate known for expressive Nero d’Avola (its Mille e una Notte) and aromatic Sicilian whites.

3

Tenuta delle Terre Nere

Benchmark Mount Etna estate for single-vineyard Nerello Mascalese reds from the volcano’s northern slopes.

4

Benanti

Historic Etna producer that helped revive the volcano’s native grapes, known for Carricante whites and Nerello reds.

5

Passopisciaro (Vini Franchetti)

Pioneering Etna estate making single-’contrada’ Nerello Mascalese from old, high-altitude vines.

Food pairing. Nero d’Avola loves grilled lamb, caponata and hard cheeses; savoury Etna reds suit mushroom dishes and roast poultry; the crisp whites are lovely with sardines and seafood pasta.

FAQ

Questions & answers

How does Sicily wine taste?

It depends where on the island it is from. Lowland reds like Nero d’Avola are warm, ripe and full, with dark cherry, plum and soft spice. Reds from Mount Etna, made from Nerello Mascalese, are lighter, fresher and more savoury, with a smoky, stony edge. The whites are crisp and citrussy.

Which wineries is Sicily known for?

Planeta and Donnafugata are among the big names driving Sicily’s modern revival. On Mount Etna, look for Tenuta delle Terre Nere, Benanti and Passopisciaro, all specialists in the volcano’s elegant Nerello Mascalese reds.

What is the difference between Nero d’Avola and Etna wine?

Nero d’Avola is a warm-climate grape grown across much of Sicily, giving bold, ripe, generous reds. Etna wine comes from high-altitude volcanic vineyards on Mount Etna, mainly from Nerello Mascalese, and is lighter, fresher and more savoury, with a stony minerality.
