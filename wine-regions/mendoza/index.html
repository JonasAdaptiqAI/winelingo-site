Mendoza Wine Region

Argentina’s largest and most important wine region, famous above all for bold, plush red Malbec grown in the foothills of the Andes.

How Mendoza wine tastes

Mendoza means Malbec, and its style is instantly appealing: deep purple in the glass, brimming with ripe plum, blackberry and black cherry, often lifted by a pretty floral note of violets. Oak ageing adds a gentle layer of vanilla, mocha and sweet spice.

What makes it so easy to love is the texture. The wine is rich and velvety, with smooth, supple tannins, yet the high-altitude vineyards keep a bright, fresh core so it rarely feels flabby or heavy. It is generous without being clumsy.

Style does shift with elevation. Warmer, lower sites give the plushest, most rounded, fruit-forward wines, while the cooler, higher Uco Valley produces more structured Malbec with firmer tannins, darker fruit and a savoury, mineral streak that rewards ageing.

Signature styles & what to look for

The signature is varietal Malbec, from soft and juicy everyday bottles to concentrated, oak-aged and single-vineyard wines built to age. On a label, the sub-region is your best clue to style.

Look for Lujan de Cuyo, the historic heartland known for classic, rounded Malbec and home to many old vines, and Valle de Uco (the Uco Valley), the high-altitude zone prized for fresher, firmer, more mineral and age-worthy reds. Specific district names such as Gualtallary or Paraje Altamira signal top high-elevation sites.

Beyond Malbec, keep an eye out for Cabernet Sauvignon, increasingly fine Cabernet Franc, the local speciality Bonarda (juicy, everyday reds) and, among whites, aromatic Torrontes and crisp high-altitude Chardonnay.

The grapes & the land

Malbec is the flagship, supported by Cabernet Sauvignon, Cabernet Franc, Bonarda, Syrah and the white Torrontes. The vineyards lie in the foothills of the Andes, among the highest in the world, from around 600 metres up to 1,500 metres and beyond in the Uco Valley.

The climate is high-altitude desert: dry, sunny and almost rain-free, with intense daytime sun and cold mountain nights. Water comes largely from Andean snowmelt carried by rivers and channels for irrigation, and the dry air keeps the vines healthy.

That big daily temperature swing is the key to the style. Strong sun ripens the fruit to deep colour and flavour, while the cold nights preserve acidity and freshness, giving wines that are rich and velvety yet still bright. Altitude also intensifies sunlight, thickening skins for more colour and structure.

Producers to know in Mendoza

1

Catena Zapata

Family bodega founded in 1902 and a pioneer of high-altitude Malbec, central to Argentina’s quality wine revival.

2

Zuccardi

Family winery founded in 1963, now a leading name for high-altitude, terroir-focused Malbec from the Uco Valley.

3

Achaval-Ferrer

Premium producer known for concentrated, low-intervention single-vineyard Malbecs and the Quimera blend.

4

Salentein

Pioneering Uco Valley estate that helped establish the high-altitude Valle de Uco as a top Malbec zone.

5

Trapiche

Historic, large-scale bodega dating to 1883, a longstanding pioneer of Argentine Malbec across many price points.

Food pairing. A classic match for grilled steak and the Argentine asado; also great with barbecued and roasted meats, lamb, empanadas and hard, mature cheeses.

FAQ

Questions & answers

How does Mendoza wine taste?

Mendoza is famous for bold, plush Malbec: ripe plum, blackberry and black cherry fruit with floral violet notes and sweet spice from oak. It is rich and velvety with supple tannins, yet kept fresh and bright by its high-altitude vineyards.

Which wineries is Mendoza known for?

Well-known Mendoza producers include Catena Zapata, Zuccardi, Achaval-Ferrer, Salentein and Trapiche. Catena Zapata in particular was central to reviving Malbec and championing high-altitude vineyards.

What does the Uco Valley mean on a Mendoza label?

Valle de Uco is Mendoza’s high-altitude sub-region. Its cooler, elevated sites tend to give fresher, firmer, more mineral and age-worthy Malbec, compared with the plusher, rounder style of warmer, lower areas like Lujan de Cuyo.
