Sonoma County Wine Region

A large, diverse California region next to Napa, ranging from foggy coastal zones to warmer inland valleys. It is celebrated for both cool-climate Pinot Noir and Chardonnay and richer Cabernet and Zinfandel further inland.

Cool-coast Pinot Noir is silky and bright with red cherry and a savoury edge, while Chardonnay runs from crisp and citrussy to gently creamy. Inland, Zinfandel is brambly, jammy and peppery, and Cabernet Sauvignon is fuller and riper with blackcurrant and soft tannin.

Five things to know about Sonoma County

  1. Sonoma was making wine commercially before Napa was. Agoston Haraszthy founded Buena Vista here in 1857 and it is still California’s oldest commercial winery. Franciscan padres had planted vines at the Sonoma mission back in 1824 and were pressing sacramental wine by 1825.
  2. The newest appellation was drawn around wind, not warmth. The Petaluma Gap, approved in December 2017 as Sonoma’s eighteenth AVA, sits in a break in the coastal hills where a daily afternoon blast off the Pacific cuts yields and stretches ripening. Three-quarters of it is Pinot Noir.
  3. It grows more vines than Napa and gets less credit. Sonoma County farms roughly 60,000 acres (24,000 hectares) of wine grapes across 19 AVAs, against Napa’s 47,000. The trade-off is that Sonoma has no single house style to sell.
  4. Almost every vineyard in the county is certified sustainable. In September 2019 Sonoma County Winegrowers announced that third-party auditors had certified 99 per cent of the county’s vineyard acreage — a whole region, not a handful of showcase estates.
  5. Sonoma Coast on a label rarely means the coast. That AVA is enormous and reaches far inland, which is why growers pushed for a tighter one. The West Sonoma Coast AVA, approved in 2022, is the name to look for if you want genuinely cold, ocean-facing Pinot Noir.

How Sonoma County wine tastes

Sonoma is big and varied, so the taste really depends on where in the county the grapes were grown, cool and coastal or warm and inland.

From the cooler, fog-cooled zones near the coast and along the Russian River, Pinot Noir is silky and fragrant, with red cherry and raspberry, a savoury, earthy edge and gentle tannin. Chardonnay from these areas ranges from crisp and citrussy to softly creamy, often with a hint of oak.

Head inland to the warmer valleys and the wines get bolder. Zinfandel turns brambly and jammy, with blackberry, black pepper and a warming kick of alcohol, while Cabernet Sauvignon is full and ripe with blackcurrant and rounded tannin. Next to neighbouring Napa, Sonoma often feels a touch more relaxed and fruit-forward.

Signature styles & what to look for

Sonoma’s two headline acts are cool-climate Pinot Noir and Chardonnay and richer, inland reds. For the former, look to the Russian River Valley and the coastal Sonoma Coast; these names on a label point to brighter, more elegant styles.

For classic California Zinfandel, seek out Dry Creek Valley, the county’s spiritual home for the grape, where many wines come from gnarled old head-trained vines that are decades, sometimes a century, old. Alexander Valley is the place for plush, ripe Cabernet Sauvignon.

Unlike much of Europe, California doesn’t use ageing tiers like ‘Reserva’; the word ‘Reserve’ on a US label is a producer’s own term rather than a legal classification. The genuinely useful clues are the grape variety and, above all, the specific AVA (American Viticultural Area). As a rule, the smaller and more precise the named area, the more distinctive the wine.

The grapes & the land

The signature grapes are Pinot Noir and Chardonnay in the cool areas, and Zinfandel and Cabernet Sauvignon where it is warmer; Sauvignon Blanc also does well.

The magic ingredient is fog. Cold water off the Pacific pulls a blanket of fog and cool air inland through gaps in the coastal hills, especially along the Russian River. This natural air-conditioning slows ripening, letting grapes hold on to fresh acidity, which is ideal for delicate Pinot Noir and Chardonnay. Further inland, beyond the fog’s reach, the valleys are sunnier and warmer, ripening Zinfandel and Cabernet to fuller, riper, bolder wines. That contrast, packed into one county, is exactly why Sonoma can do both graceful and generous so well.

Producers to know in Sonoma County

1

Williams Selyem

A benchmark for Russian River Valley Pinot Noir since the 1980s, small-batch and largely allocation-only.

2

Merry Edwards

Russian River estate celebrated for consistently fine Pinot Noir, and for its distinctive Sauvignon Blanc.

3

Gary Farrell

Hilltop Russian River winery known for single-vineyard Pinot Noir and Chardonnay.

4

Dry Creek Vineyard

Long-standing family-owned Dry Creek Valley estate, a familiar name for Zinfandel and Fume Blanc.

5

Mauritson

Sixth-generation Dry Creek Valley family making Zinfandel from old, high-elevation Rockpile vineyards.

Food pairing. Pinot Noir with salmon, duck and mushroom dishes; Zinfandel with barbecue, burgers and spiced sausage; Cabernet Sauvignon with grilled steak.

FAQ

Questions & answers

How does Sonoma wine taste?

It varies with the site. From cool coastal areas, Pinot Noir is silky and bright and Chardonnay is crisp to creamy. From warmer inland valleys, Zinfandel is brambly and peppery and Cabernet Sauvignon is full and ripe. In general Sonoma leans a little more relaxed and fruit-forward than Napa.

Which wineries is Sonoma known for?

For Russian River Pinot Noir and Chardonnay, look for Williams Selyem, Merry Edwards and Gary Farrell. For Dry Creek Valley Zinfandel, Dry Creek Vineyard and Mauritson are reliable names.

What is the difference between Sonoma and Napa?

They are neighbours, but Sonoma is larger, more varied and more affected by coastal fog, so it makes a wider range of styles, from cool-climate Pinot Noir to bold Zinfandel. Napa is warmer and more focused on rich Cabernet Sauvignon.
