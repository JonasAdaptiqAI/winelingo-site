Napa Valley Wine Region

California’s flagship region for bold Cabernet.

Rich, full-bodied Cabernet Sauvignon bursting with ripe blackcurrant, blackberry and dark cherry, wrapped in plush, velvety tannins and generous oak that adds vanilla, cedar, mocha and sweet spice. Bold, warm and polished, with real depth on the finish.

Five things to know about Napa Valley

  1. Rutherford on a label must be followed by Napa Valley. A 1989 California law, in force for everything bottled since 1 January 1990, obliges any wine naming one of Napa’s sub-appellations to print Napa Valley alongside it, just as prominently. The small print is the specific bit.
  2. It is far smaller than its fame suggests. About 47,000 acres (19,000 hectares) are planted, inside an appellation of some 225,000 acres. Sonoma County next door farms roughly 60,000 acres of vines and attracts a fraction of the attention.
  3. A 1968 zoning ordinance is why it is still farmland. Napa County created America’s first agricultural preserve that April, ring-fencing 23,000 acres for vines and later widening it to about 32,000 with a 40-acre minimum lot size. Without it the valley floor would be housing.
  4. Napa was California’s first appellation and only the country’s second. The Napa Valley AVA was approved on 28 January 1981, behind Augusta, Missouri. Seventeen nested sub-AVAs followed, from Carneros on the cool southern edge to Howell Mountain up in the hills.
  5. One of Napa’s sub-regions is not entirely Napa. Los Carneros straddles the county line into Sonoma, cooled by fog off San Pablo Bay. It is the one corner of the valley better known for Pinot Noir, Chardonnay and sparkling wine than for Cabernet.

How Napa Valley wine tastes

Napa’s calling card is Cabernet Sauvignon, and the classic Napa style is ripe, powerful and plush. Expect deep, dark fruit, cassis (blackcurrant), blackberry and dark cherry, often with a note of dried herb, tobacco or graphite in the background.

Because the fruit ripens so fully in the warm California sun, the tannins tend to feel soft and velvety rather than hard, and the wines are full-bodied with a warming touch of alcohol. Time in oak barrels adds layers of vanilla, cedar, sweet spice, and sometimes mocha or espresso.

Napa also makes excellent Chardonnay, typically riper and rounder than European versions, with baked apple, melon and, where oak is used, a creamy, buttery texture. You’ll also find Merlot, Sauvignon Blanc and old-vine Zinfandel across the valley.

Signature styles & what to look for

The icon is a single powerful Cabernet Sauvignon, or a Bordeaux-style blend adding Merlot, Cabernet Franc and Petit Verdot for extra complexity.

Unlike Rioja or Barolo, Napa has no legally defined ageing ladder. Words like Reserve appear on labels but aren’t officially regulated, so they signal a producer’s ambition rather than a fixed rule. What really shapes the wine is where the grapes grew.

That’s why it pays to learn the valley’s sub-regions (AVAs). Valley-floor districts like Rutherford and Oakville are famous for perfumed, structured Cabernet (the local dusty texture is nicknamed Rutherford dust), while hillside AVAs such as Howell Mountain, Mount Veeder and Spring Mountain give firmer, more concentrated wines. Stags Leap District is prized for supple elegance. Single-vineyard bottlings and the small-production cult wines sit at the top of the tree.

The grapes & the land

Cabernet Sauvignon is king, with Chardonnay the leading white, plus Merlot, Cabernet Franc, Sauvignon Blanc and Zinfandel in support.

Napa enjoys a warm Mediterranean climate: dry, sunny summers that ripen grapes fully and reliably. Crucially, cool air and morning fog drawn up from San Pablo Bay to the south moderate the heat, especially at the southern end of the valley, helping the grapes keep some freshness.

The valley is geologically diverse, with dozens of different soil types across its flat floor and surrounding mountain slopes. This mix of warmth, fog and varied soils is what lets Napa make Cabernet that is ripe and generous yet still structured. The region announced itself to the world at the 1976 Judgement of Paris, when Napa wines beat top French bottles in a blind tasting.

Producers to know in Napa Valley

1

Beaulieu Vineyard

One of Napa’s longest continuously operating wineries, historic home of the Georges de Latour Private Reserve Cabernet.

2

Inglenook

Historic Rutherford estate, a pioneer of French-style Napa Cabernet, now owned by the Coppola family.

3

Stag’s Leap Wine Cellars

The estate whose 1973 Cabernet won the red-wine flight at the 1976 Judgement of Paris.

4

Screaming Eagle

Tiny-production Oakville estate and one of Napa’s most sought-after cult Cabernets.

5

Dalla Valle Vineyards

Oakville hillside estate known for deeply structured Cabernet Sauvignon and Cabernet Franc.

Food pairing. A natural partner for a good steak, especially a well-marbled ribeye; also excellent with roast lamb, braised short ribs and aged hard cheeses that can stand up to its richness.

FAQ

Questions & answers

How does Napa Valley wine taste?

The classic Napa Cabernet is full-bodied and ripe, with rich blackcurrant and blackberry fruit, soft velvety tannins and generous oak notes of vanilla, cedar and mocha. Bold and polished rather than lean.

Which wineries is Napa Valley known for?

Historic names include Beaulieu Vineyard, Inglenook and Stag’s Leap Wine Cellars, while Screaming Eagle and Dalla Valle are among the celebrated modern estates.

Why is Napa Cabernet so expensive?

Land in Napa is some of the priciest vineyard land in the world, yields are kept low for concentration, and the region’s global reputation, sealed by the 1976 Judgement of Paris, all push prices up. There are, though, more affordable bottles from larger producers.
