Puglia Wine

Puglia, the ‘heel’ of Italy’s boot, is a sun-baked southern region known for rich, ripe, easy-drinking reds at friendly prices. Its signature grapes deliver bold, fruity, full-bodied wines with a soft, generous texture: jammy, high-alcohol Primitivo (the same grape as California’s Zinfandel), plush Negroamaro, and the firmer, more tannic Nero di Troia.

How Puglia wine tastes

Puglia (the long, flat ‘heel’ of Italy’s boot) makes some of the country’s most crowd-pleasing reds. Expect deep, almost inky colours and a mouthful of sweet, ripe fruit: think blackberry jam, stewed plum, black cherry and dried fig. There’s often a warm, almost liqueur-like ripeness to the fruit because the grapes bask in so much sun.

What makes these wines so approachable is their texture. The tannins (the grippy, drying compounds in red wine) are soft and plush rather than firm, and the acidity is gentle, so the wine feels round and velvety. Alcohol tends to run high, which adds to that warming, generous impression.

Alongside the fruit you’ll often catch notes of sweet baking spice, cocoa, dried Mediterranean herbs and a touch of vanilla or smoke from oak ageing. Even the more serious bottlings keep that friendly, sun-soaked character.

Signature styles & what to look for

Primitivo di Manduria is the region’s flagship — bold, dark and high in alcohol, from the same grape known as Zinfandel in California. Look for the Riserva for a richer, oak-aged version, and note the rare sweet Primitivo di Manduria Dolce Naturale (Puglia’s only DOCG, its top quality tier), a lusciously sweet red.

Salice Salentino is the classic Negroamaro-based red from the Salento peninsula: plush, savoury and often gently earthy. Castel del Monte in the north is the home of the firmer, more structured Nero di Troia. Puglia is also a serious source of dry rosato (rosé), most famously Leone de Castris’s Five Roses, the first Italian rosé ever bottled.

On a label, DOC or DOCG signals a defined origin and stricter rules; the broad ‘Puglia IGT’ covers easy, everyday bottles that are still often lovely value.

The grapes & the land

Three dark grapes define the region. Primitivo ripens early and to very high sugar, giving those jammy, high-alcohol wines. Negroamaro (its name hints at ‘black’ and ‘bitter’) brings a plush body with a savoury, slightly bittersweet edge. Nero di Troia is the most tannic and structured of the trio, capable of more age-worthy, ageable reds.

The land is hot, flat and relentlessly sunny, with red iron-rich soils. Many of the best vines are old, gnarled bush vines trained low in the traditional alberello (little tree) style, which suits the heat. Crucially, Puglia is a narrow strip flanked by two seas — the Adriatic and the Ionian — and their cooling breezes lend a touch of freshness that stops the wines from tasting merely heavy.

Producers to know in Puglia

1

Leone de Castris

Historic Salento estate dating to 1665; created Five Roses, the first bottled Italian rosé.

2

Produttori di Manduria

The region’s oldest cooperative (1932), a benchmark for classic Primitivo di Manduria.

3

Tormaresca

The Antinori family’s Puglian estate, with wines from both Castel del Monte and Salento.

4

Gianfranco Fino

Small grower celebrated for intense old-vine Primitivo, notably the bottling named Es.

5

Rivera

Long-established Castel del Monte producer known for structured Nero di Troia.

Food pairing. Made for hearty, rustic fare: orecchiette with a rich meat or sausage ragù, grilled lamb, barbecued meats and aged hard cheeses. The soft, ripe fruit also flatters tomato-based dishes beautifully.

FAQ

Questions & answers

How does Puglia wine taste?

Rich, ripe and full-bodied, with jammy dark fruit, warming alcohol and soft, easy-going tannins. It’s fruit-forward and smooth rather than firm or austere — one of the most approachable red styles in Italy.

Which wineries is Puglia known for?

Well-established names include Leone de Castris (dating to 1665), the Produttori di Manduria cooperative, Tormaresca (owned by the Antinori family), Gianfranco Fino for old-vine Primitivo, and Rivera in Castel del Monte.

Is Primitivo the same as Zinfandel?

Yes — Primitivo and California’s Zinfandel are the same grape variety (both are the Croatian Tribidrag). Puglian Primitivo tends to taste riper, jammier and more Mediterranean in character.
