Sardinia Wine Region

A large Mediterranean island with a wine identity shaped as much by Spain as by Italy, known for bold, warming reds from Cannonau (the local name for Grenache) and zesty, sea-salt-tinged Vermentino whites.

Bold, warming reds from Cannonau (Grenache), full of ripe red and dark berries, dried herbs and a peppery, sometimes wild-scrubland edge, with soft tannins and generous alcohol. The Vermentino whites are fresh and zesty, with citrus, orchard fruit and a distinct salty, sea-breeze tang.

Five things to know about Sardinia

  1. The island was Spanish for four centuries. Sardinia belonged to the Crown of Aragon and then Spain from 1323 until 1708. That is why Cannonau is Grenache and Carignano is Carignan, and why Catalan is still spoken in Alghero today.
  2. Sardinia disputes who had Grenache first. DNA makes Cannonau and Spanish Garnacha the same variety, and ampelographers place its home in Aragón. Sardinian archaeologists point to charred grape pips from Nuragic sites dated to around 1200 BC and argue the traffic ran the other way.
  3. Only one Sardinian wine holds a DOCG. Vermentino di Gallura, a DOC from 1975 and DOCG since 1996, covers 1,416 hectares of granite country in the north-east and must be at least 95% Vermentino — stricter than most Italian appellations demand.
  4. Cannonau can put a sub-zone on the label. The DOC dates from 1972 and asks for 85% Cannonau, rising to 90% for Classico. Its three sub-zones are Oliena, also called Nepente di Oliena, plus Jerzu and Capo Ferrato.
  5. Vermentino, Pigato and Favorita are the same vine. DNA typing matched Liguria’s Pigato and Piedmont’s Favorita to Sardinia’s Vermentino. Three regions, three names, three quite different wines — the differences come from site and handling, not from genetics.

How Sardinia wine tastes

Sardinia’s wine identity owes as much to Spain as to Italy, and its flagship red, Cannonau, is simply the local name for Grenache. These reds are warm and generous: ripe strawberry, cherry and darker berry fruit, a lift of dried Mediterranean herbs and garrigue, a touch of black pepper, and soft, rounded tannins. They tend to be full-bodied and high in alcohol, but rarely heavy or harsh.

The island’s leading white, Vermentino, is the fresh counterpoint: bright citrus and pear, a whisper of almond and herbs, and a briny, sea-salt tang that seems to come straight off the coast. It is crisp and easy to love, with a slightly bitter, savoury twist on the finish.

What ties the two together is that saline, sun-baked Mediterranean character. Even everyday Sardinian wines taste ripe, substantial and sunny.

Signature styles & what to look for

The two names to know are Cannonau di Sardegna (the Grenache-based red) and Vermentino, which comes in two tiers: the island-wide Vermentino di Sardegna, and the more prestigious Vermentino di Gallura from the granite hills of the north-east, Sardinia’s only DOCG and often the most structured and mineral.

Cannonau ranges from bright, juicy everyday reds to serious, barrel-aged bottlings; a Riserva must age longer before release and tends to be deeper and more savoury. Old bush vines and low yields concentrate the best examples.

Beyond these, look out for Carignano del Sulcis, a dark, plush red from old Carignan vines in the south-west, and the island’s other native grapes such as Monica (soft, easy reds) and Nuragus (light, everyday whites). For something rare, the oxidative, sherry-like Vernaccia di Oristano is a local speciality.

The grapes & the land

The key grapes are Grenache (Cannonau) and Vermentino, with Carignan, Monica and Nuragus also widely grown. Sardinia is a large, sun-soaked Mediterranean island, and abundant sunshine and heat push the grapes to full, generous ripeness, giving the reds their richness and warmth.

Constant sea breezes and sandy coastal soils keep the Vermentino fresh and lend both colours their distinctive salty, briny edge, while inland granite and schist add structure and minerality. Old, low-yielding bush vines, many of them decades old, concentrate flavour further. It is this combination of strong sun, sea air and hardy old vines that makes even the island’s simple wines taste ripe and full of character.

Producers to know in Sardinia

1

Argiolas

Leading estate at Serdiana, celebrated for its Turriga blend of old-vine Cannonau and Carignano.

2

Sella & Mosca

One of Sardinia’s largest and most historic estates, near Alghero, making a wide range of Vermentino and Cannonau.

3

Capichera

Gallura estate famous for premium, age-worthy Vermentino that put Sardinian white wine on the map.

4

Cantina Santadi

Sulcis cooperative widely regarded as Italy’s finest producer of Carignano del Sulcis, notably its Terre Brune.

5

Siddura

Gallura estate in the granite hills praised for polished Vermentino and Cannonau.

Food pairing. Cannonau loves roast and grilled meats, lamb, wild boar, hearty pasta and aged pecorino, the island’s classic sheep’s cheese. Vermentino is a natural with seafood, bottarga (cured fish roe), shellfish pasta and grilled fish.

FAQ

Questions & answers

How does Sardinian wine taste?

The Cannonau reds are warm and full-bodied, with ripe berry fruit, dried herbs and a peppery edge and soft tannins. The Vermentino whites are fresh and zesty, with citrus, a hint of almond and a salty, sea-breeze tang.

Which wineries is Sardinia known for?

Established producers include Argiolas, Sella & Mosca, Capichera, Cantina Santadi and Siddura.

Is Cannonau the same as Grenache?

Yes. Cannonau is simply the Sardinian name for Grenache, the same grape grown widely in Spain and southern France. On the island it makes warming, generous, herb-scented reds.
