Alsace Wine Region

Alsace is France’s premier region for aromatic, mostly dry white wines, famous for varietally labeled bottlings of Riesling, Gewurztraminer, and Pinot Gris in tall flute bottles.

Alsace whites are aromatic and mostly dry, with pure, vivid fruit and bright acidity: floral, citrus-and-orchard-fruit Riesling; exotic, rose-and-lychee-scented Gewurztraminer; and richer, spicy Pinot Gris. Little to no oak keeps the flavours clean and focused.

Five things to know about Alsace

  1. The Grand Crus went red in 2022. Alsace Grand Cru was a white-wine club for decades. Then Pinot Noir from Hengst and Kirchberg de Barr earned Grand Cru status from the 2022 vintage, with Vorbourg added in July 2024. Three red sites, and counting.
  2. The sweetness guessing game is over. From the 2021 harvest, every still Alsace wine must show where it sits on a four-step scale from dry to sweet. Before that, a Pinot Gris could be bone-dry or honeyed with nothing on the bottle to warn you.
  3. Grand Cru is rarer than it looks. The 51 named Grand Cru sites account for only about four per cent of what Alsace bottles. Kaefferkopf, added by decree in 2007, was the last one admitted. Nearly everything else you meet is plain Alsace AOC.
  4. The bottle shape is the law. A decree of 1955 reserved the tall flûte d’Alsace for the region’s wines, and a law of 5 July 1972 forced bottling inside Bas-Rhin or Haut-Rhin. That slim green bottle is not a house style, it is a legal condition of the name.
  5. It changed country four times in 75 years. Alsace passed to Germany in 1871, back to France in 1918, to Germany again in 1940 and back in 1945. The German legacy shows on the label: Alsace names the grape, where the rest of France names the place.

How Alsace wine tastes

Alsace is all about aromatic white wine that leaps out of the glass. The wines are usually unoaked, fermented in neutral vessels so nothing gets between you and the pure flavour of the grape, which is exactly the point here.

The style changes a lot by grape. Riesling is the region’s noble benchmark: dry, taut and mineral, with lime, green apple and a bracing streak of acidity that keeps it fresh and precise. Gewurztraminer is the showstopper, richly perfumed with rose petals, lychee and exotic spice, fuller-bodied and only just dry. Pinot Gris sits in between, broad and textured, with smoky, honeyed orchard-fruit richness.

One thing worth knowing: not every Alsace wine is bone dry. Sweetness varies from producer to producer and isn’t always stated on the label, so a Gewurztraminer or Pinot Gris can taste off-dry. When in doubt, ask, or lean on Riesling, which is almost always dry.

Signature styles & what to look for

Alsace makes it easy in one way: unusually for France, wines are labelled by grape variety, so the bottle simply says Riesling, Gewurztraminer, Pinot Gris and so on.

The quality ladder is worth learning. Standard Alsace AOC covers everyday bottlings. Alsace Grand Cru comes from 51 named top vineyards and marks the most prestigious, site-expressive wines. For sweeter styles made from late-picked grapes, look for Vendanges Tardives (late harvest, richer and often off-dry to sweet) and the rarer, lusciously sweet Sélection de Grains Nobles (from nobly-rotted grapes). For fizz, Crémant d’Alsace is the region’s traditional-method sparkling wine and excellent value.

The grapes & the land

The stars are the so-called noble grapes: Riesling, Gewurztraminer, Pinot Gris and Muscat, joined by the gentler, everyday Pinot Blanc and the region’s one significant red, Pinot Noir.

Tucked in the rain shadow of the Vosges mountains, Alsace is one of France’s driest and sunniest regions. The mountains block the rain clouds, so grapes ripen fully while cool nights preserve that hallmark acidity and perfume. The other secret is the soil: an extraordinary patchwork of granite, limestone, schist, sandstone and clay, which is why neighbouring grand cru vineyards can taste so different from one another.

Producers to know in Alsace

1

Trimbach

Historic Ribeauvillé house (est. 1626) renowned for bone-dry, long-lived Riesling, including the legendary Clos Sainte Hune.

2

Hugel & Fils

Famous family house in Riquewihr, making a broad, dependable range and a pioneer of late-harvest styles.

3

Domaine Zind-Humbrecht

Biodynamic estate led by France’s first Master of Wine, known for concentrated, powerful single-vineyard wines.

4

Domaine Weinbach

Revered Kaysersberg estate producing elegant, precise Grand Cru Riesling and Gewurztraminer.

5

Léon Beyer

Long-established Eguisheim house known for a firmly dry, food-friendly house style.

6

Domaine Marcel Deiss

Biodynamic grower championing terroir, sometimes co-planting several grapes in a single vineyard.

Food pairing. Alsace whites are famously food-friendly. Dry Riesling loves seafood, trout and choucroute (the local sauerkraut-and-pork dish); aromatic Gewurztraminer is a classic match for spicy Asian food, Muenster cheese and foie gras; and Pinot Gris suits roast pork and poultry.

FAQ

Questions & answers

Is Alsace wine sweet or dry?

Most Alsace wine is dry, especially Riesling. But sweetness varies by producer and grape and isn’t always shown on the label, so Gewurztraminer and Pinot Gris can taste off-dry. Look for Vendanges Tardives or Sélection de Grains Nobles if you specifically want a sweet, late-harvest style.

How does Alsace wine taste?

Aromatic, pure and mostly dry. Riesling is crisp and minerally with lime and apple; Gewurztraminer is exotic and perfumed with rose and lychee; Pinot Gris is richer and spicier. Because the wines see little or no oak, the grape flavours come through clearly.

Which producers is Alsace known for?

Classic names include Trimbach, Hugel & Fils, Domaine Zind-Humbrecht, Domaine Weinbach, Léon Beyer and Marcel Deiss.
