How Marlborough wine tastes

Marlborough is the region that taught the world what New Zealand Sauvignon Blanc could be, and one sniff usually gives it away. Expect a loud, lifted nose of passionfruit, gooseberry, grapefruit and green capsicum, with a fresh green note often described as freshly mown grass or blackcurrant leaf.

On the palate it is dry, light-bodied and racy, with tongue-tingling acidity that makes your mouth water. There is very little or no oak here, so nothing softens or hides the pure fruit. The finish is clean and crisp rather than rich.

Because the style is so pronounced, Marlborough Sauvignon is one of the easiest wines to recognise once you have tried a couple. It is bright and refreshing rather than serious or brooding, and it is delicious young.

Signature styles & what to look for

The headline act is Sauvignon Blanc in its classic zingy, aromatic form, usually made in stainless steel to keep the fruit fresh and unoaked. This is what fills most bottles labelled simply ‘Marlborough’.

Look out too for a growing band of more textural, complex Sauvignons: some producers ferment a portion in old oak barrels or leave the wine on its lees (spent yeast) for extra weight and a subtler, less overtly fruity style. These reward a little more attention.

Marlborough also makes lovely aromatic whites beyond Sauvignon, especially crisp Riesling and Pinot Gris, along with lively sparkling wine and increasingly good Pinot Noir from the region’s clay-rich Southern Valleys.

The grapes & the land

Sauvignon Blanc is king, planted across the wide, sunny Wairau and Awatere valleys near the top of the South Island. The climate is cool and maritime, with long, bright days and notably cold nights.

That big day-to-night temperature swing is the secret. Warm days ripen the fruit’s tropical, passionfruit character, while cold nights lock in the razor-sharp acidity that keeps the wine feeling zesty and alive.

The free-draining soils, a mix of stony river gravels and silt, stress the vines just enough to concentrate flavour. The cooler, windier Awatere Valley tends to give more herbaceous, tomato-leaf and mineral notes, while the warmer Wairau leans juicier and more tropical.

Producers to know in Marlborough

1

Cloudy Bay

The label that launched Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc onto the world stage in the 1980s and remains an international benchmark.

2

Brancott Estate

Formerly Montana, the pioneer that planted Marlborough’s first modern Sauvignon Blanc vines in the 1970s.

3

Villa Maria

One of New Zealand’s largest and most awarded family-founded wineries, with a broad, reliable Marlborough range.

4

Greywacke

Boutique label of Kevin Judd, Cloudy Bay’s founding winemaker, known for textural, characterful Sauvignon.

5

Framingham

Wairau Valley estate especially respected for its Rieslings alongside classic Sauvignon Blanc.

Food pairing. A natural with goat’s cheese, fresh oysters, green salads and anything with herbs, asparagus or a squeeze of lime; also lovely with Thai and other zesty Southeast Asian dishes.

FAQ

Questions & answers

How does Marlborough wine taste?

Most Marlborough wine is Sauvignon Blanc: bright and dry with mouth-watering acidity and loud aromas of passionfruit, gooseberry, lime and freshly cut grass. It is light, crisp, unoaked and best enjoyed young and cold.

Which wineries is Marlborough known for?

Well-known Marlborough producers include Cloudy Bay, Brancott Estate (formerly Montana), Villa Maria, Greywacke and Framingham. Cloudy Bay and Brancott were central to putting the region on the map.

Is Marlborough only about Sauvignon Blanc?

Sauvignon Blanc is the star, but Marlborough also makes excellent Pinot Gris, Riesling, sparkling wine and increasingly good Pinot Noir, especially from its cooler, clay-rich Southern Valleys.
