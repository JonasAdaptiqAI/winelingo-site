Five things to know about Burgundy

  1. 1,247 named plots, listed by UNESCO. The climats of the Côte de Nuits and Côte de Beaune became a World Heritage Site on 4 July 2015. They sit on a ribbon of hillside about 60 km long, running from Dijon to south of Beaune.
  2. One vineyard, more than eighty owners. Clos de Vougeot spreads about 50 hectares across roughly 100 parcels shared by over 80 growers. Two bottles carrying that identical Grand Cru name can be worlds apart, which is why the producer matters more than the vineyard.
  3. Grand Cru is one per cent of Burgundy. About one per cent of the region’s output is Grand Cru and ten per cent Premier Cru; more than half is plain regional Bourgogne. There are 33 Grands Crus: 24 in the Côte de Nuits, 8 in the Côte de Beaune, 1 in Chablis.
  4. Villages renamed themselves after their best vineyard. Gevrey bolted Chambertin onto its name in 1847; Puligny took Montrachet in 1879, and others followed down the slope. The famous double-barrelled names of the Côte d’Or are 19th-century marketing, not ancient geography.
  5. Grand Cru labels drop the village entirely. Each of the 33 Grands Crus is its own appellation, so a Côte d’Or bottle reads simply Chambertin or Montrachet. A Premier Cru must print the village first, then Premier Cru, then the climat. The longer label is the lesser wine.

How Burgundy wine tastes

Burgundy makes two great wines, both about finesse over power. The reds, from Pinot Noir, are pale in colour, light to medium-bodied and silky in texture, with red cherry and raspberry fruit, savoury hints of mushroom, undergrowth and forest floor, and a fresh acidity that makes them wonderfully drinkable.

The whites, from Chardonnay, span a spectrum. In cool Chablis they are lean, mineral and citrussy with a flinty edge; further south they grow richer and rounder, offering orchard fruit, hazelnut and a creamy, gently oaked warmth.

What unites them is elegance and a sense of place. Burgundy prizes subtlety, so these are wines that reveal themselves gradually rather than shouting for attention.

Signature styles & what to look for

Burgundy’s quality ladder is all about where the grapes grew. From the bottom up: broad regional wines (labelled Bourgogne), then village wines named after a commune, then Premier Cru from a superior named vineyard, and at the top Grand Cru, the greatest individual sites. The higher the rung, the more precise and ageworthy the wine.

Geography helps too. The Côte de Nuits is famed for its reds, the Côte de Beaune for whites and some fine reds, Chablis for steely Chardonnay, and the Côte Chalonnaise and Mâconnais for friendlier, better-value bottles.

Because a single vineyard is often split among many growers, the producer’s name matters as much as the vineyard. It pays to learn a few reliable names.

The grapes & the land

Burgundy is essentially two grapes: Pinot Noir for the reds and Chardonnay for the whites. Both are wonderfully transparent to the ground they grow in, which is why Burgundy obsesses over individual vineyards.

The climate is cool continental, giving grapes a long, slow ripening that preserves the high acidity and delicate, restrained fruit at the heart of the style. In cooler years the wines are lean and taut; in warmer ones, riper and rounder.

The magic ingredient is the soil: bands of limestone and marl running along the slopes of the Côte d’Or. Tiny changes in this bedrock, aspect and drainage give neighbouring plots noticeably different characters, the very idea the French call terroir.

Producers to know in Burgundy

1

Domaine de la Romanée-Conti

Burgundy’s most prestigious estate, producing only Grand Cru wines from tiny, legendary vineyards.

2

Louis Jadot

Major, respected négociant-house drawing on vineyards across the region for both reds and whites.

3

Domaine Faiveley

Historic family estate based in Nuits-Saint-Georges, established in 1825, with holdings across the Côte d’Or.

4

Bouchard Père & Fils

Long-established Beaune house and vineyard owner making a broad range of red and white Burgundy.

5

Domaine Leflaive

Renowned Puligny-Montrachet estate celebrated for benchmark white Burgundy from top Chardonnay sites.

Food pairing. Red Burgundy is glorious with roast chicken, duck, mushroom dishes and beef bourguignon. White Burgundy suits fish, shellfish, poultry and creamy sauces, with richer examples standing up to lobster or roast chicken.

FAQ

Questions & answers

How does Burgundy wine taste?

Red Burgundy is pale, silky and perfumed, with red cherry and raspberry fruit, earthy notes and fresh acidity. White Burgundy ranges from crisp and mineral to rich, nutty and creamy, depending on where it is grown.

Which producers is Burgundy known for?

Famous names include Domaine de la Romanée-Conti, Louis Jadot, Domaine Faiveley, Bouchard Père & Fils and, for white wine, Domaine Leflaive.

What do Premier Cru and Grand Cru mean?

They are the top two tiers of Burgundy’s vineyard-based classification. Premier Cru comes from a superior named vineyard, while Grand Cru sits above it, denoting the very greatest individual sites.
