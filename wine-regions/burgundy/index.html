Burgundy Wine: Grapes, Taste, Regions & Food Pairing Guide

Two grapes, endless nuance — the classic home of Pinot Noir and Chardonnay, explained simply.

Country
France (eastern France, south of Paris)
Key grapes
Pinot Noir (red), Chardonnay (white)
Signature styles
Elegant, earthy red Pinot Noir; mineral, refined white Chardonnay
Climate
Cool continental — warm summers, cold winters
Classic for
Terroir-driven wines where the vineyard site is the star
Look for on the label
Bourgogne, a village name, or Premier/Grand Cru

Burgundy (Bourgogne in French) is one of the most loved and most talked-about wine regions in the world — and it’s built on a beautifully simple idea. For the most part, just two grapes do the work: Pinot Noir for the reds and Chardonnay for the whites. Everything else is about where those grapes grow.

That’s the key to understanding Burgundy wine. Here, the exact patch of ground — the slope, the soil, the sunlight — matters more than the grape or the producer’s name on the label. Once that clicks, the region gets a lot less intimidating. This guide walks you through the taste, the regions, the food pairings and how to read a Burgundy label, all in plain language. No wrong answers here.

Where is Burgundy, and what’s the climate like?

Burgundy sits in eastern France, running roughly north to south below the city of Dijon, well south of Paris. It’s a long, narrow region — more a string of hillsides than one big block of land.

The climate is cool continental. That means warm (not hot) summers, genuinely cold winters, and a real risk of frost and rain at the wrong moment. This cool climate is a big part of Burgundy’s signature: grapes ripen slowly and hold onto their fresh acidity, which gives the wines their lively, food-friendly feel rather than a heavy, jammy one.

Because it’s cool, vintages — the year on the bottle — can vary more than in warmer places. Some years are riper and rounder, others leaner and crisper. That variation is part of the fun, not a flaw.

The key grapes and signature styles

Burgundy keeps things focused. Two grapes account for the vast majority of what’s made.

Pinot Noir is the red. It’s a light- to medium-bodied grape with soft tannins (the drying, grippy part of red wine) and bright acidity. Expect flavours of red cherry, raspberry and strawberry, often with an earthy, savoury, almost forest-floor note as the wines age. These are elegant, gentle reds — refreshment and finesse over power.

Chardonnay is the white. Here it’s the opposite of the big, buttery Chardonnay some people expect. Burgundy Chardonnay tends to be more restrained: think green apple, citrus, pear and a stony, mineral edge. Fuller versions (especially from the south) can show richer notes of hazelnut and a creamy texture, often from time in oak barrels.

There are two smaller supporting acts worth knowing: Aligoté, a crisp, zippy white grape, and Gamay, a juicy red grape more famous just to the south in Beaujolais.

  • Pinot Noir (red): light to medium body, soft tannins, red-berry and earthy flavours
  • Chardonnay (white): from crisp and mineral to rich and creamy, rarely heavy-handed
  • Aligoté: a lighter, tangy everyday white
  • Gamay: bright, fruity red, mostly found in Beaujolais to the south

What makes Burgundy wines distinctive?

The magic word in Burgundy is terroir — the idea that a wine tastes of the specific place it grew. Two vineyards a short walk apart, planted with the same grape by neighbouring growers, can produce noticeably different wines. Burgundy has spent centuries mapping exactly which slopes and soils give which character.

This is why Burgundy is organised around vineyards rather than brands. The land is divided into named plots, and the best of them have been recognised for generations. When people get excited about Burgundy, they’re usually excited about this sense of place — the way a wine can taste of a single hillside.

In the glass, the through-line is elegance and balance. Burgundy rarely tries to overwhelm you. The reds are perfumed and savoury; the whites are precise and mineral. Both are built to sit alongside food rather than shout over it. It’s also worth being honest about one thing: because top sites are small and demand is high, the most famous Burgundies can be expensive. The good news is there’s plenty of lovely, affordable Burgundy lower down the ladder — which brings us to the labels.

How to read a Burgundy label (beginner-friendly)

Burgundy labels can look cryptic because they usually name the place, not the grape. But there’s a simple ladder of quality, and once you know the four rungs, you can navigate almost any bottle. As a rule of thumb, the more specific the place named, the higher up the ladder — and usually the higher the price.

From broadest to most specific, the four levels below take you from friendly everyday bottles up to the rare, celebrated ones.

  • Regional (e.g. ‘Bourgogne’): the entry level, made from grapes across the wider region. Great everyday drinking and the friendliest on the wallet.
  • Village (e.g. ‘Gevrey-Chambertin’, ‘Meursault’): grapes from around one named village, with more local character.
  • Premier Cru (say ‘pruh-mee-ay cru’, shown as ‘1er Cru’): a step up, from a specific vineyard officially recognised as excellent within a village.
  • Grand Cru: the top rung — a handful of historic, individually named vineyards. Rare, celebrated and priced accordingly.

The main areas within Burgundy

Burgundy is a chain of sub-regions, each with its own personality. You don’t need to memorise them, but knowing the big names helps you shop.

The Côte d’Or (‘golden slope’) is the celebrated heart, split into two halves: the Côte de Nuits in the north, famous for its structured, age-worthy Pinot Noir reds, and the Côte de Beaune in the south, known for both fine reds and some of the world’s most admired white Chardonnays.

To the north, Chablis is a cooler outpost making crisp, mineral, unoaked Chardonnay — a great introduction to the leaner Burgundy style. Heading south, the Côte Chalonnaise and the Mâconnais offer excellent value in both colours, with the Mâconnais leaning towards rounder, sunnier whites. Further south still sits Beaujolais, technically its own thing, home to the juicy Gamay grape.

  • Chablis: crisp, mineral, usually unoaked white Chardonnay
  • Côte de Nuits: the star region for structured red Pinot Noir
  • Côte de Beaune: prized whites and elegant reds
  • Côte Chalonnaise & Mâconnais: friendlier-priced reds and whites
  • Beaujolais: bright, fruity Gamay reds just to the south

A few Burgundy wines and styles to try

The best way in is to start affordable and specific, then climb the ladder as your curiosity grows. Here are some approachable starting points that show what the region does well.

There’s no need to spend a fortune to enjoy Burgundy. A well-made village or regional bottle can teach you more than a rare, expensive one you’re nervous to open. Taste, take a mental note of what you like, and follow it from there.

  • A basic ‘Bourgogne Rouge’ — the easiest, friendliest introduction to Burgundy Pinot Noir
  • A bottle of Chablis — to taste crisp, mineral, unoaked Chardonnay
  • A village red from the Côte de Nuits — to meet more serious, savoury Pinot Noir
  • A white from the Côte de Beaune (such as a Mâcon or a village wine) — for richer, rounder Chardonnay
  • A glass of Beaujolais nearby — juicy Gamay that makes a fun, low-stakes comparison

Enjoying Burgundy with food

Burgundy and food are old friends — these wines were, in a sense, built for the table. The reds’ bright acidity and gentle tannins make Pinot Noir wonderfully flexible: it flatters roast chicken, mushroom dishes, salmon and slow-cooked beef (beef bourguignon is a Burgundian classic for a reason).

The whites are just as adaptable. Crisp Chablis loves oysters, seafood and goat’s cheese, while richer Côte de Beaune whites suit creamy sauces, roast poultry and fuller fish. A gentle serving tip: serve the reds slightly cool rather than room-temperature, and don’t over-chill the whites — you’ll taste more.

As always, drink what you enjoy and in moderation. The ‘right’ pairing is simply the one that makes your meal more fun.

FAQ

Questions & answers

What grapes are used in Burgundy wine?

Overwhelmingly two: Pinot Noir for the reds and Chardonnay for the whites. Aligote (a crisp white) and Gamay (a fruity red, mostly in Beaujolais) play smaller supporting roles.

What does Burgundy wine taste like?

Red Burgundy (Pinot Noir) is light to medium-bodied with red-berry fruit, soft tannins and an earthy, savoury edge. White Burgundy (Chardonnay) ranges from crisp, mineral and citrusy to richer and creamier, but rarely heavy.

Is Burgundy always expensive?

No. The most famous vineyards are rare and pricey, but entry-level ‘Bourgogne’ bottles and many village wines are very affordable and a great way to get to know the region.

How do I read a Burgundy label?

Look for the place name and its rung on the quality ladder: regional (Bourgogne), village, Premier Cru (1er Cru), then Grand Cru at the top. The more specific the named vineyard, the higher up the scale.

What’s the difference between Chablis and other white Burgundy?

Chablis is a cool, northern part of Burgundy making crisp, mineral, usually unoaked Chardonnay. Whites from further south, like the Cote de Beaune, tend to be rounder and sometimes richer.
