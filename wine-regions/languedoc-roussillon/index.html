Languedoc-Roussillon Wine Region

This sprawling southern region is France’s largest wine area and its great-value engine room, turning out everything from juicy everyday reds to serious, named-vineyard bottlings (the French appellation system labels wine by its specific place of origin, which usually signals higher quality). It is best known for warm, full-bodied Mediterranean reds, and increasingly good rosés and whites, and it actually makes more rosé by volume than famous Provence to its east.

Warm, generous Mediterranean reds full of ripe dark berries, soft tannins and a savoury, herbal scrubland note the French call garrigue. Expect full body and a touch of spice, plus increasingly fresh rosés and whites, especially the fine, crisp examples from cooler coastal and hillside spots.

Five things to know about Languedoc-Roussillon

  1. Sparkling wine was written down here first, not in Champagne. Benedictine monks at the Abbey of Saint-Hilaire near Limoux recorded their fizzing Blanquette in 1531, more than a century before Dom Pérignon was born. Blanquette de Limoux has been an appellation since 1938.
  2. It is the biggest vineyard on earth. Languedoc-Roussillon is the single largest wine-producing region in the world, responsible for more than a third of all French wine. In 2001 it made more wine than the whole of the United States.
  3. The largest protest of the French Third Republic was about wine. On 9 June 1907 between 600,000 and 800,000 growers packed Montpellier behind Marcelin Albert, furious at collapsing prices and adulterated wine. Clemenceau answered with 33,000 troops, and the clashes that followed left seven dead.
  4. Roussillon is Catalan, not Languedocien. It only became French under the Treaty of the Pyrenees in 1659, and it still feels like a separate country. It makes France’s great fortified sweet wines: Banyuls, Maury and Rivesaltes.
  5. The everyday bottles name the grape, not the place. Pays d’Oc IGP was built to put Merlot, Chardonnay or Syrah on the front label, the opposite of how French appellations work, and it is why so much affordable French varietal wine comes from here.

How Languedoc-Roussillon wine tastes

The classic style here is a warm, full-bodied red. Grenache, Syrah, Carignan and Mourvèdre blend together into wines with ripe blackberry and plum, soft, easy tannins (the drying, grippy compound in red wine) and a bit more warmth and alcohol than reds from cooler parts of France.

What makes these reds distinctive is a savoury, wild note the locals call garrigue, after the scrubby Mediterranean hillsides of thyme, rosemary and lavender. It shows up as a herbal, peppery, almost dusty edge that sits alongside the fruit and stops the wine feeling merely sweet or heavy.

There is more than reds, though. The region makes a huge amount of rosé, in fact more by volume than famous Provence next door, plus a growing number of bright, herb-tinged whites and, in Roussillon, some remarkable sweet fortified wines. Prices run from honest everyday bottles to serious, ambitious wines from named hillside sites.

Signature styles & what to look for

The headline is the Languedoc red blend, led by Grenache and Syrah. For a step up in seriousness, look for the names of specific inland appellations, an appellation being an official, named growing area whose rules usually signal higher quality. Well-regarded ones include Corbières, Minervois, Faugères, Saint-Chinian and the cooler, elegant Pic Saint-Loup.

Roussillon, the warmer zone tucked against the Spanish border, is known for concentrated old-vine reds and especially for Vin Doux Naturel, a traditional sweet wine (from Maury, Rivesaltes and Banyuls) where fermentation is stopped with grape spirit to keep natural sweetness. These range from fresh and grapey to rich, nutty and tawny.

Much of the everyday wine carries the broad Pays d’Oc label, a reliable source of good-value varietal bottlings (wine named after a single grape, such as Syrah or Chardonnay).

The grapes & the land

This is France’s largest wine region, sweeping along the southern coast from the Rhône towards Spain. The climate is hot, dry and very sunny, and that is why the reds ripen so fully and taste so generous, with ripe fruit and rounder tannins.

The main red grapes are Mediterranean classics: Grenache for warmth and red fruit, Syrah for colour and pepper, Mourvèdre for structure, and old Carignan and Cinsault for backbone and freshness. Whites lean on Grenache Blanc, Roussanne, Marsanne and Picpoul.

The land is more varied than its reputation suggests. Sea breezes cool the coastal vineyards, while inland the ground rises into rocky, schist and limestone hillsides where cooler nights and poorer soils give more structured, age-worthy wines. That mix of heat, altitude and stony ground explains the leap from cheerful everyday reds to genuinely fine bottlings.

Map of Languedoc-Roussillon’s wine appellations

The region is easiest to picture as a long crescent of coast with the vineyards stacked up behind it: the sea in front, the Pyrenees to the south-west, the Montagne Noire and the Cévennes at your back. The map below is schematic. It shows where each appellation sits relative to the others and to the five towns you will see on labels and road signs, not exact boundaries.

Walk it from west to east. Start at the Spanish border, where Banyuls and Collioure share the same terraced vineyards tumbling into the sea: Banyuls is the sweet fortified wine, Collioure the dry red, rosé and white from the same slopes. Behind them lies Roussillon proper, Catalan in spirit, with Perpignan at its centre, Rivesaltes just to the north and Maury up the Agly valley inland, both famous for vin doux naturel and, these days, for serious dry reds from old Grenache and Carignan. Head north up the coast and you meet Fitou, the Languedoc’s oldest red appellation, tucked between the lagoons and the hills, and behind it the great sprawl of Corbières, which fills the wild country south-west of Narbonne. At Narbonne itself the limestone massif of La Clape pushes into the sea, giving rich reds and some of the region’s best whites. Turn inland along the Aude towards Carcassonne and the hills to the north are Minervois; west of the city, on the cooler edge where Atlantic weather starts to win, sit Cabardès and Malepère, the only appellations here that blend Bordeaux grapes with Mediterranean ones; and south of it, in the Pyrenean foothills, Limoux makes the sparkling Blanquette and Crémant, plus fine oak-aged Chardonnay. Back on the coast road east of Narbonne, the schist hills north-west of Béziers hold Saint-Chinian and Faugères, two of the most characterful red appellations in the south. Past Béziers the flat land by the Étang de Thau, the oyster lagoon at Sète, grows Picpoul de Pinet, the crisp white built for the shellfish, with the sweet Muscat de Frontignan next door. Finally, around Montpellier, the vineyards climb again: the garrigue-covered hills of Pic Saint-Loup to the north, and the high, stony Terrasses du Larzac to the north-west, where cool nights give the Languedoc some of its most elegant reds.

Producers to know in Languedoc-Roussillon

1

Gérard Bertrand

Large biodynamic producer based at Château l’Hospitalet, making wines across many Languedoc-Roussillon appellations.

2

Mas de Daumas Gassac

Pioneering estate at Aniane, founded in the 1970s and often described as a grand cru of the Languedoc.

3

Domaine Gauby

Roussillon estate near Calce, farmed organically and biodynamically, credited with raising the region’s fine-wine reputation.

4

Domaine de l’Hortus

One of the leading estates in cooler Pic Saint-Loup, known for organic Syrah-Grenache-Mourvèdre blends.

5

Domaine Gayda

Producer near Carcassonne known for a broad range of Languedoc wines emphasising quality and value.

Food pairing. Made for hearty, rustic food: grilled lamb, sausages and duck, cassoulet, ratatouille and dishes with plenty of garlic and Mediterranean herbs. The crisp local whites and rosés suit seafood and warm-weather lunches.

FAQ

Questions & answers

How does Languedoc-Roussillon wine taste?

The classic style is a warm, full-bodied red with ripe dark fruit, soft tannins and a savoury herbal note called garrigue. The region also makes lots of rosé and increasingly good crisp whites.

Which wineries is Languedoc-Roussillon known for?

Well-established names include Gérard Bertrand, Mas de Daumas Gassac, Domaine Gauby, Domaine de l’Hortus and Domaine Gayda.

Is Languedoc-Roussillon wine good value?

Yes, it is one of France’s great value regions. Everyday bottles are affordable and honest, while named appellations like Corbières, Faugères and Pic Saint-Loup offer more serious wine without Bordeaux or Burgundy prices.

Where is Languedoc-Roussillon?

In the far south of France, along the Mediterranean coast. It runs from the Spanish border and the Pyrenees in the south-west, through Perpignan, Narbonne, Béziers and Montpellier, to the Rhône delta in the north-east, with the Massif Central and the Cévennes behind it to the north. Since 2016 it has been part of the larger administrative region of Occitanie, but as a wine region the name Languedoc-Roussillon is still the one everybody uses.
