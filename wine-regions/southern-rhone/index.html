Southern Rhône Wine Region

The Southern Rhône is the heartland of generous, Grenache-based red blends, with the prestigious Châteauneuf-du-Pape as its star and easy-drinking Côtes du Rhône as its reliable everyday workhorse. The reds are warm, full and spicy, and are typically blended from several grapes together rather than made from a single variety, with Grenache leading and Syrah and Mourvèdre adding colour and structure.

Five things to know about Southern Rhône

  1. Côtes du Rhône began as an anti-fraud stamp. In 1737 the king ordered that casks leaving the river port of Roquemaure be branded with the letters CDR so buyers could tell the real thing. The name on your everyday bottle started as a mark burned into oak.
  2. The south is the Rhône, numerically speaking. Roughly 95 per cent of the valley’s wine comes from here and about 5 per cent from the steep northern slopes. The Côtes du Rhône appellation alone spans 171 communes.
  3. Châteauneuf’s famous thirteen grapes are really eighteen. A 2009 revision listed the white, grey and black versions of varieties such as Grenache and Picpoul as separate grapes. Everyone still says thirteen. The rule book says eighteen.
  4. Gigondas could not label a white wine until 2022. It left the Côtes du Rhône-Villages and became a cru in its own right in 1971, but the appellation covered red and rosé only. White Gigondas was finally permitted in September 2022.
  5. Tavel makes rosé and nothing else. It is the only appellation in France restricted to pink wine, and it was there in the very first wave of AOCs in 1936. Tavel is deep-coloured, dry and built for food, not the pale Provençal style.

How Southern Rhône wine tastes

Southern Rhône reds are warm, generous and comforting. The backbone is Grenache, which brings ripe red and black berry fruit, a note of dried herbs (locals call this ‘garrigue’, after the wild rosemary and thyme of the scrubland), sweet baking spice, and a rounded, full body with soft tannin and a warming touch of alcohol.

Most of these wines are blends rather than single grapes: Grenache leads, while Syrah adds colour and a peppery lift and Mourvedre brings darker, meatier structure. The result feels sun-soaked and hearty without being harsh.

The style scales up and down. An everyday Côtes du Rhône is juicy, easy and made for drinking young; a Châteauneuf-du-Pape is deeper, richer and more structured, built to age for years.

Signature styles & what to look for

Think of the Southern Rhône as a pyramid. At the base is Côtes du Rhône, the reliable everyday red, great value and widely available. A step up, Côtes du Rhône-Villages (sometimes with a named village attached) offers more concentration.

Then come the individual ‘cru’ villages, each its own appellation. Châteauneuf-du-Pape is the famous star: rich, full and age-worthy, and allowed to blend up to thirteen permitted grape varieties. Gigondas and Vacqueyras are firmer, excellent-value alternatives, while Tavel is a serious, full-flavoured dry rosé.

There are no ‘Reserva’-style ageing tiers here; the key on the label is the appellation itself. As a rule, the more specific the village name, the more concentrated and characterful the wine. A little sweet Muscat de Beaumes-de-Venise rounds off the region on the pudding-wine side.

The grapes & the land

The lead grape is Grenache, supported by Syrah and Mourvèdre (the trio is often shortened to ‘GSM’), with Cinsault and others adding freshness in the blend.

The climate is warm and thoroughly Mediterranean, with abundant sunshine ripening Grenache to its plump, spicy best. The region’s signature feature is the Mistral, a strong, cold wind that funnels down the valley: it dries the vines after rain, keeps disease at bay and helps concentrate the grapes’ flavour. In Châteauneuf-du-Pape, many vineyards are famously carpeted with ‘galets’, large smooth pebbles that soak up the day’s heat and radiate it back to the vines at night, helping build those especially warm, rounded wines.

Producers to know in Southern Rhône

1

Château de Beaucastel

Iconic Châteauneuf-du-Pape estate famed for using all thirteen permitted grapes and long-lived, Mourvèdre-rich reds.

2

Domaine du Vieux Télégraphe

Historic Brunier-family Châteauneuf estate on the La Crau plateau, a benchmark for classic CdP.

3

Clos des Papes

Long-established Avril-family Châteauneuf domaine making a single, age-worthy red blend each year.

4

Domaine du Pégau

Traditional Féraud-family Châteauneuf estate known for powerful, old-school, long-lived reds.

5

Château de Saint Cosme

Leading Gigondas estate (Barruol family) making benchmark Grenache-based reds in the neighbouring appellation.

Food pairing. Roast lamb, beef daube, herby stews, grilled sausages and hard cheeses.

FAQ

Questions & answers

How does Southern Rhône wine taste?

Warm, generous and rounded, with ripe berry fruit, dried Mediterranean herbs, sweet spice and a full body with soft tannin. It is usually a blend led by Grenache. Everyday Côtes du Rhône is juicy and easy-going; Châteauneuf-du-Pape is deeper and more structured.

What is the difference between Côtes du Rhône and Châteauneuf-du-Pape?

Both are Southern Rhône reds, but they are different appellations. Côtes du Rhône is the broad, everyday category, made across a wide area for early drinking. Châteauneuf-du-Pape is a specific, prestigious village making richer, more concentrated, age-worthy wines, with up to thirteen grapes permitted in the blend.

Which wineries is the Southern Rhône known for?

In Châteauneuf-du-Pape, celebrated names include Château de Beaucastel, Domaine du Vieux Télégraphe, Clos des Papes and Domaine du Pégau. In nearby Gigondas, Château de Saint Cosme is a leading estate.
