Home of Barolo and the Nebbiolo grape.

Key grapes
Nebbiolo

Piedmont’s flagship reds from Nebbiolo — Barolo and Barbaresco — are pale garnet but powerful, with high, mouth-drying tannin and bright acidity carrying flavours of red cherry, dried rose, tar and truffle. The region also makes juicy, everyday Barbera and Dolcetto and lightly sweet, grapey Moscato.

Five things to know about Piedmont

  1. Nebbiolo is only the fourth most planted grape here. It holds about 10% of Piedmont’s 43,536 hectares. Barbera takes 31%, Moscato 22% and Dolcetto 13%. The variety the region is famous for is a minority crop, kept for the best slopes.
  2. Piedmont has no IGT wines at all. It is the only Italian region with none: 19 DOCGs, 41 DOCs and nothing in the middle tier. Around 94% of what it bottles carries an appellation.
  3. Barolo mapped its crus in 2010. The consorzio drew 181 menzioni geografiche aggiuntive — 170 vineyard names and 11 village names — across the eleven communes entitled to the wine. Cannubi and Bussia are legal boundaries, not marketing.
  4. Barolo and Barbaresco were in the class of 1980. They were among the first four wines given Italy’s new DOCG, with Brunello di Montalcino and Vino Nobile di Montepulciano. Barolo still cannot be sold with under 18 months in wood; Riserva waits 62 months.
  5. The region’s biggest wine is sweet and barely alcoholic. Asti and Moscato d’Asti come off 10,697 hectares, five times the Barolo zone, and made 8.56 million cases in 2022. Moscato d’Asti is capped at 6.5% alcohol.

How Piedmont wine tastes

The wines that make Piedmont famous are Barolo and Barbaresco, both made from the Nebbiolo grape. Do not be fooled by their pale, almost translucent colour — these are structured, long-lived reds with firm, grippy tannins, high acidity and a haunting mix of aromas: sour red cherry, dried rose petals, tar, leather and truffle.

Young, they can be austere and tannic; with age they soften and unfold into something remarkably complex. This is wine built for the table and for cellaring, not for gulping cold.

Piedmont is far more than Nebbiolo, though. Barbera gives deeply fruity, low-tannin reds with juicy acidity, Dolcetto is softer and easy-drinking, and the whites — crisp Gavi from Cortese and floral Roero Arneis — are fresh and food-friendly. Sweet, gently fizzy, low-alcohol Moscato d’Asti is the region’s charming grapey aperitif.

Signature styles & what to look for

Nebbiolo shows in tiers. Barolo and Barbaresco are the two top appellations (both DOCG, Italy’s highest classification), with Barolo generally the more powerful and Barbaresco a touch more approachable. For a gentler, earlier-drinking introduction, look for Langhe Nebbiolo or Nebbiolo d’Alba.

Ageing is written into the rules. Barolo must be aged for at least 38 months before release (with time in wood), and Barbaresco at least 26 — while Riserva bottlings are held back considerably longer. You will also see single-vineyard names (a cru, such as Cannubi or Rabajà) on the best bottles, marking wine from one prized site.

For everyday drinking, reach for Barbera d’Alba or d’Asti and Dolcetto; for whites, Gavi and Arneis; and for something sweet and low in alcohol, Moscato d’Asti.

The grapes & the land

Piedmont means ‘foot of the mountains’, ringed by the Alps and the Apennines, and it has a cool, continental climate with warm summers and cold winters. Autumn brings the nebbia — the thick fog that rolls through the Langhe hills and gives Nebbiolo its name.

Nebbiolo buds early and ripens very late, hanging on the vine into that misty October. This long, slow, cool finish is exactly what builds its trademark searing tannin, high acidity and layered perfume. The calcareous marl soils of the Langhe hills add finesse and lift.

Because the grape is so demanding and so sensitive to site, growers plant it on the best south-facing slopes and give lesser plots to Barbera and Dolcetto — which is why a single hillside can yield several very different wines.

Producers to know in Piedmont

1

Gaja

Iconic Barbaresco house that helped bring global fame to Piedmont’s Nebbiolo, also making acclaimed Barolo and other wines.

2

Giacomo Conterno

Revered traditionalist producer in Monforte d’Alba, famous for its long-lived Barolo, including the legendary Monfortino Riserva.

3

Bruno Giacosa

Historic grower and négociant renowned for classically styled, age-worthy Barolo and Barbaresco.

4

Vietti

Castiglione Falletto estate dating to the 1800s, an early champion of single-vineyard (cru) Barolo.

5

G.D. Vajra

Family estate near Barolo village known for elegant, fragrant, high-toned Nebbiolo.

Food pairing. Barolo and Barbaresco are made for rich local fare — braised beef, game, mushroom risotto and anything with truffles — while juicy Barbera loves tomato-based pasta and pizza, and Moscato d’Asti finishes a meal with fruit or light desserts.

FAQ

Questions & answers

How does Piedmont wine taste?

Its famous Nebbiolo reds, Barolo and Barbaresco, are pale in colour but powerful — firm tannin and bright acidity carrying red cherry, dried rose, tar and truffle. The region also makes juicy Barbera, soft Dolcetto and sweet, fizzy Moscato.

What is the difference between Barolo and Barbaresco?

Both are made from Nebbiolo in neighbouring areas. Barolo is generally the bigger, more powerful and longer-ageing of the two, while Barbaresco tends to be a little softer and quicker to approach.

Which wineries is Piedmont known for?

Established Nebbiolo names include Gaja, Giacomo Conterno, Bruno Giacosa, Vietti and G.D. Vajra — spanning both traditional and more modern styles.
