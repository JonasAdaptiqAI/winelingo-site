Umbria Wine Region

Umbria is a green, landlocked region in central Italy, often called the ‘green heart’ of the country. It is best known for the structured red Sagrantino di Montefalco, one of Italy’s most tannic and powerful reds (made from the thick-skinned Sagrantino grape), and for the crisp, easy white Orvieto, a blend led by Grechetto and Trebbiano. It also grows plenty of Sangiovese, the backbone grape of central Italy.

Two contrasting faces: Montefalco Sagrantino is a deep, dark, intensely tannic red full of blackberry, plum and dried herbs, while Orvieto is a light, crisp white with citrus, orchard fruit and a gently nutty finish.

How Umbria wine tastes

Umbria offers two very different experiences. Montefalco Sagrantino is one of Italy’s most powerful reds — inky and dark, with blackberry, black plum, dried herbs and a savoury, earthy depth, and some of the firmest, most mouth-coating tannins you’ll meet anywhere. Young examples can feel austere, and they genuinely reward patience.

At the other end sits Orvieto, a fresh, easy white with bright citrus and orchard fruit (apple, pear), a light body and a subtle almond note on the finish. It is made to be poured young and chilled.

Between the two is Sangiovese-based Montefalco Rosso, softer and more approachable — a good introduction to the region before you tackle full-blown Sagrantino.

Signature styles & what to look for

Montefalco Sagrantino DOCG is the flagship: 100% Sagrantino, aged for a good stretch (around three years, including time in oak) to help tame those huge tannins. There’s also a traditional sweet passito version, made from dried grapes. Montefalco Rosso is a lighter, Sangiovese-led blend.

For whites, Orvieto DOC is a blend led by Grechetto and Trebbiano (locally Procanico), typically light and crisp. Just over the border in Torgiano you’ll find the respected Torgiano Rosso Riserva DOCG. If you want power, reach for Sagrantino; for everyday refreshment, Orvieto.

The grapes & the land

The star red grape is Sagrantino, remarkably thick-skinned and one of the most tannin- and polyphenol-rich varieties in the world — which is exactly why its wines are so dark and structured. Sangiovese is the everyday backbone, while Grechetto and Trebbiano drive the Orvieto whites.

Umbria is landlocked and green — often called the ‘green heart’ of Italy — with a warm but moderate inland climate and hilly vineyards. Warm days ripen the stubborn Sagrantino fully, while cooler nights and elevation keep the Orvieto whites fresh and lively.

Producers to know in Umbria

1

Arnaldo Caprai

The estate most responsible for reviving Sagrantino, and the leading name in Montefalco today.

2

Lungarotti

A historic family estate in neighbouring Torgiano, long a benchmark for quality Umbrian reds.

3

Paolo Bea

A small, traditional Montefalco grower known for long-aged, artisanal Sagrantino.

4

Tabarrini

A family producer in Montefalco making respected single-vineyard Sagrantino.

Food pairing. Sagrantino’s big tannins want rich, fatty food: wild boar, grilled and roasted red meats and hard aged cheeses. Crisp Orvieto is lovely with fish, poultry, salads and antipasti.

FAQ

Questions & answers

How does Umbria wine taste?

It depends on the wine. Montefalco Sagrantino is dark, powerful and very tannic with blackberry and dried-herb flavours, while Orvieto is a light, crisp white with citrus and orchard fruit and a gently nutty finish.

Which wineries is Umbria known for?

Well-established names include Arnaldo Caprai and Tabarrini in Montefalco, the traditional grower Paolo Bea, and the historic Lungarotti estate in nearby Torgiano.

Why is Sagrantino so tannic?

Sagrantino is one of the most tannin- and polyphenol-rich grapes in the world, with very thick skins. That gives it deep colour and firm, mouth-coating tannins, which is why the wines often need several years to soften.
