Five things to know about Mosel

  1. The label lost two rivers in 2007. Until then it read Mosel-Saar-Ruwer. Growers dropped the Saar and the Ruwer to give export markets one name to remember. Those valleys are still there, and still hold some of the finest sites.
  2. Sonnenuhr means sundial, and the sundials are real. Jodocus Prüm, an ancestor of J.J. Prüm, set one into the slate above Wehlen in 1842 so pickers could tell the time. Several Mosel villages have one, and the vineyards took their names from them.
  3. The Bremmer Calmont reaches 65 degrees. Thirty-three hectares of slate above the village of Bremm, and among the steepest vineyards on earth. No machine can reach it, so every vine is pruned and picked by hand.
  4. Romans were shipping it in barrels by 220 AD. A carved stone wine ship from Neumagen-Dhron, made around then as a merchant’s tomb, shows six rowers, 22 oars and four fat casks. The village still calls itself Germany’s oldest wine village.
  5. It is farmed in gardens, not estates. Around 5,000 growers share 8,287 hectares across 125 wine villages, under two hectares each on average. That is why one celebrated slope usually appears under half a dozen different family names.

How Mosel wine tastes

Mosel Riesling is one of the most distinctive whites in the world: delicate, low in alcohol and thrillingly fresh. Expect bright lime, green apple, white peach and citrus, lifted by a piercing, mouth-watering acidity and an unmistakable stony, wet-slate minerality.

Many Mosel wines carry a touch of natural sweetness, but this is not a heavy or sugary style. The sweetness is held in perfect tension with that racy acidity, so the wine tastes balanced, weightless and refreshing rather than cloying, more like biting a crisp apple than eating a dessert.

Styles run from bone dry to lusciously sweet, but even the sweeter wines feel light on their feet, often under 10% alcohol. Great Mosel Riesling is also famously long-lived, gaining honeyed, petrol-like complexity with age while keeping its freshness.

Signature styles & what to look for

Germany labels many wines by ripeness at harvest, the Prädikat scale, and it is the key to Mosel’s styles. From lightest and driest to richest and sweetest: Kabinett (light, crisp, often just off-dry), Spätlese (‘late harvest’, riper and rounder), Auslese (‘select harvest’, richer and usually sweeter), then the rare, lusciously sweet Beerenauslese (BA), Trockenbeerenauslese (TBA) and frozen-grape Eiswein.

For a dry wine, look for the word trocken (dry) or Grosses Gewächs / GG, the designation for a top dry wine from a great vineyard. If a wine simply says Kabinett or Spätlese without ‘trocken’, expect a little gentle sweetness balanced by acidity.

The best sites are single vineyards, such as the famous Scharzhofberg or the vineyards around Bernkastel and Wehlen, and their names appear proudly on the label.

The grapes & the land

This is Riesling country almost to the exclusion of all else, planted along the steep banks of the Mosel river and its tributaries the Saar and Ruwer, in one of the coolest wine climates in Germany.

The vineyards cling to some of the steepest slopes in the wine world, angled to catch every ray of sun and set on dark slate soils. That slate stores daytime heat to help the grapes ripen and, many believe, lends the wines their signature stony, mineral character.

Because the climate is so cool, the grapes ripen slowly and keep very high natural acidity while staying low in alcohol, which is exactly what gives Mosel Riesling its racy, low-alcohol, crystalline style. The river itself reflects light and moderates temperatures on these precipitous banks.

Producers to know in Mosel

1

Egon Müller

Saar estate at Scharzhofberg, regarded as Germany’s most prestigious Riesling producer and famous for its sweet Auslesen.

2

Joh. Jos. Prüm

Iconic Wehlen estate (often written J.J. Prüm), celebrated for long-lived, delicately sweet Rieslings that reward patience.

3

Dr. Loosen

Bernkastel-based estate led by Ernst Loosen, known for old-vine Rieslings from the Mosel’s great slate vineyards.

4

Fritz Haag

Historic Brauneberg estate prized for precise, elegant Rieslings from the famed Juffer and Juffer Sonnenuhr sites.

5

Selbach-Oster

Zeltingen family estate respected for finely balanced, terroir-driven Rieslings across the Prädikat range.

Food pairing. Wonderful with spicy Asian food such as Thai and Sichuan, where its sweetness tames the heat; also great with pork, smoked fish, and creamy or tangy cheeses. Sweeter Auslese and beyond suit fruit tarts and blue cheese.

FAQ

Questions & answers

How does Mosel wine taste?

Mosel wine is almost all Riesling: light, low in alcohol and racy, with piercing acidity and pure lime, green apple and peach fruit over a stony, wet-slate minerality. It ranges from bone dry to sweet, but always tastes fresh and balanced.

Which wineries is Mosel known for?

Renowned Mosel producers include Egon Müller, Joh. Jos. Prüm (J.J. Prüm), Dr. Loosen, Fritz Haag and Selbach-Oster. Egon Müller and J.J. Prüm are among the most celebrated Riesling estates in the world.

Is Mosel Riesling always sweet?

No. Mosel makes everything from bone-dry to lusciously sweet. For a dry wine look for the word ‘trocken’ or ‘GG’; wines simply labelled Kabinett or Spätlese usually have a little gentle sweetness balanced by the wine’s crisp acidity.
