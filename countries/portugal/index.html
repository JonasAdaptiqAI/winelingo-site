Wine of Portugal

Portugal is part of the old world of wine. On Winelingo you can explore 4 of its wine regions — Alentejo, Dão, Douro, Vinho Verde — along with the grapes they grow and the styles they are famous for.

Portugal is a small country with an unusually deep bench. More than 250 grape varieties are native to it, most grown almost nowhere else, and most Portuguese wine is a blend of several of them rather than one named grape. That is the first thing to get used to: labels here name the place, not the variety. It is also why Portuguese wine still tastes distinctly Portuguese.

The second thing is the Douro. In 1756 the minister later known as the Marquês de Pombal had the valley surveyed and demarcated, and over the following years its limits were marked out with granite posts, making it one of the first wine regions anywhere to be legally defined and policed. It was done to protect Port, the fortified wine the British trade had made famous, and for a long time that was almost the whole story. A first serious dry red, Barca Velha, appeared as early as 1952, but only from the 1980s and 1990s did growers turn to dry wine in numbers, using the same grapes on the same schist terraces — and the Douro turned out to be a fine table-wine region that had simply never been asked.

What Portuguese wine tastes like

Portuguese reds are darker and firmer than most people expect. Touriga Nacional, the flagship, smells of violets and black fruit and carries real tannin; blended with its neighbours it makes reds that are dense but still fresh, because the best sites are high enough or cool enough to hold on to acidity. Dão, up in the granite hills, is the elegant, restrained end of the scale. Alentejo, hot and flat, is the ripe and generous end.

The whites divide just as clearly. Vinho Verde is light, tart, often faintly fizzy and low in alcohol — usually nine to eleven per cent — which makes it summer wine in the plainest sense.

Then the fortified pair. Port is sweet, warm and grippy. Madeira runs on the opposite instinct: baked, tangy and almost indestructible, with acidity that cuts straight through the sugar.

Signature grapes of Portugal

Touriga Nacional is Portugal's calling card: small berries, thick skins, aromas of violet and blackberry, and enough tannin to age well. It anchors both Port and the dry reds of the Douro and Dão. The other grapes here arrive under Portuguese aliases. Tempranillo is Tinta Roriz in the Douro and Aragonez in Alentejo. Mencía is Jaen in Dão, where it adds juicy red fruit. Albariño is Alvarinho in the far north of Vinho Verde, often riper and peachier than the Spanish version. Same vines, different names, different accents.

Wine regions of Portugal

Four Portuguese regions have pages here, and between them they cover most of what the country does. The Douro is the steep, terraced, schist-soiled valley in the north-east that makes both Port and increasingly serious dry reds. Dão sits inland and higher, on granite, sheltered by mountains, and gives Portugal's most restrained reds. Vinho Verde is the wet, green north-west, and its wines are light and sharp. Alentejo is the hot southern plain — soft, ripe reds, plus a surviving tradition of fermenting in large clay pots.

Three Portuguese bottles to start with

10 Year Old Tawny Port
Nutty and caramelised, sweet but never cloying, because years in the barrel dry the fruit out and turn it savoury; it keeps for weeks once opened and shows exactly what wood ageing does to a wine.
A dry Douro red (labelled Douro, not Port)
The same grapes as Port, fermented all the way dry: dark, firm and savoury, and the clearest way to meet the valley without sugar.
Alvarinho from Vinho Verde
A step up from basic Vinho Verde — still bright and citrusy, but with more peach and body, and excellent with anything grilled from the sea.

FAQ

Portuguese wine questions

What makes Port different from ordinary red wine?

Port is fortified. Part-way through fermentation, neutral grape spirit is added, which stops the yeast before it has eaten all the sugar. The wine is left sweet and finishes at roughly 19 to 22 per cent alcohol, against the 13 or 14 per cent of a normal red. It is made in the Douro Valley from native grapes such as Touriga Nacional and Tinta Roriz.

Does Vinho Verde mean green wine?

Literally yes, but green here means young rather than a colour. Vinho Verde is a place — the wet, green north-west corner of Portugal — and its wines were traditionally drunk within months of harvest. Most are white, light, sharply acidic and around nine to eleven per cent alcohol, often with a faint prickle of carbon dioxide. Red and rosé versions exist too.

Why is Madeira heated while it is being made?

Because heat is what makes it Madeira. Barrels once travelled as ballast through the tropics and the wine improved on the journey, so producers learned to recreate the effect. Everyday Madeira is warmed in tanks, a process called estufagem; the finest ages slowly in casks under the lodge roof, warmed by the sun, the canteiro method. The result survives months open.

Why don't Portuguese labels usually name the grape?

Because most Portuguese wine blends several native varieties, and the region is treated as the more useful piece of information. A bottle labelled Dão or Alentejo tells you the climate, the soils and the local grape mix in a single word. Some producers do bottle single varieties, Touriga Nacional especially, but blending remains the norm and the tradition.
