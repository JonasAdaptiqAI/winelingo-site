Wine of France

France is part of the old world of wine. On Winelingo you can explore 14 of its wine regions — Alsace, Beaujolais, Bordeaux, Burgundy and more — along with the grapes they grow and the styles they are famous for.

France is the country every other wine nation ends up being measured against, and the reason has less to do with the grapes than with the record-keeping. Burgundian monks spent centuries working out which slope ripened differently from the one beside it, plot by plot; those parcels, the climats of the Côte d'Or, became a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2015. Bordeaux's brokers ranked the red wines of the Médoc into five growths for the Paris exhibition of 1855 — adding Château Haut-Brion from Graves, and classifying the sweet whites of Sauternes and Barsac in a separate list of their own — and both lists are still quoted at auction today.

The modern rulebook came later. A decree-law of 1935 created the body that became the INAO, and the first six appellations d'origine contrôlée were granted on 15 May 1936 — two of them, Châteauneuf-du-Pape and Tavel, have their own pages here. France now has more than 300 wine appellations, and each one fixes the boundary, the permitted grapes and how the vines must be grown. It is why a French label usually names a place rather than a grape: the place is meant to tell you the rest.

What French wine tastes like

There is no single French flavour, and that is rather the point. The country runs from the chalk of Champagne, where grapes barely ripen, to Mediterranean scrub above Provence.

What the wines tend to share is restraint and a savoury edge. They are built to sit beside food rather than shout on their own, so acidity stays high and ripe fruit is rarely the loudest thing in the glass.

Broadly: northern whites like Chablis, Sancerre and Alsace Riesling are dry, taut and citrusy, often with a stony or smoky note. Red Burgundy is pale and light-bodied, tasting of red cherry and forest floor. Bordeaux is firmer, with blackcurrant and a cedar note from oak. Head south and the reds warm up — Châteauneuf-du-Pape is fuller and scented with dried herbs. Sauternes, golden and sweet, tastes of honey and apricot with enough acidity to stop it cloying.

Signature grapes of France

Most of the grapes the rest of the world plants were French first. Chardonnay and Pinot Noir come from Burgundy and Champagne; Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot and Cabernet Franc from Bordeaux; Sauvignon Blanc and Chenin Blanc from the Loire. Melon de Bourgogne began in Burgundy, as its name admits, but has been the Loire's grape for three centuries and is what Muscadet is made from. The Rhône gave us Syrah — dark, peppery and meaty in the north — plus Grenache, Mourvèdre and Cinsault, the warm-climate trio behind southern reds and most Provence rosé. Beaujolais has Gamay, light and crunchy. Alsace grows Riesling, Gewurztraminer, Pinot Gris and Muscat, and makes them dry more often than not.

Wine regions of France

The simplest way to hold France in your head is north to south. At the top sit Champagne and Alsace, then Chablis and Burgundy, and westward the Loire Valley with Sancerre inside it — cool places making taut whites and lighter reds. Beaujolais sits just below Burgundy. On the Atlantic side, Bordeaux and its sweet-wine neighbour Sauternes. The Rhône splits in two: the steep northern Rhône, then the broader southern Rhône with Châteauneuf-du-Pape at its heart. Warmest of all are Provence and Languedoc-Roussillon along the Mediterranean.

Three French bottles to start with

Sancerre
Sauvignon Blanc at its most precise: dry, high in acidity, grapefruit and cut grass, with a chalky finish. A clean lesson in what French restraint means.
Cru Beaujolais
Gamay from one of the ten named villages north of Lyon: light, juicy and low in tannin, but with real structure underneath. An easy red that still rewards attention.
Châteauneuf-du-Pape
Grenache-led and generous, full of red fruit and dried herbs. It shows what warm southern France does without demanding that you enjoy heavy tannin.

FAQ

French wine questions

Why don't French wine labels tell you the grape variety?

Because the French system labels by place. An appellation such as Sancerre or Chablis already fixes which grapes are allowed, so naming the region is meant to tell a local shopper the grape, the style and the growing rules all at once. It is efficient if you know the code and baffling if you don't — which is exactly what learning a handful of regions fixes.

What is the difference between Left Bank and Right Bank Bordeaux?

The Garonne and the Dordogne, which meet north of the city to form the Gironde estuary, split the region in two. On the Left Bank, gravel soils suit Cabernet Sauvignon, so the reds are firmer and more structured, tasting of blackcurrant and cedar. On the Right Bank, clay and limestone favour Merlot and Cabernet Franc, giving rounder, plummier wines that are usually easier to drink young. Same region, two very different handshakes.

Is crémant just cheaper Champagne?

Not quite. Crémant is French sparkling wine made by the same traditional method as Champagne — a second fermentation inside the bottle — but grown outside the Champagne region, in places such as Alsace, Burgundy and the Loire. Different soils and often different grapes mean a genuinely different flavour rather than simply a discount version of the same thing.

Is Chablis a grape or a place?

A place. Chablis is the northernmost part of Burgundy, and the wine is Chardonnay, almost always without noticeable oak. Its vineyards sit on Kimmeridgian limestone, an ancient seabed full of tiny comma-shaped fossil oysters called Exogyra virgula. The wines come out lean, citrusy and distinctly stony — a long way from the ripe, buttery Chardonnay many people expect.
