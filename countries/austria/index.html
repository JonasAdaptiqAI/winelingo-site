Wine of Austria

Austria is part of the old world of wine. On Winelingo you can explore 3 of its wine regions — Burgenland, Kamptal, Wachau — along with the grapes they grow and the styles they are famous for.

Signature grapes
Grüner Veltliner, Chardonnay, Riesling, Zweigelt

Austria's vineyards sit almost entirely in the east of the country, away from the Alps, in a band running from the Danube valley down towards the Hungarian border. It is a cool place to grow grapes and that shows in the glass: the wines are dry, precise and high in acidity, and most of the vineyard is planted to white varieties. Grüner Veltliner alone accounts for close to a third of it.

Modern Austrian wine really begins in 1985. Laboratories in West Germany found diethylene glycol in Austrian bottles — a handful of producers had been adding it to make cheap wine taste sweeter and fuller — and exports collapsed almost overnight. What followed was a complete rebuild: stricter law, lower yields, and a decisive move away from bulk sweetness towards dry, estate-bottled wine. In 2002 Austria introduced the DAC system, loosely modelled on the French appellations, which ties a name to a region's typical style rather than to a grape alone.

What Austrian wine tastes like

Austrian white wine is dry, brisk and clean-lined. There is very little oak in the picture and almost no tropical-fruit richness. What you get instead is citrus, orchard fruit, green herbs and a firm acidity that keeps everything upright.

Grüner Veltliner is the signature: green apple and grapefruit, a herbal note somewhere between celery and lentil, and a twist of white pepper on the finish. Riesling here is nearly always fully dry, with peach and lime over a stony core — drier and a shade fuller than most German Riesling.

The reds are lighter than you might expect. Zweigelt is juicy and soft, full of sour cherry, and takes a slight chill happily. Blaufränkisch is the serious one: dark berries, black pepper, firm acidity and real tannin, closer in spirit to a peppery Syrah than to anything soft and jammy.

Signature grapes of Austria

Grüner Veltliner is the national grape, covering close to a third of Austria's vineyards — dry, peppery, citrusy and unusually versatile at the table. Riesling takes far less ground but claims the best rocky sites, especially in the Wachau and on Kamptal's Heiligenstein, and is made bone dry. Chardonnay is a smaller presence, planted mostly in Lower Austria and Burgenland, and made fresher and less buttery than most. Among reds, Zweigelt is the most planted: a 1922 crossing of Blaufränkisch and St. Laurent, bred for easy, cherry-scented drinking. Blaufränkisch is the ageworthy one, and Pinot Noir grows here too, in small quantities.

Wine regions of Austria

Three Austrian regions have pages here, and between them they cover most of what the country is known for. The Wachau is a stretch of the Danube valley between Melk and Krems, with vines on stone terraces steep enough that they must be worked by hand — Grüner Veltliner on the loess lower down, Riesling on the bare rock above. Kamptal, just east along the river Kamp, works the same two grapes with a touch more generosity. Burgenland, down on the Hungarian border, is warmer, and is where the reds and the great sweet wines come from.

Three Austrian bottles to start with

Wachau Grüner Veltliner, Federspiel
Federspiel is the Wachau's middle category, 11.5–12.5% alcohol. Dry, peppery and refreshing, and the clearest possible introduction to Austria's signature grape.
Kamptal DAC Riesling
Bone-dry Riesling with peach, lime and a stony edge. If you avoid Riesling because you assume it is sweet, this is the bottle that settles it.
Mittelburgenland DAC Blaufränkisch
Blaufränkisch on its home turf in Burgenland: dark fruit, black pepper and firm acidity, with enough structure to sit beside roast meat.

FAQ

Austrian wine questions

How do you pronounce Grüner Veltliner?

Roughly GROO-ner felt-LEE-ner. The ü is the tricky part: shape your lips as if for "oo" and say "ee". In practice almost nobody says the whole thing — Austrians and sommeliers shorten it to "Grüner", so you can order it without ever attempting the full name out loud.

Is Grüner Veltliner sweet or dry?

Almost always dry. It can smell fruity because of its citrus and orchard-fruit aromas, but the sugar has been fermented out. Styles run from light and simple to concentrated single-vineyard bottlings that age for years. Sweet Austrian wine certainly exists, but it comes mostly from Burgenland and is labelled clearly as such.

What does DAC mean on an Austrian wine label?

DAC stands for Districtus Austriae Controllatus. It works like a French appellation: it tells you the wine comes from a named region, uses that region's approved grapes, and tastes the way wines from there are meant to taste. Austria introduced it in 2002, starting with the Weinviertel. Kamptal followed from the 2008 vintage and the Wachau from 2020.

What do Steinfeder, Federspiel and Smaragd mean?

They are three dry-wine categories used in the Wachau, set by the local growers' association Vinea Wachau rather than by law, and based on natural alcohol. Steinfeder is the lightest, up to 11.5%. Federspiel is the classic middle ground, 11.5–12.5%. Smaragd is the ripest and most concentrated, at least 12.5%, and is named after a local lizard.
