Wine of Italy

Italy is part of the old world of wine. On Winelingo you can explore 12 of its wine regions — Abruzzo, Alto Adige, Campania, Friuli-Venezia Giulia and more — along with the grapes they grow and the styles they are famous for.

Signature grapes

Italy makes wine everywhere. Vines grow in the Alpine valleys of Alto Adige, where many labels appear in German as well as Italian, and on the volcanic slopes of Sicily, and in the dry heat of Sardinia. All twenty of the country's administrative regions produce wine.

Where most countries eventually narrowed down to a handful of international grapes, Italy kept its own — hundreds of them — and wrote its rules around places rather than varieties. Wines are classified DOC or DOCG, with DOCG the stricter tier; by 2025 there were 78 DOCG zones and 341 DOCs. That is why Italian labels so often name a place and not a grape. Barolo means Nebbiolo grown in a small corner of Piedmont. Chianti Classico means mostly Sangiovese from the hills between Florence and Siena. Learning Italian wine is really learning a map, and the reward for the effort is that nearly all of it was made to be drunk with food — which is why a bottle that tastes a little tart on its own suddenly makes sense once dinner is on the table.

What Italian wine tastes like

Two things run through Italian wine: acidity and grip. Even ripe Italian reds usually finish dry and savoury rather than soft and sweet, and that briskness is exactly what makes them work with tomato, olive oil and cured meat.

Sangiovese, the country's most planted grape, tastes of sour cherry and dried herbs with something faintly like tomato leaf, and leaves a dusty tannin behind. Nebbiolo looks deceptively pale, often turning brick-orange at the rim, then arrives with tannin and acidity that need years to settle — rose petal, tar and cherry. Head south and the sun shows: Aglianico is dark and firm, Puglia's reds are riper and rounder. The whites lean towards freshness — lemon, almond, a stony edge — rather than oak and tropical fruit, though Fiano and Greco in Campania are broader and more savoury than that suggests.

Signature grapes of Italy

Sangiovese is Italy's most planted grape and Tuscany's spine: sour cherry, herbs, a grippy finish that wants food. Nebbiolo is pale but powerful — rose, tar and tannin. Corvina brings the bright sour-cherry lift to Valpolicella and, dried before pressing, the richness of Amarone. Aglianico is the dark, firm red of Campania. Among whites, Garganega tastes of almond and lemon pith, Fiano and Greco are broader and more savoury, Glera makes Prosecco, Gewürztraminer perfumes Alto Adige, and Pinot Grigio is the crisp, quiet one. Chardonnay builds Franciacorta, and Grenache, called Cannonau on Sardinia, ripens into warm, herbal reds.

Wine regions of Italy

The twelve Italian regions here run the length of the country. In the north sit Piedmont, home of Nebbiolo; Lombardy, where Franciacorta is made by the same bottle-fermented method as Champagne; Alto Adige, high, cool and Alpine; Friuli-Venezia Giulia, the centre of Italy's skin-contact orange whites; and Veneto, which gives us Prosecco, Valpolicella and Amarone. In the centre, Tuscany is Sangiovese country, Umbria grows the fiercely tannic Sagrantino, and Abruzzo makes juicy Montepulciano. Further south, Campania has volcanic soils and ancient varieties, Puglia ripens Primitivo, and Sicily and Sardinia work with heat, altitude and sea air.

Three Italian bottles to start with

Chianti Classico DOCG
At least 80% Sangiovese from the hills between Florence and Siena — tart cherry, dried herbs, and the ideal partner for anything with tomato in it.
Conegliano Valdobbiadene Prosecco Superiore DOCG
Prosecco from its original steep hills, at least 85% Glera: pear, white flowers and a soft fizz, noticeably finer than the flat-land version.
Barolo DOCG
Nebbiolo at full strength, aged at least 38 months before release. Choose a bottle with a few years on it and give it an hour open.

FAQ

Italian wine questions

What is the difference between DOC and DOCG on an Italian wine label?

Both mean the wine comes from a defined place and follows that place's rules. DOCG is the stricter tier: tighter limits on yields, longer minimum ageing, and an official tasting panel before approval. DOCG bottles also carry a numbered government seal across the neck. As of 2025 Italy had 78 DOCG zones and 341 DOCs.

Is Chianti the same thing as Chianti Classico?

No — they are two separate DOCGs. Chianti Classico covers the original hills between Florence and Siena, became its own denomination in 1996, and needs at least 80% Sangiovese. Plain Chianti covers a much larger area around it and needs at least 70%. The black rooster on the label marks Chianti Classico.

What grape is Barolo made from?

Nebbiolo, and only Nebbiolo. Barolo comes from a group of villages in the Langhe hills of Piedmont and must age at least 38 months before release, 18 of them in wood; Riserva needs 62 months. Barbaresco, next door, is also entirely Nebbiolo but with shorter minimums, so it often drinks well younger.

Why do Italian red wines taste tart or sour?

High natural acidity, mostly. Grapes like Sangiovese, Nebbiolo and Corvina hold onto acid even when fully ripe, and Italian winemaking tends not to soften it with residual sugar or heavy oak. On its own that can read as sour. With tomato, olive oil, cheese or cured meat it stops reading as sour and starts tasting refreshing.
