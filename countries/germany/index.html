Wine of Germany

Germany is part of the old world of wine. On Winelingo you can explore 5 of its wine regions — Mosel, Nahe, Pfalz, Rheingau and more — along with the grapes they grow and the styles they are famous for.

Signature grapes
Riesling, Grauburgunder, Spätburgunder, Weissburgunder

Germany makes wine near the northern edge of where grapes will ripen at all, and almost everything about German wine follows from that. The vineyards cling to steep slopes above rivers — the Mosel, the Rhine, the Nahe — because water reflects light, a slope tilts the ground towards the sun, and dark slate holds the day's heat into the night. Ripening is slow, which is exactly what you want here: it builds flavour while leaving acidity intact and alcohol low.

The wines have long been better than their reputation, and the reputation problem was mostly a labelling problem. A 1971 law organised German wine around grape sugar rather than place, and a flood of cheap sweet blends did the rest of the damage. That is now being undone. A reform passed in 2021 rebuilds the system around origin — region, village, single vineyard — and becomes binding from the 2026 vintage. Meanwhile Germany has quietly become the world's third-largest grower of Pinot Noir, behind France and the United States — a grape it has grown for centuries, and one a warming climate now ripens more reliably.

What German wine tastes like

German Riesling is the reference point. Expect green apple, lime and white peach, sometimes a smoky or flinty note where the soil is slate, and acidity that is bracing rather than tiring. Alcohol is often strikingly low — a Mosel Kabinett can sit under ten per cent — which is why these wines feel weightless even when they are not fully dry.

Sweetness is the thing people get wrong. Most German wine made today is dry or off-dry; the word to look for is trocken. Where sweetness is there, the acidity balances it, so the wine reads as fresh rather than sugary, and it handles chilli, ginger and roast pork beautifully.

The reds are lighter than expected. Spätburgunder — Pinot Noir — is red-cherried, earthy and finely tannic. Dornfelder is deeper, softer and simpler: an everyday red.

Signature grapes of Germany

Riesling is Germany's most planted grape and the one worth learning properly: high acidity, low alcohol, and an unusual knack for tasting like the place it grew. Müller-Thurgau, a Riesling crossing made in 1882, is Germany's second most planted white grape — softer, gentler, made for drinking young. Silvaner is dry and understated, and Rheinhessen grows more of it than anywhere else. Then the Pinots, which Germany calls Burgunder: Spätburgunder is Pinot Noir, Grauburgunder is Pinot Gris, Weissburgunder is Pinot Blanc. Dornfelder, bred in 1955, makes soft everyday reds.

Wine regions of Germany

Five German regions have pages here, all in the west and all shaped by rivers. The Mosel is the steepest and coolest, with blue slate soils and the most delicate Rieslings of all. The Rheingau faces south across a wide bend in the Rhine and makes fuller, firmer versions. Rheinhessen is the largest region by vineyard area and the most varied. Pfalz, just south of it, is warmer and sunnier, good for the Burgunder family as well as Riesling. The Nahe lies west of Rheinhessen, roughly between it and the Mosel, and is geologically the most mixed of the lot.

Three German bottles to start with

Mosel Riesling Kabinett
The classic starting point: low in alcohol, gently sweet, yet so fresh it drinks almost dry, and one of the few wines that genuinely loves spicy food.
Rheingau Riesling trocken
Proof that German Riesling need not be sweet at all — firm, dry and citrusy, with more body than the Mosel and enough grip for roast chicken.
Spätburgunder from the Pfalz
German Pinot Noir: light-bodied, red-fruited and savoury rather than jammy, and a good way in if big, heavy reds are not your thing.

FAQ

German wine questions

Is all German wine sweet?

No — most of it is dry these days. The word to look for is trocken, which means dry, or halbtrocken and feinherb, which mean off-dry. The traditional Prädikat terms such as Kabinett, Spätlese and Auslese describe how ripe the grapes were when picked, not how sweet the finished wine is, though wines carrying those words are often left with some sweetness.

What do Kabinett, Spätlese and Auslese actually mean?

They are ripeness levels, measured by the sugar in the juice at harvest. Kabinett is the lightest, from a normal main picking. Spätlese means late-picked, so riper and fuller. Auslese means selected — bunches chosen individually for extra ripeness. Above those sit Beerenauslese, Trockenbeerenauslese and Eiswein, all rare and sweet. Higher up the ladder does not mean better, only riper.

How is Eiswein made?

The grapes are left hanging into winter and picked only once the temperature drops to minus seven Celsius or lower, then pressed while still frozen solid. The ice stays behind in the press, so what trickles out is intensely concentrated sweet juice. Picking has to be done by hand, often before dawn, and mild winters mean some years produce almost none at all.

Why is German Riesling grown on such steep slopes?

Because in a cool climate every degree of warmth counts. Steep, south-facing slopes catch far more sun than flat land, and the rivers below bounce extra light back up onto the vines. Dark slate soils, as in the Mosel, absorb heat by day and release it overnight. The cost is that these vineyards are too steep for machinery, so the work is done by hand.
