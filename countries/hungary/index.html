Wine of Hungary

Hungary is part of the old world of wine. On Winelingo you can explore its most celebrated wine region — Tokaj — along with the grapes grown there and the styles it is famous for.

Signature grapes
Furmint, Hárslevelű, Sárgamuskotály

Hungary's wine story runs through one place above all others: Tokaj, in the far north-east, where the Bodrog joins the Tisza beneath the volcanic Zemplén hills. A royal decree usually dated to 1737, in the reign of Charles III, named the settlements entitled to call their wine Tokaji and forbade bringing grapes in from outside — one of the first legally drawn wine boundaries anywhere. Hungary has twenty-two wine districts, but this is the one with a global name, and UNESCO inscribed its historic cultural landscape in 2002.

Tokaji Aszú was the sweet wine of European courts, and the region kept the motto that went with it: vinum regum, rex vinorum, the wine of kings and the king of wines. Four decades of state-controlled production after the Second World War flattened its reputation, and rebuilding only began in earnest in the early 1990s. Tokaj's cellars are tunnels cut into rock, their walls furred with a black mould, Cladosporium cellare, that feeds on evaporating alcohol.

What Hungarian wine tastes like

Tokaji Aszú is the region's calling card, and it is unlike other sweet wines. The colour runs from deep gold to amber, the texture is thick and almost syrupy, and the flavours go to dried apricot, orange marmalade, quince, honey and caramel.

What stops it cloying is acidity. Furmint carries enormous natural acid, so a wine with 120 grams of sugar per litre or more still finishes bright rather than heavy. Ageing in wood adds a nutty, tea-like edge.

Dry Furmint tastes completely different: brisk and firm, with green apple and pear, a lick of smoke or wet stone, and a slightly waxy texture. Hárslevelű — the name means "linden leaf" — is softer and more floral, and Sárgamuskotály brings grapey Muscat perfume.

Signature grapes of Hungary

Six grapes are permitted in Tokaj, but three do most of the work. Furmint is the backbone: late-ripening, thick-skinned enough to shrivel gracefully, and armed with the high acidity that makes both great Aszú and increasingly good dry white. Hárslevelű, "linden leaf", is the aromatic partner — softer, honeyed and floral, and also prone to noble rot. Sárgamuskotály is the local name for Muscat Blanc à Petits Grains, added in small proportions for lift and perfume. The remaining three, Zéta, Kabar and Kövérszőlő, are rare.

Wine regions of Hungary

Tokaj — Tokaj-Hegyalja in full — is the only Hungarian region covered here, and it earns the space. It spreads across roughly two dozen villages, with Tokaj itself, Mád and Tarcal as the main centres, on slopes of rhyolite tuff, andesite, clay and wind-blown loess left by millions of years of volcanic activity. Autumn is the decisive season. Mists rise off the Bodrog and Tisza in the morning and burn away in the afternoon, encouraging noble rot on the grapes without letting damaging rot take hold. Single vineyards, or dűlő, increasingly appear on labels.

Three Hungarian bottles to start with

Dry Furmint
The easiest way in: a crisp, high-acid dry white with apple and smoky-stone character, and a genuinely good match for roast chicken or hard cheese.
Tokaji Aszú 5 Puttonyos
The classic. Concentrated apricot and marmalade held up by piercing acidity — a sweet wine that finishes clean rather than heavy or sticky.

Made from whole bunches carrying both shrivelled and healthy berries, so it is lighter and nuttier than Aszú. A gentler version of the same idea.

FAQ

Hungarian wine questions

What does puttonyos mean on a Tokaji label?

A puttony was a hod holding roughly 25 kilos of hand-picked, botrytis-shrivelled aszú berries, tipped into a 136-litre Gönc barrel of base wine. More baskets meant a sweeter wine. Since the 2013 vintage the three- and four-puttonyos grades have been dropped, Aszú must carry at least 120 grams of sugar per litre, and the number on the label is now optional.

Is all Tokaji sweet?

No. Aszú and the other botrytis wines made the region famous, but dry Furmint has become one of Tokaj's most talked-about wines over the last two decades. Dry Szamorodni is another dry style, aged under a film of yeast. As a rough guide, if the label says simply "Furmint" with no sweetness term, it is usually dry.

How is Tokaji Aszú made?

Pickers go through the vineyard selecting individual berries shrivelled by botrytis, or noble rot. Those berries are mashed into a paste and steeped in must or wine of the same vintage for up to a couple of days, then pressed. The wine ferments slowly and must spend at least eighteen months in wooden barrels before it can be released.

What food goes with Tokaji Aszú?

The two classic partners are foie gras and blue cheese such as Roquefort or Stilton, where the sweetness balances salt and richness while the acidity cuts through the fat. It also works with fruit-based desserts, especially apricot, apple or quince. Avoid anything sweeter than the wine itself, which flattens it and makes it taste dull.
