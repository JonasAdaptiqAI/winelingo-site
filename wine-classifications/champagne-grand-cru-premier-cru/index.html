Grand Cru and Premier Cru on Champagne

The rating belongs to the village, not the vineyard — which changes everything about how to read it.

In Champagne, Grand Cru is a postcode. The rating belongs to an entire village: 17 villages are Grand Cru, another forty-odd are Premier Cru, and every vine inside the parish boundary carries the same grade — the great south-facing chalk slope and the flat frost pocket at the bottom of the lane alike.

That is the reverse of Burgundy, where one walled hectare can be Grand Cru while the vines across the track are not. So a Grand Cru Champagne tells you which commune the fruit came from, and nothing whatever about which hillside it grew on.

The label rule is strict: “Grand Cru” may only appear if 100% of the grapes came from those 17 villages. The Premier Cru count is the loose one — reputable sources print 42 and 44 — which is a fair warning about how firm any of this is.

It was a pay scale, not a quality medal

The percentages were prices. A grower in a 100% village received the full reference price per kilo of grapes; a 90% village received 90% of it. The scale was drawn up in 1911, in the wake of the growers' revolt, and the first version ran all the way down to 22.5% — leaving roughly a quarter of the region's wine communes outside it altogether.

It no longer sets anything. Grape prices are negotiated between grower and house, and the ratings survive only as village names on labels. The words on the bottle describe a wage system that has been switched off.

Is it worth paying for?

Sometimes, and specifically. Cru status means most in blanc de blancs from the Côte des Blancs, where Chardonnay from Avize, Cramant, Oger and Le Mesnil-sur-Oger really does taste different — tighter, chalkier, longer on the finish. It means least as a general badge of quality, because the grade covers a whole commune and says nothing about the parcel, the yield or the grower.

The clearest evidence sits in the fine wine aisle. Several of the most admired Champagnes carry no cru mention at all, because they are blended across dozens of villages on purpose. Judge the producer first and the cru second, and read “Premier Cru” as a note about geography rather than a rung below Grand Cru.

Where the map simply runs out

Every one of the 17 Grand Cru villages lies in the Marne. The Côte des Bar, down in the Aube and home to a good number of the region's most interesting small growers, has no cru rating of any kind — not because the wine is worse, but because the scale was drawn while the Aube was still fighting to be admitted to Champagne at all. A century on, the map still shows you which villages had the better lawyers in 1911.

FAQ

Questions & answers

How many Grand Cru villages are there in Champagne?

Seventeen, all of them in the Marne. The Premier Cru tier below is usually given as 42 villages, though 44 is also published — the scale was a price agreement, not a tidy register.

Does Grand Cru Champagne taste better?

Not reliably. The rating covers an entire village rather than a single vineyard, so it tells you nothing about the specific parcel or the grower. It is most meaningful for Chardonnay from the Côte des Blancs.

Can a Champagne say Grand Cru if only some grapes qualify?

No. The term may only be used when 100% of the grapes come from Grand Cru villages. “Premier Cru” requires all the fruit to come from Premier Cru villages, or from a mix of Premier and Grand Cru.

Is the échelle des crus still used?

Not as a pricing system. Grape prices are now negotiated directly between growers and houses, but the Grand Cru and Premier Cru village names created by the scale are still permitted on labels.
