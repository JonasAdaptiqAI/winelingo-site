What “cuvée” means on a wine label

Grande Cuvée, Tête de Cuvée, Cuvée Spéciale — which of these are regulated? None of them.

Cuvée means a blend, or a batch — and on most labels it means nothing at all. There is no legal definition of Grande Cuvée, Tête de Cuvée, Cuvée Spéciale or Cuvée Réservée. Any producer may print any of them on any wine, at any price.

Which is not to say they are always empty. When Krug writes Grande Cuvée it is describing the most elaborate blend in Champagne. When a supermarket own-label writes Cuvée Spéciale it is describing a font choice.

The same vocabulary turns up on Crémant, on Cava and on £5 supermarket reds, with exactly the same legal force behind it: none.

The one place cuvée is a measurement

In Champagne the word is precise, and the precision has nothing to do with prestige. A press load — a marc — is 4,000kg of whole grapes, from which no more than 2,550 litres of juice may legally be taken. The first 2,050 litres are the cuvée. The next 500 are the taille.

They taste different. Cuvée juice is better balanced, higher in acid and finer in aroma; the taille is fruitier, deeper in colour and coarser, and many houses sell it off or keep it for lesser wines. So “100% cuvée” on the back of a Champagne is a genuine claim about what went in — and it is the only version of the word that is.

The prestige cuvées that earned the name

Krug Grande Cuvée is rebuilt every year from the house's library of reserve wines: each edition blends more than 120 separate wines from ten or more harvests, some of the reserves fifteen years old. Dom Pérignon belongs to Moët & Chandon; the first vintage was 1921 and it was not sold until 1936, and it is only ever made in a declared year. Cristal was created in 1876 for Tsar Alexander II in a clear, flat-bottomed lead-crystal bottle — clear so he could see what was in it, flat because a punt is somewhere to hide a bomb — and is likewise made only in declared years.

None of these needed a regulation. Reputation did the work — which is exactly why the words are free for anyone else to borrow.

Read these words instead

Plenty of French label terms are defined in law and therefore useful. The appellation itself. The vintage. The sweetness ladder from Brut Nature to Doux, which is fixed by grams of sugar per litre. Blanc de Blancs and Blanc de Noirs. And in Alsace and Burgundy — though not in Champagne — Grand Cru is a genuine vineyard classification.

The practical habit: when a bottle leans on a prestige word, look for the appellation next to it. If you cannot place the appellation, the cuvée language is doing the selling. FAQ

Questions & answers

Does cuvée mean the wine is better quality?

No. Cuvée simply means a blend or a batch, and it has no quality meaning in law. Producers use it freely at every price level.

What does Grande Cuvée mean on Champagne?

Literally “great blend”. It usually signals a house's flagship bottling — Krug Grande Cuvée is the famous example — but the term is unregulated, so any producer can use it.

What is a tête de cuvée?

Trade shorthand for a producer's very top wine, such as Dom Pérignon at Moët, or Cristal at Louis Roederer. The phrase itself rarely appears on the label.

What is the difference between cuvée and taille in Champagne?

They are the two legal fractions of the press. From 4,000kg of grapes, the first 2,050 litres of juice are the cuvée and the next 500 the taille. Cuvée juice is finer and better balanced.
