Wine classifications explained

The quality systems on the world’s labels — Grand Cru, Riserva, Prädikat, 1855 — decoded in plain language, so you know what the tier on the bottle actually means.

Burgundy

Burgundy, France · The vineyard — the land itself

Burgundy grades the LAND, not the producer. The better the plot, the more specific the label gets — and the more specific the label, the higher the quality.

Grand Cru
The top ~1%. So prized the label drops the village and names only the vineyard — ‘Montrachet’, ‘Chambertin’, ‘Romanée-Conti’. Tiny plots, legendary for 800+ years.
Premier Cru (1er Cru)
~10%. A specific superior vineyard plot within a village; the label reads village + plot, e.g. ‘Meursault 1er Cru Les Charmes’.
Village
~37%. Grapes from one named village — ‘Gevrey-Chambertin’, ‘Pouilly-Fuissé’. Better terroir, more character.
Regional (Bourgogne)
~50%. The base — grapes from anywhere in Burgundy; the label just says ‘Bourgogne Rouge’ or ‘Bourgogne Blanc’.
Key insight. The more specific the label, the higher the quality. A label saying just ‘Bourgogne’ = the whole region (entry level); a label saying just ‘Montrachet’ = one single hillside (the summit).

Bordeaux (1855)

Bordeaux (Médoc), France · Châteaux — the producers

Bordeaux’s famous 1855 Classification ranks the top Médoc estates (châteaux), not the vineyards, into five ‘Growth’ tiers.

Premier Cru (First Growth)
The five legends: Lafite, Latour, Margaux, Haut-Brion, and Mouton Rothschild.
2nd–5th Growth (Deuxième–Cinquième Cru)
The next four tiers of classified Médoc châteaux from the 1855 ranking.
Cru Bourgeois
A separate, lower classification sitting below the 1855 list.
Unclassified Bordeaux
Everything else — often excellent value.
Key insight. TRAP: ‘Premier Cru’ in Bordeaux means one of the top 5 estates in the world. In Burgundy the very same words mean the SECOND-highest tier. Same name, opposite meaning.

Saint-Émilion

Saint-Émilion, Bordeaux, France · Châteaux — its own system, separate from 1855

Saint-Émilion (Right Bank Bordeaux) runs its own classification — and the naming is a famous trap.

Premier Grand Cru Classé A
The summit — e.g. Cheval Blanc.
Premier Grand Cru Classé B
The next tier of top estates.
Grand Cru Classé
Classified estates below the ‘Premier’ levels.
Grand Cru
TRAP: despite the grand name, this is the BOTTOM tier of the classification.
Key insight. ‘Grand Cru’ sounds top-tier, but in Saint-Émilion it’s the lowest rung. The real summit is ‘Premier Grand Cru Classé A’.

Champagne

Champagne, France · The villages where the grapes grow (the échelle des crus)

Champagne grades the VILLAGES on a scale called the échelle des crus, based on grape quality.

Grand Cru
17 villages rated 100%. ‘Grand Cru Champagne’ means 100% of the grapes come from these top villages.
Premier Cru
42 villages rated 90–99%.
Other villages
Rated 80–89%.
Key insight. Most non-vintage Champagne (including standard Moët and Veuve Clicquot) is a blend across many villages and carries no cru label at all. See also ‘Grande Cuvée & prestige terms’.

Grande Cuvée & prestige terms

Champagne, France · Marketing terms — NOT a regulated classification

These prestige words signal a producer’s intent, not an official quality tier. Cuvée just means ‘a blend or batch’.

Cuvée
Literally ‘a specific blend or batch’. Every Champagne house makes several.
Grande Cuvée
‘Great blend’ — usually a house’s flagship/prestige bottling (e.g. Krug Grande Cuvée, blended from 100+ wines across 10+ vintages). But it has NO legal definition; any house can use it.
Tête de Cuvée
‘Head of the blend’ — informal for a house’s very top wine. Dom Pérignon is Moët’s; Cristal is Roederer’s. The words rarely appear on the label.
Key insight. Unlike ‘Grand Cru’, ‘Grande Cuvée’ is not regulated. It signals premium intent from the producer — not a classified quality tier.

Chianti Classico

Tuscany (Chianti Classico zone, between Florence and Siena), Italy · Aging tiers — longer minimum aging (and a touch more alcohol) marks a higher tier

Chianti Classico is a Sangiovese-based DOCG red from Tuscany, sold in three quality tiers that climb with required aging. Annata is the everyday bottling, Riserva is aged longer, and Gran Selezione sits at the top — estate-grown only, longest-aged, and now built from at least 90% Sangiovese.

Gran Selezione (top)
The flagship tier: only fruit from the producer’s own estate vineyards, the longest aging, and — under the updated rules — a minimum 90% Sangiovese with the rest filled only by native grapes (no Cabernet or Merlot).
Riserva
A step up from the base wine, held back longer before release for added depth and structure.
Annata (base)
The standard ‘vintage’ bottling and the freshest, most approachable expression — what most people picture as Chianti Classico.
Key insight. The tiers signal minimum aging and pedigree, not necessarily your personal preference — a fresh, fruit-forward Annata can be the perfect everyday pour, while Gran Selezione is the estate-grown, longest-aged top rung. One trap to remember: the recent rule change raised Gran Selezione’s Sangiovese minimum to 90% and banned international grapes like Cabernet and Merlot from it, so newer Gran Selezione is even more purely Sangiovese than older bottles.

Brunello di Montalcino

Montalcino, Tuscany, Italy · Aging tiers — longer aging = higher tier (all 100% Sangiovese)

Three tiers from the same Montalcino hills, all made from 100% Sangiovese, separated mainly by how long the wine must age before release. More time in oak and bottle means a higher tier — and a bigger, age-worthy wine.

Brunello di Montalcino Riserva
The top tier — made only in the best vintages and held back even longer than standard Brunello for added depth and longevity.
Brunello di Montalcino
The classic, powerful, age-worthy wine — DOCG rules require a long maturation before it can be sold.
Rosso di Montalcino
The younger, fresher, more affordable sibling — same grape and zone, but ready to drink much sooner.
Key insight. All three are 100% Sangiovese from the same place — the tier is about aging time, not grape or quality of fruit. The big trap: Rosso is not a lesser wine, just a younger, earlier-release version, while Brunello and Riserva are legally required to age for years (Brunello can’t be sold until its 5th year after harvest; Riserva its 6th).

Barolo & Barbaresco

Piedmont (Langhe), Italy · Aging tiers — longer mandatory aging = higher tier

Both wines are made from 100% Nebbiolo in neighboring Piedmont zones, and the tiers are defined by how long the wine must age before release — including a minimum stretch in wood. Barolo is aged longer than Barbaresco, and each gets a longer-aged Riserva version at the top.

Barolo Riserva
The longest-aged tier — a Barolo held back for over five years before it can be sold, typically the most structured and age-worthy.
Barolo
Standard Barolo: just over three years of required aging, with at least a year and a half in wood before release.
Barbaresco Riserva
Barbaresco’s reserve tier — held back roughly four years, longer than standard Barolo but with less wood time than the Barolo rules require.
Barbaresco
The entry tier here: same Nebbiolo grape as Barolo but released sooner, so it’s often a touch more approachable when young.
Key insight. The tier order isn’t about quality of grape — both are 100% Nebbiolo. It’s about mandated aging time: Barolo must age longer than Barbaresco, and Riserva versions longest of all. Don’t assume “Barbaresco = lesser wine”; it’s just released earlier and from a different (slightly warmer, earlier-ripening) zone.

Rioja (aging tiers)

Rioja, Spain · Aging tiers — longer aging (especially in oak barrel) = higher tier

In Rioja, the tier on the label tells you how long the wine was aged, not how good or how expensive it is — more total time, and more time in oak barrels, moves a wine up the ladder. The numbers below are the legal minimums for RED wines; whites and rosés have shorter requirements.

Gran Reserva
The longest-aged tier, made only in better vintages — expect mellow, savoury, leathery flavours from years of aging.
Reserva
A step up in concentration and oak influence, balancing fresh fruit with aged complexity.
Crianza
The entry point for oak-aged Rioja — approachable, with gentle vanilla and spice from a year in barrel.
Genérico / Joven
Fresh, fruit-forward, little or no oak; ‘joven’ means young, and ‘genérico’ is the catch-all for wines that don’t meet a higher aging tier.
Key insight. The tier is about TIME (especially time in oak), not quality or price — a great young Joven can outshine a tired Gran Reserva. Also remember these words (Crianza, Reserva, Gran Reserva) are used across much of Spain, not just Rioja, and the aging numbers shown here are the minimums for RED wine — whites and rosés need less.

Valpolicella & Amarone

Veneto, Italy · Style & richness ladder — same Corvina blend, different grape-drying method

Four wines from one Corvina-based blend, set apart mostly by how the grapes are treated: fresh juice for light Valpolicella, re-fermenting on Amarone skins for Ripasso, and partially raisined (“appassimento”) grapes for the powerful, dry Amarone and the sweet Recioto. More drying and concentration means a richer, higher-alcohol wine, so the ladder is really about method, not aging months.

Amarone della Valpolicella (DOCG)
The flagship: grapes are air-dried to raisins before pressing, then fermented all the way out, giving a dry, powerful, full-bodied red.
Recioto della Valpolicella (DOCG)
Amarone’s sweet ancestor — the same raisined grapes, but fermentation is halted so natural grape sugar remains, making a rich dessert red.
Valpolicella Ripasso (DOC) / Superiore
A middle step often called ‘baby Amarone’: ordinary Valpolicella is refermented on the spent Amarone skins to pick up extra body, color and warmth.
Valpolicella (DOC)
The entry point: light, fresh and cherry-fruited, made from the same blend but with no concentration step — easy everyday drinking.
Key insight. The ladder is set by grape-drying, not the grape — all four are mostly Corvina. The big trap: Amarone and Recioto use the SAME dried grapes; the only difference is sweetness (Amarone is fermented bone-dry and high in alcohol, Recioto is stopped sweet). And “Ripasso” earns its richness from being re-passed over Amarone skins, not from drying.

Beaujolais

Beaujolais, France · Place tiers — more specific place = higher tier

Beaujolais is a region in southern Burgundy, France, making mostly light, juicy reds from the Gamay grape. Its three tiers climb by geographic specificity, not by aging: the tighter the named place on the label, the more serious and ageworthy the wine.

Cru Beaujolais
The top tier: 10 individually named hillside villages making the most structured, ageworthy Gamay — several can improve for 5–10+ years.
Beaujolais-Villages
The middle tier — a step up in concentration from base bottlings, blended from a defined group of better-sited villages.
Beaujolais
The entry tier: light, fruity, everyday Gamay meant to be drunk young and slightly chilled.
Key insight. Beaujolais Nouveau is NOT a quality tier — it’s a separate style of young wine released the third Thursday of November, just weeks after harvest. Don’t confuse the fun, simple Nouveau with the serious, ageworthy Cru wines that sit at the top of the ladder.

German Prädikat

Mosel, Rheingau & other German wine regions, Germany · Ripeness tiers — riper grapes at harvest = higher tier

German Prädikat ranks wines by how ripe the grapes were at harvest (their must weight, measured in degrees Öchsle), not by how sweet the finished wine tastes. The bottom rungs are everyday wines that can be bone dry, while the top rungs are rare, intensely concentrated dessert wines.

Trockenbeerenauslese (TBA)
The pinnacle: individually picked, raisined grapes give an extraordinarily sweet, honeyed, age-worthy dessert wine made in tiny quantities.
Eiswein
Picked and pressed while frozen so the water stays as ice, concentrating a piercingly sweet, high-acid nectar.
Beerenauslese (BA)
A rich, sweet dessert wine from individually selected nobly-rotten or overripe berries — rare and made only in great vintages.
Auslese
From specially selected ripe bunches; often off-dry to sweet, but can be made dry — a step up in richness and intensity.
Spätlese
Literally “late harvest” — riper grapes than Kabinett, yet it can be made bone dry (look for “trocken” on the label).
Kabinett
The base rung: light, crisp wines from normally-ripe grapes, ranging from dry to lightly off-dry and low in alcohol.
Key insight. The ladder measures grape RIPENESS at harvest, not sweetness in the glass. A Spätlese or Kabinett can be completely dry — look for the word “trocken” (dry) on the label. The exact Öchsle minimums also vary by region and grape variety, so treat the numbers as typical ranges, not universal cutoffs.

Port styles

Douro Valley, Portugal · Style + aging tiers — fortified sweet wine, two aging paths

Port is a sweet, fortified red wine from Portugal’s Douro Valley, made in two broad families: fruity Ruby styles aged mostly in bottle, and nutty Tawny styles aged for years in barrel. Within each family, longer or more selective aging generally means a higher, more prized tier.

Vintage Port
The pinnacle Ruby style: only made in exceptional (‘declared’) years from the best grapes, bottled young, and meant to age in your cellar for 20+ years where it throws sediment and turns silky.
Late Bottled Vintage (LBV)
A more affordable, ready-to-drink take on Vintage: from one harvest but kept longer in cask, so it’s usually filtered and drinkable on release with no decades of waiting.
Tawny with Age Indication (10 / 20 / 30 / 40 Year)
The flagship Tawny style: years in oak fade the color to amber and build flavors of caramel, nuts and dried fig — the bigger the number, the older and more concentrated the blend.
Tawny / Reserve Tawny
An entry barrel-aged Port: golden-brown and gently nutty, softer and mellower than Ruby without the price of an age-stated Tawny.
Ruby / Reserve Ruby
The everyday entry point: deep red, bold and grapey with fresh berry and chocolate flavors, made to drink now rather than age.
Key insight. Two families, two aging paths: Ruby styles (Ruby → Reserve → LBV → Vintage) age in the BOTTLE and stay red and fruity; Tawny styles age in the BARREL and turn amber and nutty. The trap is reading a Tawny’s ‘10/20/30/40 Year’ as the wine’s actual age — it’s the average age of the blend, not how long that specific bottle sat.
