The gentle soul of southern blends

Cinsault (sometimes spelled Cinsaut) is one of the unsung workhorses of the warm south of France — a pale, perfumed, low-tannin red built for charm rather than power.

For most of its life it has played a supporting role, softening and adding fragrance to blends in the Rhône, Languedoc and Provence, and forming the backbone of countless pale, refreshing rosés.

But it has a famous claim to fame: cross Cinsault with Pinot Noir and you get Pinotage, South Africa’s signature grape. And lately, sommeliers have rediscovered old-vine Cinsault as a delicious, light-and-juicy red in its own right — exactly the kind of easy-drinking style modern wine lovers crave.

What it tastes like

Think light, fresh and floral. Cinsault is pale in colour, low in tannin, and built around bright red fruit with a pretty, perfumed lift — easy to drink and easy to love.

It rarely tries to be serious or powerful; its appeal is juiciness, fragrance and gulpability. Often served slightly chilled.

• Aromas & flavours: strawberry, raspberry, red cherry, cranberry, dried rose petals, potpourri, a hint of white pepper and sometimes a savoury, herbal edge • Texture: soft, light-bodied and supple — almost the red-wine equivalent of a refreshing summer drink

Styles to know

Rosé: A classic ingredient in the pale, dry, fragrant rosés of Provence and the wider south of France. Its low colour and delicate red fruit are ideal for that style.

Light red blends: A traditional softening partner for Grenache, Syrah and Carignan in Rhône and Languedoc reds, adding perfume and suppleness to firmer grapes.

Single-varietal “glou-glou” reds: The exciting modern story. Old-vine Cinsault (especially from South Africa) makes light, juicy, fragrant reds — often served chilled — that have become darlings of the natural-wine and sommelier scene. (“Glou-glou” is wine slang for an easy, gluggable, drink-it-fast style.)

Where it shines

• Southern Rhône & Languedoc, France — a traditional blending grape across the warm south, lending fragrance and softness to bigger reds and rosés. • Provence, France — a key component in the famous pale dry rosés. • South Africa — home to prized old, gnarled Cinsault vines that produce some of the most exciting single-varietal examples in the world; the grape is also one parent of Pinotage. • Lebanon — a long-standing player in the reds of the Bekaa Valley (a classic component of Château Musar).

Cinsault thrives in heat and drought, which is why it’s a fixture in hot, dry, sun-soaked regions.

What shapes it

Old vines matter: The best single-varietal Cinsault comes from old, low-yielding vines (“old bush vines” in South Africa). Older vines naturally produce fewer, more concentrated grapes, turning a humble grape into something genuinely characterful.

Carbonic maceration: Many of the juicy modern styles use this technique — fermenting whole, uncrushed grapes in a sealed, carbon-dioxide-rich tank. It boosts vivid fruit and floral aromas while keeping tannins low, perfect for that light, gluggable profile.

Climate: A heat- and drought-lover, it ripens reliably in hot southern regions — but its joy is always lightness and perfume rather than power, however warm the site.

Good to know

Food: Endlessly versatile precisely because it’s light. Charcuterie, grilled vegetables, roast chicken, salmon, and Mediterranean dishes all work. The rosés are summer all-rounders.

Serving: Serve it cool — 12-14°C, lightly chilled — which suits both the reds and rosés and shows off the fresh fruit.

Value: Old-vine bottlings aside, everyday Cinsault and Cinsault-based rosés are easy on the wallet and made for casual drinking.

Good to know: Don’t expect a big, bold red. Cinsault’s joy is in its lightness and perfume — it’s a wine for refreshment and pleasure, not pondering.

Food pairing. Light, perfumed and easygoing, it loves grilled vegetables, charcuterie and herby Mediterranean fare. Its red-berry and floral lift sings with thyme, olive and a squeeze of lemon.
