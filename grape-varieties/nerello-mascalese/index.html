The overview

Nerello Mascalese is the grape that has turned Mount Etna into one of the wine world’s most talked-about regions. Grown on the slopes of an active volcano in eastern Sicily, it produces pale, perfumed, high-acid reds that have drawn endless (and not unfair) comparisons to Burgundy and Barolo.

The name nods to the town of Mascali, on Etna’s eastern slopes, where it was prized. For most of its history it was a quietly local, blending-and-bulk grape. Then, from the early 2000s, a wave of pioneering growers realised that Etna’s old, ungrafted, high-altitude vines were sitting on a goldmine — and Nerello Mascalese became a global cult.

Its character: Light to medium in colour and body, fragrant and red-fruited, with bright acidity, fine but real tannin, and — crucially — a smoky, mineral, almost ashy volcanic signature. Elegant rather than powerful; about perfume and tension, not weight.

If you love delicate, savoury, terroir-driven reds — Pinot Noir, Nebbiolo, cru Beaujolais — Etna’s Nerello is squarely in your wheelhouse.

Styles to know

Etna Rosso (the icon): The flagship. By law a minimum of 80% Nerello Mascalese, with up to 20% of the darker, plumper Nerello Cappuccio allowed. Pale, perfumed, high-acid, with red cherry, dried herbs, blood orange and that smoky volcanic mineral note.

Single-contrada bottlings: Etna is mapped into “contrade” — individual named vineyard districts, much like Burgundy’s climats. Each gives a subtly different expression of soil, altitude and lava flow. Hunting these down is half the fun for enthusiasts.

Etna Rosato: Increasingly fashionable — a pale, mineral, savoury rosé with real character and grip.

Sparkling: Some producers make traditional-method fizz from Nerello, taking advantage of its high acidity.

Aromas & flavours: red cherry, cranberry, blood orange, dried rose, thyme, wild herbs, smoke, ash, crushed stone, a saline-mineral edge.

What shapes it

Volcanic soils: Etna’s vineyards sit on layers of weathered lava, volcanic ash and black sand. These free-draining mineral soils are widely credited for the smoky, stony, almost salty character in the glass — the famous volcanic “minerality.”

Altitude: Vines climb from around 400m to over 1,000m on the mountain’s slopes. High altitude means big day-to-night temperature swings, which preserve acidity and aromatic freshness even under the Sicilian sun. This is why a southern wine can taste so cool and tense.

Old, ungrafted vines: Phylloxera, the root louse that devastated Europe’s vineyards, struggles to survive in loose, sandy soil — and Etna’s volcanic sands gave many vines a refuge. As a result they were never grafted onto American rootstock and still grow on their own original roots (“piede franco”), some of them extremely old. These ancient alberello (bush-trained) vines give concentrated, characterful fruit.

Contrada by contrada: Different lava flows of different ages and altitudes produce measurably different wines — the basis for Etna’s Burgundian obsession with single-vineyard sites.

A gentle hand: Because the grape is naturally pale and perfumed, the best growers favour restrained winemaking and older or larger oak, so as not to bury its delicate aromatics.

Good to know

”The Barolo of the South.” You’ll hear this often — and the comparison holds: firm structure hiding behind a pale colour, high acid, savoury perfume, a strong sense of place. It also recalls red Burgundy in its delicacy.

Food: Its freshness and savoury edge make it hugely versatile — roast chicken, pork, mushroom dishes, tuna and swordfish (Sicilian classics), tomato-based pasta, medium cheeses. A red that can even handle some fish.

Ageing: The best Etna Rosso ages gracefully for a decade or more, gaining dried-fruit, earthy and balsamic complexity.

A young region rediscovered. Much of Etna’s modern fame is barely two decades old, which means there’s still real discovery — and occasionally real value — to be found.

Food pairing. This elegant Etna grape is wonderfully food-friendly and loves medium-intensity dishes like roast pork, mushroom risotto, and charcuterie. Make pairings pop with red cherry, volcanic minerality, and a touch of herbs.

Producers to seek out for Nerello Mascalese

For Nerello Mascalese, head to the volcanic slopes of Mount Etna in Sicily, where growers bottle single contrade (vineyard districts) that show the mountain’s terroir in fine detail.

1

Tenuta delle Terre Nere

Marco de Grazia’s Etna estate, a pioneer of single-contrada Etna Rosso

2

Benanti

Etna standard-bearer, with Rovittello and Serra della Contessa

3

Passopisciaro

Andrea Franchetti’s north-slope estate with a range of single-contrada bottlings

4

Graci

organically farmed Etna, notably Contrada Arcuria on the northern slope

5

Girolamo Russo

graceful Etna Rosso from contrade around Passopisciaro, such as San Lorenzo
