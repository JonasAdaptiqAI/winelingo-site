Look for racy, mouth-watering acidity like Riesling, but paired with a fuller, slightly waxy or lanolin texture that gives the wine more weight on the palate. A dry Chenin (Vouvray or Savennières) shows quince, apple and a touch of honey even when there is no sweetness, so trust the sharp finish to tell you it’s dry. Because it ranges from bone-dry to dessert-sweet, always check the label or producer for the style rather than assuming.

The most versatile grape you’ve never heard of

Chenin Blanc might be wine’s best-kept secret. Few grapes can do so many things so well: it makes bone-dry whites, lightly sweet wines, lusciously sweet dessert wines, and sparkling — sometimes from the very same vineyard, depending on the vintage.

Two things tie all those styles together: vivid, mouth-watering acidity and a core of orchard fruit, honey and quince. That high natural acidity is its secret weapon — it keeps even the sweet versions feeling fresh rather than cloying.

It’s the signature white of France’s Loire Valley and the most-planted white grape in South Africa, yet it flies under the radar — which often makes it a value goldmine.

Styles to know

Chenin’s range is its defining trait. The same grape can land anywhere on this spectrum:

Dry: Crisp and textured, with apple, pear, quince and a chalky, mineral edge. South African “Steen” and dry Vouvray/Savennières fit here.

Off-dry (lightly sweet): A touch of sweetness balanced by zippy acidity — approachable and food-friendly. Many Vouvrays sit here; the label may say “sec” (dry), “demi-sec” (off-dry) or “moelleux” (medium-sweet).

Lusciously sweet: From grapes affected by “noble rot” (a beneficial mould that shrivels grapes and concentrates sugar), making honeyed, apricot-rich dessert wines that can age for decades. The richest of these are labelled “doux.”

Sparkling: Crisp, appley and excellent value — the Loire’s answer to Champagne (look for Crémant de Loire).

• Aromas & flavours: green apple, pear, quince, chamomile, honey, ginger, wet wool/lanolin, and apricot or marmalade when sweet

Where it shines

• Loire Valley, France — the spiritual home. Vouvray (every sweetness level, plus sparkling), Savennières (dry, mineral, age-worthy and serious), and the great sweet wines of Coteaux du Layon and Quarts de Chaume. • South Africa — the world’s largest planting, known locally as “Steen.” Ranges from easy everyday whites to ambitious, barrel-aged, old-vine bottlings that rival the Loire. • Elsewhere — California, Argentina, Australia and New Zealand make smaller amounts, often in a fresh, dry style.

The Loire shows its mineral, age-worthy side; South Africa often shows a riper, sun-filled fruitiness.

What shapes it & a note on sweetness

Reading the sweetness: This is the one tricky thing about Chenin, because the label runs across four distinct French tiers: sec (dry) → demi-sec (off-dry) → moelleux (medium- to lusciously sweet) → doux (the sweetest, often botrytised). When in doubt, ask — or check the alcohol (very low alcohol often signals leftover sweetness).

Noble rot (botrytis): The “good” mould behind the great sweet styles. It shrivels grapes on the vine and concentrates their sugar and flavour into honeyed, apricot-laden nectar.

Oak & lees ageing: Top dry Chenin (Savennières, premium South African) is often barrel-fermented and aged on its lees (spent yeast), adding a richer, waxy, textured feel without masking the grape’s acidity.

That “wet wool” note: Don’t be alarmed — a lanolin or wet-wool character (sometimes called honeyed or waxy) is a classic, prized Chenin signature, not a flaw.

Good to know

Food: Its acidity makes it a food chameleon. Dry styles pair with roast chicken, pork, creamy cheeses and richer fish; off-dry versions are a brilliant, classic match for spicy Thai and Indian food (a hint of sweetness tames the heat). Sweet styles love blue cheese and fruit desserts.

Ageing: Both the dry and sweet versions are among the longest-lived whites in the world — that high acidity is a natural preservative. Great Loire Chenin can evolve beautifully for decades.

Value: Genuinely one of wine’s great-value grapes, especially from South Africa. Because it’s underrated, quality often outruns price.

Serving: Dry and sparkling at 8-10°C; richer dry and sweet styles a touch warmer, 9-12°C, to let the honeyed aromas show.

Food pairing. A chameleon that works dry or off-dry, suiting roast chicken, pork and spicy Thai dishes. Its quince, honey and apple notes shine alongside ginger, lemongrass and butter.

Producers to seek out for Chenin Blanc

For Chenin Blanc at its finest, explore the Loire’s Vouvray and Savennières, then South Africa’s old bush-vine vineyards around Stellenbosch and the Swartland.

1

Domaine Huet

Vouvray’s standard-bearer, from dry sec to sweet moelleux.

2

Nicolas Joly

Coulée de Serrant, biodynamic Savennières from a historic monopole.

3

François Chidaine

Vouvray and Montlouis Chenin of vivid purity.

4

Ken Forrester

Stellenbosch’s King of Chenin, from old bush vines.

5

The Sadie Family

Skurfberg, old-vine Swartland Chenin of remarkable depth.
