Why Xinomavro matters

Xinomavro (ksee-NOH-mah-vroh) is northern Greece’s noble red — and the grape most often compared to Nebbiolo, the variety behind Italy’s Barolo. The name literally means ‘acid-black’ in Greek, which tells you almost everything: high acidity, firm tannin, and a paradoxically pale colour for such a powerful, structured wine.

Like Nebbiolo, it trades fruit power for perfume and grip. Young examples can be austere and demanding; with age they unfold into something complex and savoury that wine lovers chase.

Aromas & flavours: red cherry, dried tomato (a true signature), black olive, dried herbs, leather, spice and a smoky, almost sun-dried character.

It’s not a soft crowd-pleaser — it’s a serious, age-worthy red that has put Greece firmly on the fine-wine map and offers Barolo-like thrills at a far gentler price.

Styles to know

Xinomavro is shaped heavily by how it’s grown and made, so styles vary more than the single grape suggests.

Traditional & structured: Pale but tannic and high-acid, aged in barrel and built to soften over a decade or more. Austere when young, magnificent with time — the classic, age-demanding style.

Modern & approachable: A new generation picks riper, manages the tannins more gently and uses oak more subtly, making reds you can enjoy younger without losing the grape’s savoury soul.

Rosé & sparkling: Its high acidity makes excellent dry rosé and even traditional-method sparkling — fresh, tangy and food-friendly.

Blends: Sometimes softened with a splash of riper local or international grapes to round out its sharp edges.

The constant across all styles is that tomato-and-olive savouriness and a mouth-watering acidity that screams for food.

Where it shines

Northern Greece is the whole story, and the appellations have distinct accents.

• Naoussa (Macedonia, Greece) — the benchmark. 100% Xinomavro by law; powerful, tannic and the most age-worthy expression.

• Amyndeo — higher and cooler, giving lighter, more elegant and red-fruited wines, plus excellent rosé and sparkling.

• Rapsani — on the slopes of Mount Olympus, where Xinomavro is blended with local Krassato and Stavroto for a softer, rounder result.

Best examples: Boutari, Thymiopoulos, Kir-Yianni, Alpha Estate, Markovitis.

Naoussa for the full Barolo-like experience, Amyndeo for charm and freshness — tasting both is the fastest way to understand the grape’s range.

Good to know

Patience pays — or decant. Young traditional Xinomavro can be tight and tannic. Give a good Naoussa several years in the cellar, or decant it an hour ahead to let it open. With age it develops gorgeous dried-fruit, tomato and leather complexity.

Food is essential. That high acidity and firm tannin make it a poor sipper but a brilliant table wine — lamb, slow-cooked stews, tomato-based dishes, aged cheeses and anything with savoury, sun-dried Mediterranean flavours.

The Nebbiolo parallel: Both are pale, high-acid, high-tannin and aromatic rather than fruity, and both reward ageing. If you love Barolo or Barbaresco, Xinomavro is your next obsession — at a fraction of the cost.

Don’t judge by colour. Its light hue fools people into expecting a delicate wine; the tannin and structure underneath tell a very different story.

Food pairing. Xinomavro is firm and tannic, so it loves rich, slow-cooked dishes like lamb, braised beef, and mushroom stews, with its tomato, dried-cherry, olive, and dried-herb accents shining alongside hearty Mediterranean fare.
