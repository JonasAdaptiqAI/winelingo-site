Smell it before you sip: Viognier hits you with ripe apricot and floral perfume that few other dry whites match. On the palate notice how round, soft, and full it feels with little of the mouth-watering tartness you get from Sauvignon Blanc or Riesling. Think of it as a richer, more aromatic alternative to an oaked Chardonnay, with the body to match but its own stone-fruit signature.

Why Viognier matters

Viognier is the most seductive of the great whites — a grape that smells like a bouquet and a fruit bowl at the same time. At its best it’s intensely aromatic, full-bodied and almost oily in texture, yet bone dry. There’s no other white quite like it.

Its spiritual home is the tiny appellation of Condrieu in France’s northern Rhône, where it nearly went extinct in the 1960s (down to a few acres) before a global revival. Today it’s grown worldwide, from California to Australia to southern France.

Aromas & flavours: apricot, peach, honeysuckle, orange blossom, ginger, a waxy honeyed richness.

The catch: it’s naturally low in acidity, so it relies on perfume, body and texture rather than crispness. That makes it gloriously hedonistic when ripe — and flabby when handled badly, which is why producer choice matters so much.

Styles to know

Viognier walks a line between flamboyant and overdone, and winemaking decides which side it lands on.

Pure & aromatic (Condrieu-style): Full-bodied, dry, exploding with apricot and floral notes. Often partly fermented or aged in oak for texture, but the best keep the oak invisible so the perfume leads.

Oaked & rich: New-World versions sometimes pile on new oak, giving vanilla and a heavier, sometimes blowsy wine. Riper and more obvious — splits opinion.

Lighter, fresher: Cooler sites and earlier picking give a more restrained, peachy style with better acidity.

As a blending partner: In the northern Rhône, a small splash of Viognier is co-fermented with the red grape Syrah in Côte-Rôtie. Counterintuitively, adding a white grape to a red stabilises colour and adds a haunting floral lift — a classic trick worth knowing.

Where it shines

The geography tells you what to expect in the glass.

• Condrieu (northern Rhône, France) — the benchmark. Pure varietal Viognier, dry, profound, ageable and expensive.

• Château-Grillet — a tiny, legendary appellation in its own right, an enclave entirely surrounded by Condrieu but legally a separate AOC; one of France’s smallest standalone appellations, made solely from Viognier.

• Languedoc & southern France — much more affordable, often labelled by variety; ripe, friendly and a great place to meet the grape.

• California (Central Coast) — ripe, full and floral, sometimes oak-driven.

• Australia (Eden Valley, Yarra) — increasingly refined and balanced, plus the famous Shiraz-Viognier co-ferments.

Best examples: E. Guigal, Yves Cuilleron, Georges Vernay (Condrieu); Yalumba (Australia).

Start in the Languedoc for value, then trade up to a true Condrieu to taste what the fuss is about.

Good to know

Serve it right. Because acidity is low, over-chilling makes Viognier taste dull and heavy. Serve only lightly chilled (around 10–12°C) and use a larger glass so the aromatics can open up.

Drink most of it young. The exuberant perfume that makes Viognier special fades with age. A few top Condrieus can develop, but as a rule, enjoy it within 2–4 years to catch it at its most aromatic.

Food: Its richness and stone-fruit/floral profile pair brilliantly with aromatic dishes — Thai and Indian-spiced food, roast pork, apricot-glazed chicken, soft and washed-rind cheeses.

Common confusion: Despite the similar look on the page, Viognier is unrelated to Vouvray (a place, made from Chenin Blanc). If a wine smells overwhelmingly of apricot and flowers, Viognier is your best guess.

Food pairing. Viognier is lush and aromatic, suiting roast chicken, mild curries, and creamy seafood. Its peachy richness blooms with apricot, honeysuckle, and a pinch of ginger.

Producers to seek out for Viognier

Viognier reaches its aromatic peak in Condrieu on the northern Rhône, and that steep granite slope is where to seek it out.
