Spain’s zesty white star

Verdejo (ver-DAY-ho) is Spain’s most exciting native white grape — fresh, herbaceous and full of personality. Once nearly forgotten and used for heavy, oxidised wines, it was revived in the 1970s into the crisp, modern style we love today.

Think of it as a Spanish cousin to Sauvignon Blanc: zesty and green, but with a distinctive nutty, almost bitter twist on the finish that’s all its own.

It has a lovely textural quality — a slight grip and weight that sets it apart from simpler whites.

Aromas & flavours: lime, green apple, white peach, fennel, fresh-cut grass and a signature note of bitter almond or laurel.

Styles to know

Verdejo offers more variety than most people expect from a crisp white.

Fresh & unoaked: the standard, and the reason for its fame. Bright, aromatic, herbaceous and zesty, made in steel tanks (which preserve purity and add no flavour). Drink young and cold.

Lees-aged / textured: some producers rest the wine on its lees (the spent yeast left after fermentation) for added body, a creamy richness and savoury depth, while keeping the freshness. More serious and complex.

Oak-aged (traditional & rare): a small number of producers barrel-age Verdejo for a richer, nuttier, age-worthy style that nods to its historic roots.

Sparkling and blends: it also appears in Spanish sparkling wines and is sometimes blended with Sauvignon Blanc or Viura.

Where it shines

Verdejo is closely tied to one Spanish region above all.

• Rueda (Castilla y León) — its heartland and the benchmark. High-altitude vineyards on a stony plateau, with hot days and cold nights that build aroma while preserving the crucial acidity. Verdejo from Rueda is the gold standard.

• Increasingly planted elsewhere in Spain, and with small experimental plots abroad, but Rueda remains overwhelmingly its home.

If a label says “Rueda,” it’s very likely Verdejo (the region requires a high proportion of it), so the place name is a handy shortcut to the grape.

What shapes it

Altitude and big day-night swings. Rueda’s high, continental plateau gives scorching days and cold nights. The warmth ripens the fruit and builds aroma; the cold nights lock in the bright acidity that makes the wine so refreshing.

Stony soils. Rueda’s poor, pebbly ground stresses the vine just enough to concentrate flavour.

Lees ageing. Resting the wine on its spent yeast adds a creamy texture and savoury, nutty depth without masking the fresh fruit — a popular way to give Verdejo more interest.

Protecting freshness. Because Verdejo oxidises (browns and tires) easily, modern winemaking handles it carefully and cool, often picking at night to keep it crisp and vibrant.

Good to know

Food: wonderfully versatile — grilled fish and seafood, green salads, goat cheese, gazpacho and lighter poultry. Its herbal lift loves anything with fresh herbs or a squeeze of lemon.

Serving: drink it well chilled and generally young, while its aromatics are at their most vivid.

Value: Rueda Verdejo is one of the best-value crisp whites around — characterful, food-friendly and rarely expensive.

Good to know: if you love Sauvignon Blanc but want something with a little more texture and a savoury, nutty edge, Verdejo is your wine — familiar enough to enjoy instantly, distinctive enough to keep exploring.

Food pairing. Verdejo is zesty and versatile, loving grilled fish, tapas, and goat cheese. Its herbal, citrus character pops with green apple, fennel, and a squeeze of lime.

For zesty, herb-scented Verdejo, look to Rueda in Castilla y León — from crisp everyday bottles to serious lees-aged wines built to age.
