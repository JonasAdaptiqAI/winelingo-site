Why Vermentino matters

Vermentino is the white of the Mediterranean coast — Sardinia, Liguria, Tuscany and across the water in Corsica and southern France, where it’s also called Rolle. If a wine tastes like a seaside lunch, it’s probably this grape.

Its signature is a combination you rarely get together: aromatic and floral like a perfumed white, but with a salty, almost briny tang and a faint bitter twist on the finish. That salinity makes it feel like it was grown to be drunk with the sea breeze, which it essentially was.

Aromas & flavours: lime, grapefruit, green apple, white flowers, fresh herbs, crushed almond, sea salt.

It’s medium-bodied with good acidity and just enough texture to feel substantial — the kind of white that converts people who think they only like reds.

Styles to know

Most Vermentino is made in one broad style, with meaningful regional shading.

Crisp & coastal (the norm): Unoaked, fermented in steel to protect its aromatics. Zesty citrus, herbs and that saline snap. Best drunk young and cold within a year or two of the vintage.

Fuller & textured: Some Sardinian and Ligurian producers leave the wine on its lees (spent yeast) for extra body and a subtle creaminess, or use large old barrels that add weight without obvious oak flavour. These feel rounder and more serious.

Skin-contact / orange: A growing niche leaves the juice in contact with the grape skins (the bit usually discarded for white wine), giving deeper colour, more grip and a tannic, savoury edge. Distinctive and food-friendly.

The through-line in every style is freshness — Vermentino is rarely about power, always about lift.

Where it shines

The grape changes character with each home, which is half the fun.

• Vermentino di Gallura (Sardinia) — the only DOCG (Italy’s top tier) for the grape, from the island’s granite-rich north. Powerful, ripe and mineral, the benchmark for serious Vermentino.

• Liguria (Italy) — grown on terraces above the sea; lighter, herbal and bracingly saline, made for the local seafood and pesto.

• Tuscan coast — riper, rounder, slightly more peachy, often labelled simply Vermentino.

• Corsica & Provence/Languedoc (as Rolle) — bright, herbal and citrussy; in southern France it’s a key blending grape that adds perfume to white blends.

Best examples: Capichera, Argiolas (Gallura); Punta Crena (Liguria).

Good to know

Food is the whole point. Vermentino’s salinity and herbal cut make it almost foolproof with seafood — oysters, grilled fish, shrimp, anything with lemon. It also loves herbs and olive oil: pesto, salads, vegetable antipasti.

Drink it young and cold. With rare exceptions, this is not a grape to cellar. Buy the most recent vintage you can find and enjoy it fresh, well-chilled.

One grape, many names: Vermentino (Italy), Rolle (southern France), and it’s closely tied to Pigato and Favorita in Italy — historically treated as separate but genetically the same or near-identical. Don’t be thrown by the labels.

Value: Generally affordable, with even the top Gallura bottles staying reasonable — a smart pick for warm-weather drinking.

Food pairing. Vermentino is bright and saline, made for seafood pasta, grilled prawns, and pesto. Its citrus-and-herb lift pops with basil, lemon zest, and a whisper of sea salt.
