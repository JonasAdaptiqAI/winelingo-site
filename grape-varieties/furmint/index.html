Hungary’s great white

Furmint is the noble grape behind Tokaji — the legendary sweet wine from Hungary’s Tokaj region that kings and tsars prized for centuries. But it has a thrilling second act: today it makes some of central Europe’s most exciting bone-dry whites too. It’s a grape of real pedigree and versatility.

The common thread across everything Furmint does is electric acidity and structure. It’s high-acid, medium-bodied and capable of remarkable concentration, with a smoky, waxy, mineral signature that runs through both its dry and sweet wines.

Expect green apple, pear, lime and quince lifted by a distinctive smoky, flinty minerality and a waxy texture. In its sweet form it adds honey, apricot and marmalade — but always with that bracing acidity keeping it fresh rather than cloying.

Styles to know — dry to lusciously sweet

Dry Furmint (Száraz): The modern revelation — bone-dry, racy and mineral, often compared to fine Chablis or dry Riesling, with green apple, smoke and a waxy depth. Increasingly serious and age-worthy.

Late Harvest: Lightly sweet, a gentle introduction to Tokaj’s richer side.

Tokaji Aszú: The classic. Individually picked, nobly-rotted (botrytised) berries are blended into a dry base wine to make it sweet. You may still see a puttonyos number (3 to 6) on older or traditional labels as a sweetness scale — but since the 2013 vintage the rules tightened, the lower levels were dropped, and today’s Aszú is simply a richly sweet wine (minimum 120 g/l residual sugar, roughly the old 5–6 puttonyos). Expect lush apricot, honey and orange-marmalade flavours held taut by piercing acidity.

Eszencia: The free-run nectar of botrytised grapes — almost unimaginably sweet, syrupy and rare, with sugar so high it barely ferments. A once-in-a-lifetime curiosity.

What shapes it — noble rot explained

Botrytis (“noble rot”): Tokaj’s autumn mists and afternoon sun create perfect conditions for Botrytis cinerea, a benevolent fungus that shrivels the grapes, concentrating their sugar and acid and adding signature apricot, honey and saffron flavours. This is the magic behind sweet Tokaji — “rot” that makes one of the world’s great wines.

High acidity is the secret: Furmint’s naturally fierce acidity is exactly what stops these intensely sweet wines from tasting heavy. It’s the tension between sweetness and acidity that makes great Tokaji so balanced.

Volcanic soils: Tokaj’s volcanic and loess soils are credited with Furmint’s smoky, flinty mineral character — most noticeable in the dry wines.

Oak & lees aging: Both dry and sweet Furmint are often aged in barrel and on the lees, adding a nutty, waxy richness and helping the best wines age for decades.

Good to know

Astonishing aging: Top Tokaji Aszú is one of the longest-lived wines on earth — bottles can improve for 50 years or more. Even dry Furmint rewards a few years’ cellaring, gaining honeyed, smoky complexity.

Food: Dry Furmint is a fantastic, slightly off-beat food white — great with roast chicken, rich fish, pork and hard cheese. Sweet Tokaji is classic with blue cheese, foie gras, and fruit or nut desserts; remarkably, it can also stand up to spicy food.

A wine of history: Tokaj was delimited by royal decree in 1737, making it one of the earliest officially demarcated wine regions — though, fairly, Chianti’s boundaries were drawn a little earlier (1716). Either way it long predates Bordeaux’s famous 1855 classification, a nice hint at Tokaji’s pedigree (just note it’s a region delimitation, not a ranking of estates).

Don’t assume it’s sweet: “Furmint” or “Tokaj” on a label no longer means dessert wine — the dry style is now everywhere. Check before you pour.

Food pairing. High in acidity and minerally (think dry Tokaji), it’s superb with smoked fish, pork and rich poultry. Its green-apple and smoky-stone notes pop with citrus, dill and a hint of smoke.

Producers to seek out for Furmint

Furmint is the soul of Tokaj in north-east Hungary, made both bone-dry and as sweet Aszú, so taste both styles to really know the grape.
