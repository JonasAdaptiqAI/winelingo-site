Smell it before you sip: the perfumed rose-petal and lychee aromatics are so distinctive that Gewürztraminer is one of the easiest grapes to identify blind. Compared to a crisp Riesling, it feels rounder, oilier, and richer in the mouth with noticeably lower acidity, so even bone-dry versions can taste faintly sweet from sheer ripeness and body. Watch for a slightly bitter, spicy finish, and note that many Alsace bottlings carry some residual sugar even when not labelled as dessert wine.

The most aromatic white there is

Gewürztraminer (geh-VURTS-trah-mee-ner) is impossible to mistake for anything else. Stick your nose in the glass and it practically leaps out: roses, lychee and exotic spice, perfumed like a tropical garden. ‘Gewürz’ is German for ‘spice’, and the name fits.

It’s a full-bodied, low-acid white with an oily, almost viscous texture and golden colour. Many examples carry a few grams of sweetness, which balances all that perfume — though plenty are made fully dry.

Aromas & flavours: lychee, rose petal, Turkish delight, ginger, candied orange peel, allspice, and a waft of grapefruit or honey.

Styles to know

Because it can be dry or sweet, and the perfume reads as ‘sweet’ even when it isn’t, knowing the style before you pour is half the battle.

Dry to off-dry (Alsace): the classic — powerful, spicy and full, with just a hint of sweetness rounding off the finish. Rich enough to feel almost weighty.

Vendanges Tardives (late harvest): richer and sweeter, from extra-ripe grapes, honeyed and concentrated.

Sélection de Grains Nobles: lusciously sweet dessert wine from nobly-rotted grapes — rare and expensive.

New World dry styles: Germany, Alto Adige, New Zealand and Oregon make leaner, fresher, often bone-dry versions.

Where it shines

Alsace, France is the undisputed home. This sheltered, sunny strip on the German border, with its patchwork of granite, limestone and clay soils, produces the world’s benchmark Gewürztraminer — rich, dry-ish and intensely perfumed. The best come from the region’s named ‘Grand Cru’ vineyards.

Other regions of note:

• Alto Adige (northern Italy) and Germany — fresher, more delicate and often drier • New Zealand (Gisborne) and USA (Oregon, California, New York) — fruity, accessible styles • It’s a pink-skinned mutation of the old Traminer (Savagnin) family, often linked by name to the village of Tramin (Termeno) in Alto Adige — though that ‘named after Tramin’ story is folk etymology, and DNA studies haven’t confirmed a birthplace there.

What shapes it

Ripeness and sugar: Gewürztraminer is a pink-skinned grape that ripens fast and loses acidity quickly. Picked late for full perfume, it builds high sugar — so winemakers must decide how much to ferment to dryness and how much sweetness to leave. This is why bottles vary so much in sweetness.

Skin contact: its pink skins mean even gentle skin contact (letting juice sit with the skins) deepens the colour to gold and lifts those spicy, grippy aromatics.

Low acidity: the grape’s natural lack of acidity is its biggest challenge. Too little, and the wine tastes heavy or flabby; the best sites and careful picking preserve just enough freshness to keep it lively.

Oak: almost never used — it would bury the delicate perfume.

Good to know

The famous food match: Gewürztraminer is the classic partner for spicy and aromatic food — Thai, Indian, Moroccan, and pungent washed-rind cheeses like Munster. Its perfume and gentle sweetness tame chilli heat where a crisp dry white would clash.

Serve cooler than you think, around 8–10°C, to keep the rich texture in check. Use a generous glass to let the aromas bloom.

Love-it-or-hate-it: the overt rose-and-lychee perfume divides people. If you find most whites boring, this is the antidote; if you like subtle and steely, it may feel too much.

Aging: dry Alsace versions can age 5–10 years, turning honeyed and smoky; the sweet late-harvest wines last decades.

Food pairing. Aromatic and full-bodied, it shines with spicy Thai, Indian curries, and Moroccan tagines. Lean into its lychee, rose, and ginger notes alongside soft washed-rind cheeses.

Producers to seek out for Gewürztraminer

Alsace is the heartland for Gewürztraminer, where a run of family houses coax lychee, rose and spice into full-bodied whites; that is the place to seek it out.

1

Trimbach

Ribeauvillé house, a reference for classically dry Alsace whites.

2

Domaine Zind-Humbrecht

Biodynamic Turckheim estate, grand cru bottlings like Rangen.

3

Domaine Weinbach

Kaysersberg estate; the perfumed Cuvée Laurence Gewürztraminer.

4

Hugel

Riquewihr family, pioneers of Alsace late-harvest wines.

5

Marcel Deiss

Biodynamic Bergheim estate working grand cru sites.
