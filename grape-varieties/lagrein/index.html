An Alpine red with attitude

Lagrein (say “lah-GRINE”) is the dark, brooding native red of Italy’s far north — the German-speaking Alpine region of Alto Adige (Südtirol). It’s a grape most people have never heard of, yet it makes deeply coloured, full-bodied reds with a personality all their own.

The magic is the contrast: ripe, dark plummy fruit and firm tannin, but cooled by a high-altitude freshness and a distinctive violet-and-bitter-chocolate twist on the finish. It tastes both warm and cool at once — fruit from the sun, lift from the mountains.

Aromas & flavours: blackberry, dark plum, black cherry, violet, bitter (dark) chocolate, and a savoury, faintly smoky earthiness.

Styles to know

Lagrein comes in two main guises, and the label usually tells you which.

Lagrein Dunkel (“dark”) — the serious red: this is the one to seek out. Deep purple-black, full-bodied, with grippy tannin and dark fruit. Often given some oak ageing to round out its rustic edge. The riserva versions are built to age.

Lagrein Kretzer (“rosé”): the same grape made as a deeply-coloured, dry, characterful rosato — fuller and more savoury than your average pink, great with food.

As a family note, DNA studies show Lagrein is an offspring of Teroldego, the dark native red of nearby Trentino. Through that lineage it’s part of the broader north-eastern Alpine family connected to Pinot (a forebear of Teroldego) and distantly related to Syrah — which helps explain its mix of dark fruit, perfume and freshness.

Where it shines

Lagrein is almost entirely an Alto Adige speciality, and the best sites are very specific.

• Gries & Bolzano (Alto Adige, Italy): the warm, gravelly valley floor around the city of Bolzano is the grape’s spiritual home, giving the ripest, most complete examples. Look for “Gries” on the label. • Alto Adige / Südtirol broadly: excellent cooperatives and growers across the region make reliable, well-priced Lagrein. • Trentino (Italy): a little is grown just to the south, in the same Alpine orbit.

Tiny plantings exist in Australia and the USA, but for now this is one to enjoy as a genuine Italian regional gem.

What shapes it

Altitude & the valley floor: Lagrein needs warmth to ripen its tannins, so it’s planted on the warmer, sandy-gravelly valley floors rather than the cool slopes. The high-altitude day-night temperature swing then locks in bright acidity and that perfumed lift — the source of its fresh-yet-rich balance.

Taming the tannin: young Lagrein can be firm and a touch rustic. Winemakers soften it with careful, gentle extraction and a period in oak.

Oak ageing: time in barrel (often large casks, sometimes smaller barriques for riserva) rounds the tannin and adds a layer of spice and cocoa, polishing the wild edges without burying the fruit.

Malolactic fermentation: a natural second, softening conversion that turns sharp malic acid (think green apple) into softer lactic acid (think cream) — standard for reds, and it gives Lagrein a rounder, smoother mouthfeel.

Good to know

At the table: its dark fruit, fresh acidity and savoury edge make it a natural with hearty Alpine food — speck and cured meats, braised beef, venison, mushroom dishes and aged mountain cheese.

Serving: 16–18°C in a standard red glass; a short decant helps the bigger, younger bottlings show their fruit.

Ageing: everyday Lagrein is delicious within a few years, while well-made riserva can develop happily for a decade, gaining leather and earth.

Why it’s worth a punt: if you like the body and dark fruit of Syrah or Malbec but want something with mountain freshness and a story, Lagrein delivers — and because it’s little-known, it’s often a bargain for the quality.

Food pairing. Dark and savory from northern Italy, it suits braised beef, venison, and aged cheeses. Its blackberry, plum, and cocoa depth loves grilled meats and a hint of smoke.
