Why Verdicchio matters

Verdicchio is Italy’s most quietly serious white grape, grown almost entirely in the Marche, a green coastal region on the Adriatic side of central Italy. The name comes from verde (green), a nod to the faint green tint in the wine and the unripe-grape edge in young examples.

What sets it apart is a two-part personality. There’s a tangy, citrus-and-almond freshness up front, then a low, slightly bitter note on the finish — like the skin of an almond. That gentle bitterness is a feature, not a flaw, and it’s what makes Verdicchio so good with food.

Aromas & flavours: lemon, green apple, fennel, white peach, almond, a saline mineral lift.

For decades it was dismissed as cheap fish-and-chips wine sold in a fish-shaped bottle. The grape has been thoroughly re-evaluated: top bottles age beautifully and rival far pricier whites, which is exactly why it’s worth knowing.

Styles to know

Verdicchio comes in a wider range of styles than most people expect.

Fresh & unoaked (the classic): Light-to-medium body, crisp acidity, citrus and almond. Made in steel tanks to keep it bright. This is the everyday style and superb value.

Riserva & lees-aged: Held longer before release and often rested on its lees (the spent yeast left after fermentation, which adds body and a creamy, bready depth). These are fuller, rounder and built to age — fine examples gain honey, hazelnut and dried-herb notes over 5–10 years.

Sparkling (spumante): A small amount is made fizzy, dry and brisk — a regional aperitif you’ll rarely see exported.

Passito (sweet): Rare dessert version from dried grapes, golden and honeyed.

If you’ve only had the cheap fish-bottle version, seek out a Classico or Riserva — same grape, completely different ambition.

Where it shines

Two appellations in the Marche carry the flag, and the difference between them is worth knowing.

• Verdicchio dei Castelli di Jesi — the larger, better-known zone inland from the port of Ancona. Generally lighter, zestier and more immediately refreshing. Look for Classico on the label for the historic heartland.

• Verdicchio di Matelica — a small, higher, landlocked valley. With no sea breeze and bigger day-to-night temperature swings, these are firmer, more structured and more mineral, often the more age-worthy of the two.

Best examples: Bucci, Garofoli, Umani Ronchi (Castelli di Jesi); Bisci, La Monacesca (Matelica).

A reliable rule: Jesi for bright and friendly, Matelica for tighter and serious.

Good to know

It ages — really. Verdicchio is one of the few Italian whites that genuinely rewards a few years in the bottle, developing nutty, honeyed, almost saline complexity. Don’t be afraid of a Riserva with 5+ years on it.

Food: Its built-in saline-almond bite makes it a natural with seafood — brodetto (Adriatic fish stew), grilled fish, fritto misto, shellfish. The lees-aged styles also handle roast chicken and creamy pasta.

Value: Pound for pound, one of the best-value quality whites in Italy. A serious bottle still costs a fraction of a comparable Burgundy.

Common mix-up: Verdicchio is unrelated to Verdejo (Spain) or Verdelho (Portugal/Australia) despite the similar names — easy to confuse, entirely different grapes.

Food pairing. Verdicchio is a coastal classic with shellfish, brodetto, and linguine alle vongole. Its almond-tinged finish shines with lemon, dill, and briny olives.
