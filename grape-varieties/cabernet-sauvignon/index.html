Cabernet Sauvignon is the world’s most famous red wine grape, and for good reason. It makes deep, full-bodied wines with firm structure and classic flavours of blackcurrant, plum and cedar. If you like a red with real backbone, this is your grape.

Here’s the friendly guide to what Cabernet Sauvignon wine tastes like, where it grows, the styles it makes and why, plus how to serve it and the food pairings that make it sing. No wrong answers — just the good stuff, explained plainly.

What Cabernet Sauvignon wine tastes like

Cabernet Sauvignon is a bold, full-bodied red. The first thing most people notice is the tannin — that grippy, mouth-drying feeling, a bit like strong stewed tea. It also has fresh acidity that keeps the wine lively, so despite its size it rarely feels heavy or flat.

On flavour, think dark fruit: blackcurrant (its signature note), black cherry and plum. Around that fruit you’ll often find savoury, aromatic touches — cedar, tobacco, pencil shavings and a hint of green bell pepper. When the wine is aged in oak barrels, you’ll pick up vanilla, baking spice, cocoa and sometimes a whiff of smoke or cigar box.

A quick jargon check: ‘tannin’ comes from grape skins, seeds and oak, and gives red wine its structure and ageing power. ‘Body’ is how full and weighty the wine feels in your mouth. Cabernet is high on both.

  • Body: full
  • Tannin: high and firm
  • Acidity: medium to high, keeps it fresh
  • Core flavours: blackcurrant, black cherry, plum
  • Savoury notes: cedar, tobacco, green pepper, mint
  • From oak: vanilla, spice, cocoa

Why Cabernet Sauvignon tastes this way

A lot of Cabernet’s character comes down to its grapes. The berries are small and thick-skinned, and most of a red wine’s colour, tannin and dark-fruit flavour lives in the skin. More skin relative to juice means a darker, more tannic, more concentrated wine — which is exactly why Cabernet is so bold.

That thick skin also ripens slowly, so Cabernet needs warmth and a long growing season to fully ripen. In cooler spots or cooler years it can show more of those green, herbal, bell-pepper notes; in warmer places it leans riper and juicier, with softer, plusher fruit.

Cabernet is also firmly linked with oak ageing. Time in barrel softens the tannins, adds vanilla and spice, and lets the wine develop the cedar and tobacco complexity that fans love. Its structure means good examples can age gracefully for many years, gradually turning smoother and more savoury.

Where Cabernet Sauvignon grows: classic regions

Cabernet is grown almost everywhere wine is made, but a handful of regions are its true heartlands. In the Old World (Europe and its long traditions), Bordeaux in France is the spiritual home. In the New World (everywhere else — the Americas, Australia, and beyond), it has found some spectacular second homes.

Old World: Bordeaux, specifically the ‘Left Bank’ areas like the Médoc and Pessac-Léognan, is where Cabernet built its reputation. Here it’s usually blended (more on that below) and tends to be more restrained, structured and savoury — often described as elegant and ‘classic’. It also plays a starring role in Italy’s Tuscan coast, in the wines known as Super Tuscans.

New World: Napa Valley in California is Cabernet’s most famous New World address, making rich, ripe, generously fruity styles. Australia shines with it too — Coonawarra (known for minty, structured Cabernet) and Margaret River. Chile offers superb value and reliably juicy, approachable examples, while South Africa and Washington State are also well worth exploring.

  • France — Bordeaux (Médoc, Pessac-Léognan): structured, savoury, classic
  • USA — Napa Valley: rich, ripe, full-bodied
  • Australia — Coonawarra & Margaret River: minty, firm, age-worthy
  • Chile — great value, juicy and approachable
  • Italy — Tuscany (Super Tuscans): polished blends
  • Also great: South Africa, Washington State, Spain

Styles Cabernet Sauvignon makes

Cabernet’s biggest split is blended versus single-variety (called ‘varietal’). In Bordeaux and many classic regions it’s blended, most often with Merlot and Cabernet Franc. Merlot adds softness and plummy roundness, filling in Cabernet’s firm frame — the two are a famous partnership. This is the classic ‘Bordeaux blend’.

On its own, Cabernet shows off its pure blackcurrant-and-cedar character with full tannic structure. New World regions in particular love this varietal style, often labelling the wine simply as ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’.

Beyond that, styles range from everyday, fruit-forward and easy-drinking (great value from Chile and other warm regions) to serious, oak-aged, age-worthy wines built to improve for a decade or more. There’s also a small amount of rosé made from Cabernet, though red is overwhelmingly its calling.

  • Bordeaux-style blends: Cabernet with Merlot and Cabernet Franc
  • Varietal Cabernet: pure, structured, blackcurrant-led
  • Everyday styles: juicy, fruit-forward, approachable
  • Premium styles: oak-aged, complex, built to age

How to serve Cabernet Sauvignon

A few simple moves make a big difference with a wine this bold. Serve it slightly below room temperature — around 16 to 18°C. Too warm and the alcohol dominates; a short spell in the fridge (15–20 minutes) before pouring gets it into the sweet spot.

Because Cabernet is tannic, giving it some air really helps. Decanting (pouring it into a jug or carafe) or simply opening the bottle ahead of time softens the tannins and opens up the aromas — especially with younger or more premium bottles. If you don’t have a decanter, a large glass and a bit of patience do much the same job.

Use a big, round-bowled glass to let those cedar and blackcurrant aromas gather. And a gentle, non-preachy note: this is a wine to savour slowly with food and good company — a little goes a long way.

  • Serve at 16–18°C (chill briefly if too warm)
  • Decant or open early to soften tannins
  • Use a large, round-bowled glass
  • Best enjoyed with food

Classic Cabernet Sauvignon food pairings

Cabernet’s firm tannins love protein and fat, which soften those tannins and make the wine taste smoother and fruitier. That’s why it’s the go-to red for a good steak — the classic match for a reason.

Think hearty, savoury dishes: roast lamb with rosemary, beef burgers, grilled and barbecued meats, and rich beef stews or braises. Hard aged cheeses like mature Cheddar are a lovely, simple pairing too. For plant-based plates, meaty mushrooms (like portobello) and roasted or grilled vegetables stand up well to its structure.

One tip: Cabernet’s tannins can clash with very spicy food or delicate fish, so it’s happiest alongside robust, savoury cooking. With riper, fruitier New World styles, even a square of dark chocolate can work a treat.

  • Steak, especially ribeye or sirloin
  • Roast lamb with herbs
  • Burgers, grilled and barbecued meats
  • Rich beef stews and braises
  • Mature hard cheeses like Cheddar
  • Portobello mushrooms and roasted veg

FAQ

Questions & answers

What does Cabernet Sauvignon taste like?

It’s a full-bodied red with high, grippy tannins and fresh acidity. Expect blackcurrant, black cherry and plum, with savoury notes of cedar, tobacco and sometimes green pepper, plus vanilla and spice when it’s aged in oak.

Is Cabernet Sauvignon sweet or dry?

It’s a dry red wine, meaning it isn’t sweet. Riper, warm-climate styles can taste rich and fruity, which some people read as ‘sweeter’, but there’s very little actual sugar.

What food goes best with Cabernet Sauvignon?

Rich, savoury dishes. Steak is the classic match, along with roast lamb, burgers, grilled meats, beef stews and mature hard cheeses. Its tannins soften against protein and fat.

Where does the best Cabernet Sauvignon come from?

Its classic homes are Bordeaux in France and Napa Valley in the USA. Coonawarra and Margaret River in Australia, plus Chile, Tuscany, South Africa and Washington State all make excellent examples.

What’s the difference between Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot?

Cabernet is bolder, with firmer tannins and more structure; Merlot is softer, rounder and plummier. They’re often blended together, as in classic Bordeaux, because they balance each other beautifully.
