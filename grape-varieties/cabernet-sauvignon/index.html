The world’s red benchmark

If red wine had a flagship grape, this would be it. Cabernet Sauvignon is the most planted red wine grape on earth and the backbone of many of the world’s most famous (and expensive) bottles.

It’s prized for a powerful combination: deep colour, firm tannin, blackcurrant fruit and an almost architectural structure that lets the best examples age for decades.

For beginners, Cabernet is a great reference point for “big red.” Once you know its blackcurrant-and-grippy-tannin profile, you have a yardstick to compare every other red against.

Understanding its structure

Cabernet’s calling card is tannin — those compounds (from grape skins and oak) that make your mouth feel dry and grippy, like over-steeped tea. Young Cabernet can feel firm, even austere; with age, tannins soften and the wine turns silky.

It’s also high in acidity and usually full-bodied, which is exactly why it ages so well — structure is a wine’s skeleton.

• Aromas & flavours: blackcurrant (cassis), black cherry, cedar, graphite/pencil lead, mint or eucalyptus, tobacco, and — with age — leather and forest floor • A classic marker: many people detect a leafy or minty note, the savoury edge that keeps all that fruit from feeling sweet

Styles to know

Classic blend (Bordeaux-style): Cabernet is often blended with Merlot and Cabernet Franc. Merlot fleshes out Cabernet’s firm middle, giving a more complete, balanced wine. This “Bordeaux blend” template is copied worldwide.

Bold single-varietal (New World-style): Warmer regions make riper, fuller, fruit-forward Cabernet with softer tannins, generous oak and higher alcohol — plush and crowd-pleasing.

Restrained & structured (Old World-style): Cooler-climate Cabernet is leaner, more savoury, with firmer tannins, fresh acidity and earthy, herbal notes built for the cellar.

• Cooler climate cues: more herbal, leafy, fresh, firmer • Warmer climate cues: riper, jammier, rounder, higher alcohol

Where it shines

• Bordeaux (Left Bank), France — the spiritual home. Médoc and Pauillac make structured, cedary, age-worthy reds (think Château Latour, Mouton Rothschild). • Napa Valley, USA — rich, ripe, opulent Cabernet with plush tannins and bold oak; a global benchmark for the powerful style. • Coonawarra, Australia — distinctive minty, eucalyptus-tinged Cabernet on famous red “terra rossa” soil. • Maipo Valley, Chile — reliably excellent, often with a signature minty-herbal lift and great value. • Also superb: Tuscany (“Super Tuscans”), Margaret River (Australia), Stellenbosch (South Africa), Washington State.

What shapes it

Oak ageing: Cabernet loves oak. New oak barrels add vanilla, cedar, baking spice and toast, and help soften tannins over time. Heavy oak = those sweet, spicy, smoky notes you taste in many premium bottles.

Ageing potential: Top Cabernet is one of wine’s great cellar candidates — firm tannin and acidity preserve it, and over 10-20+ years fruit gives way to leather, cigar box and earth.

Climate: Warmth dictates the style. Cooler sites keep it leaner, fresher and more herbal; warmer sites push it riper, rounder and higher in alcohol.

Good to know

Food: Made for protein and fat. Steak is the cliché for good reason — fat and tannin cancel each other out, leaving the wine smoother and the meat richer. Also great with lamb and hard aged cheese.

Serving: Serve at 16-18°C. Young, tannic examples benefit from decanting (pouring into a carafe to let air soften them).

A quick tip: Avoid pairing with very spicy food, which can make tannins taste harsh and accentuate the alcohol.

Common confusion: A “Cabernet” label almost always means Cabernet Sauvignon — the lighter, more aromatic Cabernet Franc nearly always spells out “Franc” in full.

Food pairing. Cabernet Sauvignon is full and structured, built for steak, lamb, and aged hard cheeses. Lean into blackcurrant, cedar, and a hint of dark chocolate.

Cabernet Sauvignon is full and structured, built for steak, lamb, and aged hard cheeses. Lean into blackcurrant, cedar, and a hint of dark chocolate.
