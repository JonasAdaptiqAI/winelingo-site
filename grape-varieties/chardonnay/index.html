The world’s most versatile white

Chardonnay is the chameleon of white wine. It’s the most famous white grape on the planet, yet it has no single “taste” — because more than almost any other variety, what you get in the glass is shaped by where it’s grown and how it’s made.

The grape itself is fairly neutral, like a blank canvas. That’s its superpower: it faithfully expresses climate, soil and winemaking, so Chardonnay ranges from steely and lemony to rich, buttery and tropical.

It’s also the great white grape of Burgundy — the source of the world’s most coveted dry whites — and a pillar of Champagne. If a wine snob has one white grape they revere, it’s usually this one.

Styles to know — unoaked vs oaked

The single most useful thing to understand about Chardonnay is the oak divide.

Unoaked / cool-climate: Crisp, fresh and lean, with citrus, green apple and pear, sometimes a flinty, mineral edge. Think Chablis. No creamy or vanilla notes — just bright fruit and acidity.

Oaked / warm-climate: Round, rich and fuller-bodied, with vanilla, toast, baking spice and often a buttery texture, plus riper fruit like peach and tropical pineapple. Think classic Napa or oaked Burgundy.

Sparkling: As a key Champagne grape it brings finesse, citrus and the backbone for long ageing. “Blanc de Blancs” Champagne is 100% Chardonnay.

• Unoaked cues: lemon, green apple, mineral, crisp • Oaked cues: vanilla, butter, toast, peach, creamy

What shapes it — the winemaker’s toolkit

No grape responds to winemaking like Chardonnay. A few terms worth knowing, in plain words:

Oak ageing: Fermenting or ageing the wine in oak barrels adds vanilla, toast and spice and a fuller texture. New oak gives more; older or no oak keeps it fresh.

Malolactic fermentation (“malo”): A natural process that converts sharp malic acid (think green apple) into softer lactic acid (think milk). This is what creates that buttery, creamy character and a rounder feel.

Lees ageing & bâtonnage: “Lees” are the spent yeast cells left after fermentation. Leaving the wine on its lees — and stirring them, called bâtonnage — adds a richer, creamier, breadier (brioche-like) texture and more depth.

Mix and match these and you can steer Chardonnay from laser-sharp to lush and decadent.

Where it shines

• Burgundy, France — the benchmark. Chablis is steely and unoaked; Meursault and Puligny-Montrachet are richer, oak-aged and profound. The reference points for great Chardonnay. • Champagne, France — a foundation grape of the world’s most famous sparkling wine. • California, USA — from rich, oaky, tropical Napa styles to fresher, cool-coast versions. • Australia — Margaret River for elegance; cooler regions now make restrained, balanced styles after moving on from 1990s super-buttery wines. • Also excellent: New Zealand, Chile, South Africa, and increasingly cool-climate sites worldwide.

Best examples: Chablis, Meursault, Puligny-Montrachet, blanc de blancs Champagne.

Good to know

Food: A food superstar because of its range. Crisp unoaked styles love oysters, shellfish and goat cheese; richer oaked styles shine with roast chicken, creamy pasta, lobster with butter, and pork.

Serving: Don’t over-chill. Lighter unoaked styles at 8-10°C; richer oaked styles at 10-13°C, where the aromas open up. Too cold mutes all that complexity.

The “ABC” backlash: In the 2000s, heavily oaked, buttery Chardonnay became so common that some drinkers declared “Anything But Chardonnay.” The grape never deserved the blame — the style did. Today’s Chardonnay is far more balanced, so it’s well worth a fresh look.

Value tip: If you tried Chardonnay once and found it too “buttery,” try an unoaked one like Chablis — it may taste like a completely different grape.

Food pairing. Chardonnay is versatile and full, suiting roast chicken, creamy pasta, and buttered seafood. Lean into apple, lemon, and toasty vanilla when oaked.
