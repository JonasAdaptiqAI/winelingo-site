The overview

Palomino is one of wine’s most fascinating chameleons. On its own, as a still white wine, it is famously neutral — pale, soft, low in acidity and short on obvious aroma. Yet it is the grape behind Sherry, one of the most complex and underrated wines on the planet. The magic isn’t in the grape; it’s in what the cellar does to it.

Its home is Jerez, in the sun-baked, chalk-soiled southwest of Spain (Andalucía). There, Palomino’s very neutrality becomes a virtue: it is a blank canvas onto which fermentation, fortification, a living veil of yeast, and years in barrel paint extraordinary flavours.

Why it matters: Without Palomino there is no fino, no manzanilla, no amontillado, no oloroso — no Sherry as we know it. To understand this grape you have to understand Sherry, because that transformation is the whole point.

Think of Palomino not as a flavour but as a process. The grape is humble; the wine is anything but.

The Sherry styles to know

Fino & Manzanilla (the pale, dry, delicate ones): Aged under “flor” — a living veil of yeast (see below) — that keeps the wine pale and gives saline, almondy, bread-dough, green-apple notes. Bone dry, light, brilliant chilled with food. Manzanilla is essentially fino from the seaside town of Sanlúcar de Barrameda, often even fresher and saltier.

Amontillado: Starts life under flor like a fino, then the flor dies and the wine ages in contact with air — gaining amber colour, hazelnut, dried herbs and depth while staying dry.

Oloroso: Fortified higher so flor never forms; aged fully oxidatively for a rich, dark, walnutty, intense (but still bone-dry) wine.

Palo Cortado: A rare, prized in-between — fino-like finesse with oloroso-like richness.

Note: Sweet Sherries (cream, PX) get their sugar from other grapes; the dry backbone is Palomino. The grape itself makes dry wine.

What shapes it

Flor — the living veil: This is the heart of Sherry’s magic. After fermentation, a film of special yeast can grow on the wine’s surface inside the barrel. This “flor” protects the wine from air (keeping it pale) and feeds on it, creating the distinctive tangy, saline, yeasty, almond flavours of fino and manzanilla. Few wines on earth are made this way.

Biological vs oxidative ageing: “Biological” = aged under flor (pale, fresh finos). “Oxidative” = aged with air contact, no flor (dark, nutty olorosos). Amontillado does both in sequence. This single choice explains the whole Sherry spectrum.

Fortification: Neutral grape spirit is added to raise the alcohol. The exact level decides whether flor can survive — fino and manzanilla are fortified to around 15%, where flor thrives, while oloroso is taken to roughly 17% or higher, where flor cannot form. That one decision sets the style.

The solera system: Sherry is blended through a tiered stack of barrels, fractionally drawing off older wine and topping up with younger. Each bottle is a blend of many years, giving consistency and immense complexity. Sherry is rarely a single vintage.

Albariza soil: Jerez’s dazzling white, chalky soil — soft and rich in calcium carbonate — retains scarce moisture and reflects sunlight, prized for growing healthy Palomino in a hot, dry climate.

Good to know

Sherry is dry (mostly) — and seriously underrated. The biggest myth is that Sherry means sweet. Fino, manzanilla, amontillado and oloroso are all bone dry. They are among the most food-friendly, complex and best-value wines in the world.

Food is where it sings. Fino and manzanilla with olives, almonds, jamón, fried fish and sushi is a revelation — the salty, savoury wine and salty, savoury food lift each other. Amontillado and oloroso love nuts, hard cheeses, mushrooms and rich broths.

Treat fino like white wine. Serve it cold and, once opened, drink it within a few days — the delicate flor character fades fast. Older oxidative styles (oloroso, amontillado) are far more robust once open.

Beyond Sherry: Small amounts of Palomino make light, neutral still whites in places like the Canary Islands, South Africa and Galicia — but its true genius is, and always will be, in the bodegas of Jerez.

Food pairing. As bone-dry Fino or Manzanilla Sherry, Palomino is a classic apéritif and loves salty, savoury bites: olives, almonds, jamón, and fried fish. Its bracing, briny edge sings with anchovies, lemon, and sea salt.
