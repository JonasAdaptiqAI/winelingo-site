Spain’s quiet workhorse white

Macabeo is one of Spain’s most important white grapes that almost nobody asks for by name — partly because in Rioja it goes by Viura instead. It’s a mild, fresh, approachable white: gentle apple-and-citrus fruit, soft acidity and a delicate floral note. Not flashy, but genuinely useful and very food-friendly.

Its real claim to fame is double: it’s the backbone of Spain’s traditional-method sparkling Cava, and the principal white of Rioja. Two of Spain’s most famous wines lean on this unassuming grape.

Aromas & flavours: green apple, lemon and citrus pith, white flower, fennel, a touch of fresh almond — and, when barrel-aged, honey and toasted nuts.

Styles to know

Macabeo’s mildness is its superpower — it takes on whatever style the winemaker chooses.

Fresh, unoaked still white: the everyday face. Light-bodied, crisp, apple-and-citrus, made for drinking young and cold. Easy and refreshing.

Barrel-aged Rioja Blanco (as Viura): the classic, ambitious style. Time in oak and on lees turns it rich, nutty and honeyed, with a creamy texture — these can age beautifully for years and are one of Spain’s great hidden secrets.

Cava (sparkling): the steady, appley base of most Cava blends, bringing freshness and a gentle apple-and-bread character, usually alongside Xarel·lo and Parellada.

Macabeo = Viura — same grape, the name just depends on the region.

Where it shines

Macabeo is spread across northern Spain and into southern France.

• Rioja (Spain): as Viura, the dominant white grape — both crisp modern styles and the famous oak-aged, age-worthy whites. • Penedès & Catalonia (Spain): the heart of Cava country, where Macabeo is a core sparkling grape. • Rueda, Aragón & broadly across the north: widely planted for fresh everyday whites. • Roussillon & Languedoc (southern France): known here as Maccabeu, used in southern French whites and traditional fortified wines.

It’s a grape of the western Mediterranean, equally at home still or sparkling.

What shapes it

Oak & lees — the big lever: left in steel, Macabeo is light and zesty. Given barrel fermentation, oak aging and extended time on its lees (spent yeast, which adds creaminess and savoury depth), it transforms into the rich, golden, nutty Rioja Blanco style. Same grape, two completely different wines.

The traditional (Champagne) method for Cava: Cava gets its bubbles from a second fermentation inside the bottle, then ages on its lees — that’s what gives the gentle bready, biscuity note alongside Macabeo’s apple fruit. The longer the lees aging, the more complex.

Blending partners in Cava: Macabeo brings freshness and fruit; Xarel·lo adds structure and an earthy grip; Parellada lends delicacy and florals. Together they balance.

Picking time: because its acidity is only moderate, growers often pick a touch early to keep the wine fresh and lively.

Good to know

At the table: fresh Macabeo is a natural aperitif and shines with seafood, tapas, salads and lighter chicken or veg dishes. The richer barrel-aged style steps up to roast poultry, creamy sauces and hard cheeses.

Serving: chill the fresh and sparkling versions well (6–10°C); the oaked Rioja whites show more at a touch warmer, around 10–12°C.

Aging: drink the crisp versions young. But top oak-aged Rioja Blanco is a classic ager — bottles can gain honeyed, complex depth over a decade or more.

Worth knowing: next time you enjoy a Cava or a white Rioja, you’re likely drinking Macabeo without realising it. Learning the name unlocks a whole category of excellent, well-priced Spanish whites.

Food pairing. Light and easygoing, this Spanish white (and Cava grape) loves tapas, grilled prawns, and almond-laced dishes. Lean into its green apple, citrus, and floral freshness with salty bites.
