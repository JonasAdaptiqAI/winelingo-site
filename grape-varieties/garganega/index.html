The grape behind Soave

You may never have seen ‘Garganega’ on a label, but you’ve almost certainly drunk it: it’s the grape behind Soave, the gentle Italian white from the hills near Verona in the Veneto.

Soave spent decades as a byword for bland, mass-produced white, which sells the grape short. Good Garganega is quietly lovely — light-bodied, fresh and subtly floral, with a signature note of green almond and a delicate, lingering bitterness on the finish that’s part of its charm.

Aromas & flavours: lemon, ripe pear, green almond, white blossom, marzipan, and a chamomile or honeyed note in riper examples.

An understated style

Garganega isn’t a grape that shouts. It’s about texture and subtlety rather than punchy aromatics — closer in spirit to a quiet white Burgundy than to zesty Sauvignon Blanc.

Everyday Soave: light, crisp, citrusy and clean — an easy aperitif and seafood wine.

Soave Classico: from the original hillside heartland, with more depth, a richer almond-and-pear character, and genuine mineral cut.

Recioto di Soave: a rare sweet version made from dried grapes — golden, honeyed and luscious, the white answer to the region’s famous Amarone.

Where it shines

Garganega is tied to the Veneto in northeast Italy, and the difference between flat and volcanic soils is everything.

• Soave Classico — the volcanic hillside zone east of Verona, the source of the best, most mineral wines • Soave (plain) — the larger, flatter plains area; reliable but usually simpler • Gambellara — a small neighbouring zone making a similar, often excellent style

The finest single vineyards, like those on the volcanic slopes of Monte Foscarino or Calvarino, produce age-worthy whites that surprise people who think Soave is just a cheap quaffer.

What shapes it

Yields: Garganega is naturally vigorous and crops heavily. Pushed for volume, it makes thin, watery wine — which is exactly how Soave got its dull reputation. The best growers cut yields hard to concentrate flavour.

Soil: wines from volcanic basalt and limestone hillsides have far more minerality and tension than those from the fertile plain.

Lees ageing: many quality producers rest the wine on its lees — the harmless dead yeast cells left after fermentation — which adds a creamy, slightly nutty richness and roundness without needing oak.

Oak: usually avoided or used lightly. Garganega’s delicate character is easily smothered, so most is made in steel tanks to keep it fresh.

Good to know

Read the label. Look for ‘Soave Classico’ for a meaningful step up in quality, and seek out single-producer estate bottles over supermarket own-label.

Food: a brilliant table wine — risotto, fresh-water fish, lighter pasta, fried vegetables, soft cheeses. Its gentle bitterness and saline edge make it more flexible than it first appears.

Aging: most is for drinking young and fresh, within two or three years. But top Classico wines can age 5–10 years, developing honey, toasted almond and a richer, waxier texture.

A common mix-up: Garganega is occasionally confused with Greco (it once carried the synonym ‘Grecanico’), but they’re unrelated grapes from different parts of Italy.

Food pairing. Light, fresh and the soul of Soave, it’s a natural with antipasti, white fish and risotto. Its pear, almond and white-blossom notes brighten with lemon, basil and toasted nuts.

Producers to seek out for Garganega

For Garganega at its best, look to Soave Classico in the volcanic hills east of Verona, where the top estates make lemony, almond-scented whites that can age.

Pieropan

The benchmark Soave Classico estate (Calvarino, La Rocca).
