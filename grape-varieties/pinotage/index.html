South Africa’s homegrown red

Pinotage is the grape South Africa invented. In 1925, a Stellenbosch University professor named Abraham Perold crossed elegant, fussy Pinot Noir with hardy, sun-loving Cinsault (then called “Hermitage” at the Cape). The name fuses the two: Pino(t) + (Hermi)tage = Pinotage. The goal was Pinot Noir’s charm on a vine tough enough for African heat.

The result is a deeply coloured, full-bodied red that tastes like neither parent. At its best it offers dark bramble fruit, a smoky-savoury edge and real richness for the money.

It’s also wine’s great divider. A generation of badly made Pinotage earned it a reputation for harsh, rubbery, paint-stripper notes — and that reputation still lingers. Modern winemaking has largely fixed the problem, so the grape deserves a fresh, open-minded taste.

Styles to know

Classic dry red: the main event — medium-to-full, with blackberry, plum and bramble fruit over a smoky, earthy core. Good ones are plush and generous.

”Coffee” Pinotage: a hugely popular modern style where heavily toasted oak chips or staves are used to layer on mocha, chocolate and espresso flavours. Polarising — fun and crowd-pleasing to some, an artificial sugar-rush to others. If a label shouts “coffee” or “chocolate,” this is what you’re getting.

Cape Blends: South Africa’s signature red blends, where Pinotage is by convention a meaningful component (usually 30–70%) alongside Bordeaux grapes like Cabernet. The Pinotage adds colour and a savoury Cape twist.

Rosé and lighter reds: its deep colour and bright fruit also make juicy, well-priced rosés and easy-drinking light reds.

What shapes it — and the famous off-notes

Pinotage’s reputation problems came from chemistry, not the grape itself. Understanding them explains why older or cheaper bottles can taste odd while modern ones are clean.

Isoamyl acetate is a compound that smells of banana, nail-polish or acetone. Fast, hot fermentations used to push Pinotage in this direction; cooler, gentler fermentation now keeps it in check.

”Burnt rubber”: an off-putting note once common in cheap Pinotage. Its exact cause is still debated — vine stress, oxidation and rushed winemaking are all implicated, and some research points to other sources too. Whatever the trigger, careful canopy management and better cellar hygiene have largely engineered it out.

Oak: because the grape is naturally rich and a bit rustic, oak is used heavily — French barrels for finesse, or toasted staves for that mocha “coffee” style. Oak is doing a lot of the talking in many bottles.

The takeaway: buy from a reputable producer (look to Stellenbosch and Swartland), and you’ll meet a clean, modern grape, not the bogeyman of wine lore.

Good to know

At the table: its smoky-savoury richness loves the braai (South African barbecue). Think grilled and charred meats, boerewors sausage, sticky barbecue, even a burger.

Value: Pinotage remains one of the best-value full-bodied reds out there — partly a legacy of its underdog reputation.

Aging: most is built to drink young, within 3–5 years, while the fruit is generous. Serious single-vineyard and Cape Blend bottlings can age a decade or more.

A myth to retire: “all Pinotage tastes of rubber” is decades out of date. The grape that won the world over only recently — South Africa’s wines were locked out of many markets until the 1990s — is still catching up on first impressions.

Food pairing. Bold and smoky, South Africa’s Pinotage was born for the braai: grilled and barbecued meats, boerewors, and burgers. Its dark berry and coffee notes pop with charred, smoky, and peppery flavours.
