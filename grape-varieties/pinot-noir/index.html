The thinking drinker’s red

Pinot Noir is the great seducer of the wine world: pale and translucent in the glass, light-to-medium bodied, with silky, low tannin and a haunting mix of red fruit, flowers and earth. It charms rather than overpowers.

It’s also famously difficult — a thin-skinned grape that bruises easily, ripens fussily and reflects its vineyard with almost unnerving sensitivity. Two Pinots from neighbouring plots can taste meaningfully different, which is exactly why obsessives adore it.

It matters because it’s the red half of Burgundy, the spiritual benchmark for elegance over power, and the grape that most rewards paying attention. If big tannic reds feel like a wall, Pinot Noir is the door.

Styles to know

Cool-climate (classic Burgundy): Light, pale and high-acid, with red cherry, cranberry, rose and a savoury, earthy, sometimes mushroomy undertone. Delicate and detailed.

Warm-climate (much of the New World): Riper and rounder, with darker fruit — black cherry, plum, baking spice — and a fuller, more upfront feel. Easier to love on first sip.

Oaked vs lighter-handed: Time in oak barrels adds vanilla, clove and a silkier texture; less-oaked styles stay brighter and more transparent. “Whole-bunch” winemaking (fermenting with the stems) can add a peppery, floral, structural lift.

Beyond still red: It’s also a key grape in Champagne (pressed pale for sparkling) and makes some of the finest rosé and sparkling wines worldwide.

Aromas & flavours: red cherry, raspberry, cranberry, rose, forest floor, mushroom.

Where it shines

Pinot is a cool-climate specialist — too much heat bakes away its delicacy.

• Burgundy, France — the undisputed benchmark; villages like Gevrey-Chambertin, Vosne-Romanée and Volnay define the grape, with prices to match. • Oregon (Willamette Valley), USA — cool, marginal and Burgundy-like in spirit; among the New World’s finest. • Central Otago & Martinborough, New Zealand — vivid, pure-fruited and structured. • Sonoma Coast & Sta. Rita Hills, California — riper, plusher styles from fog-cooled sites. • Mornington Peninsula & Yarra Valley, Australia; Germany (as Spätburgunder) — elegant, increasingly serious.

The common thread is coolness: morning fog, high latitude or altitude. Pinot needs enough warmth to ripen but not so much that it loses its perfume and freshness.

What shapes it

Climate above all: Cool sites preserve the high acidity, pale colour and red-fruit-and-earth character; warm sites push it darker, riper and fuller. This single factor explains most of the differences between bottles.

Oak ageing: Pinot is usually aged in oak barrels, which soften it and add subtle vanilla and spice. Too much new oak can bury its delicacy, so the best producers use it with restraint.

Whole-bunch / stem inclusion: Fermenting with some of the grape stems (rather than just the de-stemmed berries) can add savoury spice, floral lift and extra texture — a stylistic choice you’ll notice in many top wines.

Site sensitivity (terroir): More than almost any grape, Pinot telegraphs where it grew — the soil, slope and microclimate. This is why Burgundy is mapped into hundreds of named vineyards: small differences genuinely show up in the glass.

Good to know

Serving: Don’t over-chill it, but do give it a light chill — around 13–15°C, noticeably cooler than a warm room (~20°C) yet not fridge-cold — to let its aromatics sing. A wide bowl glass helps.

Food: Wonderfully versatile thanks to low tannin and high acidity. It’s a classic with duck, salmon, roast chicken, mushroom dishes and earthy fare — and one of the few reds that genuinely flatters fish.

Ageing: Fine Pinot ages gracefully, trading primary fruit for savoury, gamey, forest-floor complexity over 5–15+ years; everyday versions are best young and fresh.

Value & myth: Top Burgundy is eye-wateringly expensive, which scares people off — but excellent, affordable Pinot now comes from Oregon, New Zealand, Chile, Germany and Burgundy’s outer villages. “Pale equals weak” is the biggest beginner myth: with Pinot, that translucent colour hides remarkable depth and length.

Food pairing. Silky and medium-bodied, Pinot Noir is wonderfully food-friendly: salmon, duck, mushroom dishes, and roast chicken. Its cherry and earthy notes pop with thyme, beetroot, and a touch of smoke.
