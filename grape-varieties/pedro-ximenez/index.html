The sweetest, darkest wine on the shelf

Pedro Ximénez — almost always written as PX — is a pale white grape that gets turned into one of the most extreme wines in the world: a near-black, syrup-thick, intensely sweet fortified wine from southern Spain.

The trick is sunshine. The grapes are picked ripe, then laid out on mats in the open air to raisin for a week or two. As they shrivel, the water evaporates and the sugar concentrates to staggering levels. The result is more like liquefied raisins than anything you’d call a glass of white wine.

It matters because PX is the benchmark for a whole category of “dessert in a glass” wines, and because, used in small doses, it sweetens and deepens many other Sherries you’ll meet. Tasting it once tells you what extreme ripeness and oxidation can do.

Styles to know

Sweet PX Sherry (the classic): Opaque brown-black, thick enough to coat the glass, with stratospheric sweetness balanced by a savoury, coffee-and-molasses edge. This is what people mean by “a PX.”

Aged & solera PX: Long oxidative aging (often via a solera — a stack of barrels where older and younger wines are gradually blended) builds layers of dried fig, date, espresso and old leather. Old bottlings (VOS/VORS, 20–30+ years) are profound and pricey.

As a sweetener: PX is also blended in tiny amounts into cream Sherries and some sweetened Olorosos to round them out — so you often taste it without realising.

Dry table wine (rare): In a handful of places PX is made into a dry, fairly neutral white, but this is the exception, not the rule.

Where it shines

PX is a southern-Spain speciality, made under the hot Andalusian sun where grapes can be dried outdoors reliably.

• Montilla-Moriles — PX’s true home, where it’s the main grape and the warm climate ripens it to perfection. • Jerez (Sherry country) — uses PX for its sweet Sherries, often buying raisined grapes or wine from Montilla. • Málaga — historic sweet wines that can also feature PX.

Climate is everything here: you need relentless sun to raisin the fruit, so PX rarely makes sense in cooler regions.

What shapes it

Sun-drying (the defining step): Laying the picked grapes out to raisin in the open air is what transforms an ordinary pale white grape into a syrup. The longer they shrivel, the more the water evaporates and the sugar concentrates — this single process drives the wine’s sweetness, density and dark colour.

Oxidative aging: After fermentation is stopped and the wine fortified, long aging in barrel (and time exposed to air) deepens the colour further and builds the dried-fruit, coffee and leather complexity. The wine is meant to brown and concentrate, not stay fresh.

The solera system: Most serious PX is matured by fractionally blending across stacked barrels of different ages, smoothing out vintage variation and lending consistency and layered, mellow depth.

Good to know

Serving & pairing: A small pour goes a long way. It’s a dessert in itself, but it’s magic poured over vanilla ice cream, or alongside dark chocolate, blue cheese, coffee tart, or sticky toffee pudding.

Aging: Already oxidative and stable, sweet PX is essentially indestructible — an opened bottle keeps for weeks or months, and unopened bottles last for years.

Common confusion: PX is a white grape even though the wine is black — the colour comes from sun-drying and oxidation, not from dark skins. And don’t confuse Pedro Ximénez the grape with Palomino, the (different) grape behind dry Fino and Manzanilla Sherry.

Value: Even modest bottlings deliver huge flavour, so a half-bottle stretches across many desserts. Excellent bang for the buck.

Food pairing. Lusciously sweet and syrupy, PX is a dessert in a glass. Pour it over vanilla ice cream, or pair with dark chocolate, blue cheese, and dried figs to echo its raisin, fig, and molasses richness.

Producers to seek out for Pedro Ximénez

To taste Pedro Ximénez at its sweetest and most concentrated, look to the sun-baked bodegas of Montilla-Moriles and Jerez, where the grapes are dried on mats before pressing.

1

Alvear

Montilla-Moriles PX benchmark; the region’s oldest bodega, famed for its Solera 1927

2

Toro Albalá

Montilla-Moriles specialist, celebrated for its vintage-dated Don PX

3

González Byass

Noé, a deeply concentrated old-solera Pedro Ximénez from Jerez

4

Emilio Lustau

San Emilio, a raisiny, treacle-dark Pedro Ximénez from Jerez

Valdespino

El Candado, a sun-dried Pedro Ximénez from one of Jerez’s oldest houses
