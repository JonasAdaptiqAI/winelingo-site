Bordeaux’s secret weapon

Petit Verdot is the classic “seasoning grape” of Bordeaux: deeply coloured, firmly tannic and packed with violet perfume, but historically used in tiny amounts — often just a few percent of a blend.

Its job in a Bordeaux blend is to add structure and lift: a shot of inky colour, extra grip, and a distinctive floral, peppery top note that the main grapes (Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot) don’t provide. A little goes a long way.

It matters because it shows how blending works — great red Bordeaux isn’t one grape but a recipe, and Petit Verdot is the spice that ties it together. In warmer New World regions it has also stepped out as a bold variety in its own right.

Styles to know

As a blending grape (the traditional role): A small slice of a Bordeaux-style blend, where you’d notice it as extra colour, a firmer backbone and a violet-and-spice fragrance rather than as a wine on its own.

As a single varietal (New World): In warm regions it ripens fully and is bottled solo — full-bodied, dark and tannic, with blackberry, violet and black-pepper notes. Think of it as Cabernet Sauvignon’s bigger, more perfumed cousin.

Aromas & flavours: blackberry, violet, black pepper, leather, sage. Powerful and savoury rather than sweet-fruited.

Where it shines

Petit Verdot ripens late and needs warmth, which shapes where it succeeds.

• Bordeaux, France — its homeland, where it’s used in small doses in the great Médoc blends. In cool vintages it can struggle to ripen, which is partly why it stays a minor player. • Spain (Castilla–La Mancha), Australia, California, Argentina (Mendoza), South Africa — warm New World sites where it ripens easily and is increasingly bottled as a varietal.

The through-line is heat: give Petit Verdot reliable sun and it goes from a bit-part blender to a confident soloist.

What shapes it

Ripeness: This is the big one. Under-ripe, Petit Verdot is harsh and green; fully ripe, it’s plush and floral. That’s why its fortunes flip so dramatically between cool Bordeaux and warm New World vineyards.

Oak ageing: Time in oak barrels softens its firm tannins and adds notes of vanilla, cedar and spice, helping tame its naturally grippy structure. (“Tannin” is the drying, gum-gripping sensation in red wine; oak rounds it off.)

Small berries: Petit Verdot has thick skins relative to its juice, which is exactly why it delivers so much colour and tannin per grape — useful for boosting a pale, soft blend.

Good to know

Food: Its power loves rich, savoury food — roast lamb, grilled steak, hard aged cheeses, anything with char and fat to match the tannin.

Ageing: Built to last. The colour, tannin and acidity that make it useful in blends also mean varietal versions can age for a decade or more, softening into leather and dried-herb complexity.

Name & myth: “Petit” (“small”) refers to its small berries, not a light wine — it’s one of the bigger, more tannic reds out there. Don’t confuse it with Petite Sirah, a completely unrelated grape with a confusingly similar name.

Food pairing. Deep, firm and powerful, Petit Verdot wants rich red meat: roast beef, braised short ribs, and venison. Its violet, blackberry, and spice notes pop with peppercorn, thyme, and charred edges off the grill.

Producers to seek out for Petit Verdot

Usually a supporting player in Bordeaux blends, Petit Verdot steps into the spotlight as a varietal wine in warmer spots of Spain, Australia and a handful of Médoc estates.

1

Marqués de Griñón

2

Pirramimma

McLaren Vale estate long admired for its ripe varietal Petit Verdot

3

Château Belle-Vue

Haut-Médoc estate, among the first in Bordeaux to bottle Petit Verdot on its own

4

Château Bolaire

Bordeaux Supérieur property planted with an unusually high share of Petit Verdot
