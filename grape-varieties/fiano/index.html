An ancient white worth rediscovering

Fiano is one of southern Italy’s oldest and most distinctive white grapes, grown around Avellino in Campania (the region behind Naples) since Roman times. After nearly disappearing in the 20th century, it has roared back as one of Italy’s most exciting whites — and for good reason.

What sets Fiano apart is texture and depth. Where many southern Italian whites are simple and crisp, Fiano is medium-bodied, rich and almost waxy, with a savoury, nutty, honeyed character layered over orchard fruit and flowers. It feels substantial in the mouth.

Expect honey, hazelnut, pear and orange-blossom notes with a smoky, mineral undertow. It’s a white for people who like their whites with body and complexity rather than just zing — closer in spirit to a fine white Burgundy than to a light Italian apéritif wine.

Styles to know

Fiano di Avellino DOCG (the benchmark): From the cooler hills around Avellino, this is the grape at its finest — structured, mineral, age-worthy and complex, with a savoury, smoky depth. The reference point for serious Fiano.

Coastal & southern Fiano (incl. Sicily, Puglia): Warmer sites give a rounder, riper, more immediately fruity style — softer and friendlier, lovely young.

Unoaked vs. oaked: Most Fiano is made in stainless steel to preserve its purity and that nutty, mineral character. Some ambitious producers use a little oak or extended lees ageing for extra richness and a creamier texture.

Whichever style, the through-line is the same: a textured, savoury white that’s fuller than you expect from the Italian south.

What shapes it

Altitude is everything in Avellino: the best Fiano grows in cool, high-altitude hill vineyards inland from the coast. The cooler nights preserve acidity and slow ripening, giving the wine its freshness, mineral cut and ability to age — warmer lowland sites give riper but simpler wine.

Lees ageing & bâtonnage: “Lees” are the spent yeast cells left after fermentation. Leaving the wine resting on them (and sometimes stirring them, a technique called bâtonnage) adds body, a creamy texture and a savoury, bready, nutty depth — perfectly suited to Fiano’s naturally rich style.

Soils — clay and limestone, not mainly volcanic: Fiano di Avellino’s hills are largely calcareous clay and limestone, with only some volcanic influence. People sometimes reach for “volcanic” to explain the wine’s smoky, flinty edge, but that label fits neighbouring Campanian zones better than Avellino — here the mineral character owes more to those cool, calcareous hillside soils.

Restraint with oak: Because Fiano already has so much natural texture and flavour, most winemakers keep oak subtle so the grape’s own honeyed, nutty character leads.

Good to know

It ages — unusually for an Italian white: Good Fiano di Avellino can develop beautifully over 5–10 years, gaining honeyed, toasty, almost waxy complexity. Many Italian whites are best young; Fiano is a genuine exception.

Food: Its body and savoury depth handle richer dishes than most whites — roast chicken, creamy pasta, grilled fish, soft cheeses, even mild pork. A great choice when a light white would feel too thin.

Serving: Don’t over-chill it. Too cold and you mute the honeyed, nutty aromatics; around 10–12°C lets the texture and perfume show.

Not the same as Greco: Fiano and Greco are Campania’s two great whites and often confused. Roughly: Fiano is rounder, nuttier and more honeyed; Greco is firmer, more mineral and citrus-driven.

Food pairing. Textured and nutty, this southern Italian white loves grilled fish, roast chicken and creamy pasta. Its honeyed pear and hazelnut tones pair gorgeously with toasted nuts, lemon and herbs.
