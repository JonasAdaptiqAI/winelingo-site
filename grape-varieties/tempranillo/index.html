Why Tempranillo matters

Tempranillo is Spain’s flagship red grape and the backbone of its most famous wines. If Spain had a single signature, this would be it.

The name comes from temprano, Spanish for “early” — it ripens early, well before most other red grapes. That suits Spain’s high, sun-baked plateaus, where summers are hot and dry but nights turn cold.

On its own it is medium-bodied with moderate, ripe tannins (the drying, grippy compound from grape skins) and gentle acidity. That balance makes it approachable, but its real magic shows when it meets oak and time.

Aromas & flavours: red cherry, dried fig, plum, leather, tobacco, dill and vanilla from oak.

Styles to know

Tempranillo spans a huge range, often signposted on the label by ageing terms rather than the grape name itself.

Joven (young): little or no oak. Bright, juicy, cherry-fruited and meant for drinking now. Fresh and easygoing.

Crianza / Reserva / Gran Reserva: these are Spanish legal terms for how long a wine has aged in barrel and bottle before release — Crianza is the youngest, Gran Reserva the oldest (often 5+ years). The longer the ageing, the more savoury, silky and complex the wine, trading bright fruit for leather, tobacco and dried fruit.

Modern “high-expression”: a richer, darker, more concentrated style using new oak and riper grapes, especially from Ribera del Duero. Powerful and bold.

Rosado (rosé) and blends: Tempranillo also makes crisp pink wines and is the key partner in Rioja blends, often softened with Garnacha (Grenache).

Where it shines

Three Spanish regions define Tempranillo, and they taste noticeably different.

• Rioja — the classic. Elegant, savoury, with red fruit, leather and the telltale sweet-dill note of American oak. Often blended with Garnacha. The benchmark for graceful, age-worthy Tempranillo.

• Ribera del Duero — higher and colder, giving darker, denser, more powerful wines with firmer structure. Here it’s often called Tinto Fino or Tinta del País.

• Toro — hotter and burlier still, producing inky, high-alcohol reds where the grape goes by Tinta de Toro.

Beyond Spain it stars in Portugal’s Douro and Dão (as Tinta Roriz or Aragonez), and appears in Argentina, Australia and the US — usually in a juicier, fruit-forward style.

What shapes it

Oak is the big one. Tempranillo loves barrel ageing, and the type of oak leaves a clear fingerprint. Traditional Rioja uses American oak, which adds sweet vanilla, coconut and dill. Modern producers favour French oak for subtler spice and tighter structure. Ageing in oak means the wine rests in wooden barrels, which slowly add flavour and soften the texture.

Altitude and climate. Grown high and cool (Ribera del Duero), it keeps acidity and makes structured, ageable wine. Grown hot and low (Toro), it turns riper, darker and more alcoholic.

Blending. Because Tempranillo can lack acidity and aromatic lift on its own, it’s frequently blended — with Garnacha for fruit and body, or Graciano and Mazuelo for acidity and colour.

Good to know

Food: a natural with roast lamb, grilled meats, chorizo and aged Manchego cheese. The savoury, leathery older styles love slow-cooked stews.

Ageing: Reserva and Gran Reserva wines can age gracefully for a decade or more, softening into leather, dried fruit and forest floor. Many are already cellar-aged when you buy them, so they’re ready to drink — a rare gift.

Value: Crianza-level Rioja is one of the wine world’s great bargains — genuine complexity for a modest price.

Common confusion: “Rioja” is a place, not a grape. Most red Rioja is Tempranillo-based, but the label tells you the region, not the variety.

Food pairing. Tempranillo is a natural with roasts and grilled meats, especially lamb, chorizo, and slow-cooked stews. Its savoury, leathery edge pops alongside dill, smoked paprika, and tomato.

Producers to seek out for Tempranillo

To taste Tempranillo at its best, look to Spain’s two great heartlands — the elegant, savoury reds of Rioja and the deeper, firmer wines of Ribera del Duero.

1

Vega Sicilia

Ribera del Duero estate whose Único is Spain’s most celebrated Tempranillo (Tinto Fino).

2

López de Heredia

Rioja’s great traditionalist, famed for the long-aged Viña Tondonia.

3

La Rioja Alta

Rioja house known for its cellar-aged Gran Reserva 890 and 904.

4

Marqués de Murrieta

Historic Rioja estate behind the long-lived Castillo Ygay Gran Reserva.

5

Dominio de Pingus

Peter Sisseck’s cult Ribera del Duero Tempranillo from old vines.

FAQ

Questions & answers

Is Tempranillo a red or white wine?

Tempranillo is a red wine grape. Spain’s flagship grape, the backbone of Rioja.

What food pairs with Tempranillo?

Tempranillo is a natural with roasts and grilled meats, especially lamb, chorizo, and slow-cooked stews. Its savoury, leathery edge pops alongside dill, smoked paprika, and tomato.

How should I serve Tempranillo?

Serve at 16–18°C / 61–64°F. Use a large-bowl red glass. Decant: 30–60 minutes.
