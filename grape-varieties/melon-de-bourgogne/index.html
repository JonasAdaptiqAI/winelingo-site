The quiet hero of Muscadet

Despite the Burgundian name, this grape’s home is the opposite end of France — the Atlantic mouth of the Loire, around Nantes, where it makes the wine called Muscadet. (Confusingly, “Muscadet” is the wine and the place; “Melon de Bourgogne” is the grape. It has nothing to do with Muscat/Moscato either.)

On its own, Melon is almost shy: pale, bone-dry, high in acid, and low on obvious fruit. That neutrality is exactly the point. It is a blank, crisp canvas built to frame food rather than shout over it.

Why it matters: in a world of richer, oakier whites, Muscadet is the go-to for something lean, salty and refreshing — and it is one of the great value buys in French wine.

What shapes it: “sur lie”

The single most important thing to understand about good Muscadet is a French phrase on the label: sur lie (“on the lees”).

Lees are the spent yeast cells left over after fermentation — a fine sediment at the bottom of the tank. Most wines are pumped off this sediment quickly. Sur lie Muscadet is instead left resting on it through the winter and bottled straight off the lees.

What it does: the lees add a subtle creaminess, a faint yeasty/bready note (think the smell of fresh bread dough), and sometimes a tiny prickle of CO2 — without ever making the wine taste oaky or sweet. It turns a thin, simple white into something with texture and a salty, mineral tang.

On the label: look for “Muscadet Sèvre et Maine sur lie.” Sèvre et Maine is the best sub-zone; “sur lie” is your quality signal.

Styles to know

Everyday sur lie Muscadet: light, dry, zesty — green apple, lemon, a whiff of sea air. Drink it young and cold. The classic, inexpensive oyster wine.

Cru Communal Muscadet: a newer, serious tier from named villages like Clisson, Gorges and Le Pallet. These are aged much longer on the lees — the minimum varies by village but runs well beyond two years (Clisson, for example, requires at least two years, and many are kept longer still). The result is richer, deeper and built to age — a revelation if you only know cheap Muscadet.

Aged Muscadet: with bottle time, top examples develop honey, dried herbs and a waxy, almost Chablis-like complexity. Most people drink Muscadet far too young to ever see this.

Good to know

The food match: Muscadet and shellfish is one of wine’s perfect pairings. The high acidity and saline edge cut through oysters, mussels, clams and prawns. A plate of fruits de mer and a cold bottle of sur lie is the whole point.

Aromas & flavours: green apple, lemon, white flowers, wet stone, a sea-salt/saline tang.

Best examples: Muscadet Sèvre et Maine sur lie; Cru Communal wines from Clisson, Gorges or Le Pallet.

Common confusion: Melon de Bourgogne (this grape) is unrelated to Muscat, to Moscato, and to the white Burgundy grape Chardonnay. The Burgundian name is a historical leftover, nothing more.

Food pairing. The classic Muscadet grape is crisp, saline, and made for shellfish — oysters, mussels, and fried calamari shine alongside it. Lean into briny, lemony, and herb-flecked flavours to make it sing.

Producers to seek out for Melon de Bourgogne

To taste Melon de Bourgogne at its best, seek out cru bottlings from Muscadet Sèvre et Maine aged sur lie, where a humble grape turns saline and textured.

1

Domaine de la Pépière

Muscadet Sèvre et Maine reference, including the old-vine cru Clos des Briords.

2

Domaine de l’Écu

Pioneering biodynamic Muscadet Sèvre et Maine, bottled by single soil type.

3

Domaine Luneau-Papin

Long-lived, mineral Muscadet Sèvre et Maine from the heart of the Pays Nantais.

4

Domaines Landron

Organic Muscadet Sèvre et Maine from Jo Landron at La Louvetrie.

5

Michel Brégeon

Structured Muscadet from the granite of the Gorges cru.
