The grape that actually smells like grapes

Moscato is the friendly, floral, openly sweet white that wins people over instantly — and it is one of the very few grapes whose wine genuinely smells of fresh grapes. Layer on orange blossom, peach and honeysuckle and you have one of wine’s most charming, immediate perfumes.

The Moscato most people meet is Moscato d’Asti from Piedmont: gently fizzy, low in alcohol (often around 5–6%), sweet and grapey. It is light, refreshing and very easy to love.

A note on names: “Moscato” is the Italian for the Muscat family of grapes. The best, most aromatic member — and the one behind Moscato d’Asti — is Muscat Blanc à Petits Grains (“small-berried white Muscat”). “Muscat” and “Moscato” are the same word in different languages.

Why it’s sweet but not heavy: how it’s made

The magic of Moscato d’Asti is that it is sweet and fizzy yet feather-light, and that comes from a clever technique.

Stopping fermentation early: in fermentation, yeast eats grape sugar and turns it into alcohol. For Moscato d’Asti, winemakers chill the wine to halt the yeast partway through — so plenty of natural grape sugar is left unconverted (the sweetness) and the alcohol stays very low.

The gentle fizz (frizzante): the CO2 produced by that same fermentation is trapped in the tank, giving a soft, frothy sparkle rather than the firm bubbles of Champagne. This whole “one tank, stop early” approach is sometimes called the Asti method.

Why it doesn’t cloy: Moscato keeps just enough acidity to balance the sugar, so it tastes fresh and juicy rather than syrupy.

Styles to know

Moscato d’Asti: lightly sparkling (frizzante), sweet, low-alcohol, the delicate benchmark.

Asti Spumante: fully sparkling (spumante), sweet and a bit more alcoholic and frothy than Moscato d’Asti — the same grape, fizzier.

Dry Muscat: in Alsace and elsewhere, the same grape is made bone-dry — startlingly perfumed and grapey on the nose but not sweet on the palate. A great trick for fooling tasters.

Sweet, richer Muscats: fortified or late-harvest versions, from amber Muscat de Beaumes-de-Venise (southern France) to Australia’s intense, treacly Rutherglen Muscat — luscious dessert wines from the same family.

Orange Muscat & Moscato Rosa: aromatic pink and orange-tinged offshoots used for sweet, fragrant wines.

Good to know

Aromas & flavours: fresh grape, orange blossom, peach, honeysuckle, mandarin, elderflower.

Food & serving: serve cold. Moscato d’Asti is a lovely partner to fresh fruit, light fruit tarts and panettone, and its low alcohol and gentle sweetness make it a great brunch or aperitif wine. Sweet Moscato also tames spicy food — chilli heat is softened by a touch of sugar.

A great beginner’s wine — without snobbery: Moscato d’Asti is genuinely well-made and serious in its own right, not a “training-wheels” wine to grow out of. Even experts keep it in the fridge.

Drink it young: the appeal is fresh, grapey fruit and lively fizz, so buy recent vintages and don’t cellar it.

Food pairing. Lightly sweet and gently fizzy, Moscato loves fresh fruit, light pastries, and not-too-sweet desserts. Brighten it with peach, apricot, and honey, or serve it with a fruit tart.
