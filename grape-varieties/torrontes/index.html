Argentina’s aromatic white

Torrontés (tor-on-TESS) is Argentina’s signature white grape and one of the great aromatic surprises in wine. Lift the glass and it smells intensely floral and exotic — yet the wine itself is bone-dry and crisp. That contrast is its whole charm.

It fools people constantly: the perfumed nose promises something sweet, but the palate is fresh, zesty and clean.

There are actually three Torrontés varieties in Argentina; the finest and most aromatic is Torrontés Riojano, the one behind the best bottles.

Aromas & flavours: rose petal, orange blossom, jasmine, peach, white grape and a twist of fresh citrus.

Styles to know

Torrontés is usually made in one core style, with a couple of variations worth knowing.

Fresh, dry & unoaked: the classic. Picked to keep acidity high, fermented in steel tanks (which add no flavour of their own, just preserve purity), and bottled young. Floral, lively and refreshing — drink it within a year or two of the vintage.

Off-dry versions: some producers leave a touch of sweetness to echo the perfumed aromas. Pleasant and easy, but the dry style is the benchmark.

A note on balance: because the grape is so aromatic and can be low in acid if grown in the heat, the best examples come from high, cool vineyards that keep it zippy. Poorly made versions can taste flat or oddly soapy (a slippery, detergent-like flatness on the finish) — so region matters.

Where it shines

Altitude is the secret to great Torrontés, and Argentina has it in spades.

• Salta / Cafayate (northern Argentina) — the gold standard. Most vineyards here sit around 1,500–1,900 metres, among the highest commercial vines on Earth (the region’s record-holding red plots climb even higher). The thin air and fierce sun build aroma, while cold nights lock in acidity. Intense, precise, electrifying.

• La Rioja (Argentina) — the grape’s namesake home, producing softer, rounder, more floral examples.

• Mendoza — Argentina’s biggest wine region makes plenty of reliable, fruity Torrontés.

It’s almost entirely an Argentine story; you’ll rarely see it grown well anywhere else.

What shapes it

Altitude is everything. High vineyards get strong sunlight (for aroma and ripeness) and very cold nights (for acidity). That huge day-night temperature swing is why Salta’s wines are so vivid and balanced.

Picking date. Harvest too late and the grape loses acid and turns flabby; pick at the right moment and it stays crisp and lifted. Timing is critical with such an aromatic variety.

Winemaking restraint. The best Torrontés is made simply — cool fermentation in neutral steel, no oak — to protect those delicate floral perfumes. Oak would smother them, so it’s almost never used.

Good to know

Food: a dream with spicy and aromatic food — Thai, Indian, Vietnamese — where its floral lift and freshness tame the heat. Also great with ceviche, sushi, goat cheese and herby salads.

Serving: drink it well chilled. It’s at its most exuberant cold, straight from the fridge.

The big myth: “smells sweet, so it must taste sweet.” Most Torrontés is completely dry. The perfume is aroma, not sugar — a perfect lesson in how the nose can mislead the palate.

Drink young: this is a freshness-driven wine. Buy the most recent vintage you can find and enjoy it within a couple of years.

Food pairing. Torrontés is wonderfully aromatic and suits spiced, fragrant dishes like Thai curry, ceviche, and herby salads. Lean into its floral lift with lime, cilantro, and ginger.

Producers to seek out for Torrontés

For fragrant, floral Torrontés at its finest, look to the high-altitude vineyards of Salta and the Cafayate valley in northern Argentina.

1

Colomé

Salta estate making Torrontés from some of the world’s highest vineyards.

2

El Esteco

Cafayate producer with old-vine Torrontés under its Don David and Old Vines labels.

3

Susana Balbo

Argentine winemaker known for her signature and Crios Torrontés.

4

El Porvenir de Cafayate

Cafayate family winery whose Laborum bottling showcases the grape.
