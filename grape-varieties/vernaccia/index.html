Why Vernaccia matters

Vernaccia di San Gimignano is one of Italy’s most historic white wines — it was the first wine in the country to receive official DOC status, back in 1966. It comes from the towered hilltop town of San Gimignano in Tuscany, a region otherwise ruled by red Sangiovese.

The grape (more precisely Vernaccia di San Gimignano, to distinguish it from unrelated ‘Vernaccias’ elsewhere) makes a dry, medium-bodied white with a distinctive bitter-almond twist on the finish — a savoury, slightly nutty edge that sets it apart from softer, fruitier whites.

Aromas & flavours: green apple, lemon, pear, white flowers, a hallmark bitter-almond and mineral finish.

It’s crisp, food-friendly and unshowy — a wine of texture and grip rather than perfume.

Styles to know

Vernaccia is fairly focused stylistically, but the gap between entry and top tier is real.

Fresh & unoaked (everyday): Steel-fermented, bright and zesty with that almond bite. Light-to-medium body, clean and dry. Drink young.

Riserva & barrel-influenced: The best producers age the wine longer, sometimes on its lees (spent yeast, adding body) or in barrel, building texture, a waxy richness and the ability to age several years. These reveal the grape’s serious side — more honeyed, more complex, more mineral.

Unlike many Italian whites, well-made Vernaccia develops attractively with a few years in bottle, gaining nutty and saline depth while keeping its backbone of acidity.

Where it shines

This is a single-origin specialty, which makes it easy to navigate.

• Vernaccia di San Gimignano (Tuscany) — essentially the only place it matters. The DOCG zone surrounds the famous medieval town, and the wine has been made here for centuries.

Within that, the variation comes from producer and ambition rather than sub-region. Sandy and clay soils, plus the Tuscan hill climate, give a wine that’s riper than a northern Italian white but firmer and more savoury than southern whites.

Best examples: Montenidoli, Panizzi, Teruzzi, San Quirico.

A tasting tip: try a fresh bottle next to a Riserva from a top grower to see how much texture and length the grape can develop — the leap surprises most people.

Good to know

Beware the name. ‘Vernaccia’ is one of the most confusing words in wine — it’s been used for many unrelated grapes across Italy. Vernaccia di Oristano (Sardinia) is a completely different grape making a sherry-like oxidative wine, and Vernaccia di Serrapetrona (Marche) is a red sparkling. The famous Tuscan white is Vernaccia di San Gimignano specifically — and because the name was so widely used, historic references to ‘Vernaccia’ (including Dante’s mention in the Divine Comedy) can’t reliably be pinned to the San Gimignano white.

Food: Its almond-edged dryness suits Tuscan classics — pici pasta, white-bean dishes, pecorino cheese, roast chicken, and lighter seafood.

Value: Reasonably priced for its pedigree, especially given it’s a genuinely historic, age-worthy wine.

Don’t over-chill it. Too cold and you mute the texture and almond finish that make it interesting — cool, not icy, is best.

Food pairing. Vernaccia is crisp with a gentle bitter twist, great with antipasti, roast chicken, and fried artichokes. Its lemony bite plays nicely with almond, thyme, and olive oil.
