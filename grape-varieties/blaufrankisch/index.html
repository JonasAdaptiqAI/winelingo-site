Why it matters

Blaufränkisch (BLAU-frenk-ish) is Central Europe’s great underrated red — Austria’s signature dark grape, also widely grown in Hungary (as Kékfrankos) and Germany (as Lemberger).

It strikes a distinctive middle ground: dark berry and cherry fruit, a peppery, spicy lift, firm-but-fine tannin and refreshing acidity. Many tasters reach for comparisons to Syrah for the spice and Pinot Noir for the freshness.

It’s a grape that tastes of place — savoury, mineral and energetic rather than sweet and jammy — which is exactly why sommeliers love it.

Styles to know

Fresh & juicy: lighter, unoaked bottlings full of crunchy red and black fruit with a peppery snap — superb lightly chilled.

Structured & oak-aged: from top sites, a deeper, firmer, spicier wine with real tannin and ageing potential, often rested in oak.

Rosé: as a dry pink it’s vivid, peppery and bone-dry.

In blends: it’s a key partner in Austria’s celebrated red blends, where it lends structure and spice. The fork to watch is fruity-and-fresh versus serious-and-savoury.

Where it shines

Burgenland, Austria is the benchmark, especially around the warm, shallow lake Neusiedlersee and the slate and limestone hills of areas like Leithaberg and Mittelburgenland (nicknamed “Blaufränkischland”).

• Warmer lake sites: riper, rounder, fuller wines. • Cooler slate slopes: more mineral, peppery and elegant.

Hungary (as Kékfrankos) makes everything from everyday reds to serious bottles, and it’s the dominant grape in modern Egri Bikavér (“Bull’s Blood”), the famous spicy Hungarian blend.

Germany (as Lemberger) and a growing band of US producers (Washington State, the Finger Lakes) round out its range — proof of how well it travels.

What shapes it

Soil character: Blaufränkisch is famously transparent to where it grows. Limestone and slate are widely linked to its mineral, peppery elegance, while warmer sites give riper fruit.

Oak ageing: barrels round its firm tannin and add spice and depth. Restrained oak keeps the wine savoury and fresh; heavier new oak makes it richer and more international in style.

Tannin & acidity: the grape brings both grip (drying tannin) and zip (acidity), which is why it pairs so well with food and can age — but also why over-extraction can make it austere.

Whole-bunch / cooler picking: some growers ferment with whole clusters or pick earlier to emphasise that signature pepper and freshness.

Good to know

Three names, one grape: Blaufränkisch (Austria) = Kékfrankos (Hungary) = Lemberger (Germany/USA). Spotting any of these means the same wine.

Food: its acidity and pepper love hearty fare — goulash, roast pork, duck, mushrooms, grilled sausages and paprika dishes.

Not Cabernet Franc: the old nickname “Lemberger” and the “Franc” sound cause mix-ups, but it’s a completely separate variety.

Ageing & value: serious examples can develop for a decade, and because the grape is still under the radar, it remains one of the best-value structured reds in Europe. Try it lightly chilled in summer.

Food pairing. Blaufränkisch is peppery and medium-bodied, loving roast pork, mushroom dishes, and spiced sausages. Lean into blackberry, black pepper, and a touch of smoky spice.
