Big, inky and built like a tank

Petite Sirah is one of the most intense, darkly coloured reds you can buy — opaque purple-black, massively tannic and full-bodied, with brooding blueberry and pepper fruit.

Despite the name, it is not Syrah and not “petite” in any sense that matters to the drinker. Botanically it’s a French grape called Durif — a 19th-century crossing of Syrah and an obscure variety, Peloursin, bred in south-east France. The “petite” refers only to its small berries.

It matters as the go-to grape for people who want maximum colour, grip and power in a glass — and as a textbook example of how a confusing name can hide a wine’s true identity.

Styles to know

Bold varietal Petite Sirah: The classic — inky, full-bodied and seriously tannic, with blueberry, blackberry, black pepper, plum and dark chocolate. Big, structured and often oak-aged. Not a shy wine.

Blending component: Its dense colour and tannin make it a popular splash in blends, where it props up softer grapes like Zinfandel (the two are a long-standing California pairing) with backbone and depth.

Aromas & flavours: blueberry, blackberry, black pepper, dark chocolate, plum. Powerful and savoury, with a grippy, mouth-coating texture.

Where it shines

Though it was bred in France, Petite Sirah found fame far from home and is now overwhelmingly a New World wine.

• California, USA — the heartland of the named-varietal “Petite Sirah” style; warm sites and old-vine plantings give the ripe, concentrated, age-worthy wine it’s famous for. • Rutherglen, Australia — its other New World stronghold, usually labelled under the original name Durif, producing equally bold, dark and powerful reds. • Parts of South America (and Israel) — smaller plantings in a similarly inky register.

The warm-climate connection is the key: this grape needs heat and sun to ripen its thick skins fully, which is why cooler regions — including much of its French birthplace — rarely bother with it.

What shapes it

Small berries, thick skins: Almost everything about the wine flows from this. More skin per drop of juice means more colour, tannin and flavour extracted — the source of that inky look and chewy grip.

Oak ageing: Long ageing in oak barrels is common, softening the fierce tannins and layering in vanilla, mocha and spice. (Tannin is the drying, astringent feel; oak helps tame it and adds richness.)

Old vines: Some of California’s prized Petite Sirah comes from very old vineyards, which naturally yield smaller crops of more concentrated fruit — adding further to the wine’s density.

Good to know

Food: Match its power with equally bold food — barbecue, braised short ribs, burgers, peppered steak, anything smoky and rich. It will flatten delicate dishes.

Ageing: With that colour, tannin and acidity, good Petite Sirah ages impressively, softening over 10–15+ years into something more savoury and leathery. Young bottles benefit from decanting.

The name trap: Petite Sirah (= Durif) is not Syrah and not Petit Verdot — three different grapes with maddeningly similar names. If a wine just says “Durif,” it’s the same thing as Petite Sirah.

Food pairing. Big, inky and tannic, Petite Sirah is made for barbecue: smoked brisket, ribs, and burgers. Its blueberry and black-pepper punch loves smoky, charred, and molasses-glazed flavours.

Producers to seek out for Petite Sirah

For inky, powerful Petite Sirah, California is the home ground, especially old-vine plantings across Napa, Sonoma and the Livermore Valley.
