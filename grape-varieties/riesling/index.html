Riesling Wine: Taste, Grapes, Regions & Food Pairing

The aromatic white grape that can be bone-dry or lusciously sweet — and pairs with food better than almost anything.

Riesling is one of the world’s great white grapes, and one of the most misunderstood. Say the name and many people think “sweet” — but Riesling can be bone-dry, off-dry, richly sweet, or anything in between. What ties every style together is bright, mouth-watering acidity and a pure, perfumed aroma of orchard fruit and flowers.

If you like a white wine that tastes fresh and alive, Riesling is worth getting to know. Here’s what it tastes like, where it grows, the styles it makes, why it tastes the way it does, plus how to serve it and the food pairings that make it sing. No wrong answers — just a friendly map to a grape that rewards a little curiosity.

What does Riesling wine taste like?

Riesling is a light- to medium-bodied white with high acidity — that mouth-watering, zippy freshness you feel on the sides of your tongue. Because it’s white, there’s no tannin (the grippy, drying feel you get from red wines), so it comes across as clean and lively rather than heavy.

The flavours lean towards orchard and citrus fruit: green apple, lime, lemon and, in riper examples, peach and apricot. There’s often a floral lift — think jasmine or honeysuckle — and a distinctive mineral or “stony” quality that many drinkers describe as tasting like wet rock or slate.

One famous quirk: as Riesling ages, it can develop a note people call “petrol” or “kerosene.” It sounds odd, but in a good aged Riesling it’s a prized sign of maturity, sitting alongside honey and toast. If that’s not your thing, simply drink Riesling young and fresh.

Dry, off-dry or sweet? Understanding Riesling styles

Riesling’s biggest surprise is its range of sweetness. The same grape makes everything from crisp, bone-dry wines to unctuous dessert wines, and the sweetness level is a choice made in the winery, not a fixed trait of the grape.

Dry Riesling (often labelled “trocken” in Germany, or simply dry in Alsace and Australia) is crisp, refreshing and food-friendly. Off-dry Riesling has a touch of sweetness that’s balanced by the high acidity, so it tastes fresh rather than sugary — a great match for spicy food. Sweeter styles, like German Spätlese and Auslese, are richer and rounder.

At the luxurious end sit dessert wines made from grapes affected by “noble rot” (a beneficial mould that concentrates sugar and flavour) or picked frozen for ice wine. These are intensely sweet yet kept in balance by Riesling’s signature acidity. Because sweetness varies so much, it’s always worth checking the label or asking — the word “dry” or “trocken” is your friend.

  • Dry / trocken — crisp and refreshing, great with food
  • Off-dry — a hint of sweetness, brilliant with spice
  • Sweet (Spätlese, Auslese) — richer, rounder, often lower in alcohol
  • Dessert (noble rot, ice wine) — luscious and concentrated, balanced by acidity

Where does Riesling grow? Old World and New World regions

Riesling’s spiritual home is Germany, where it’s the flagship grape. The Mosel produces delicate, low-alcohol wines of piercing freshness from steep, slate-covered slopes, while regions like the Rheingau and Pfalz often make fuller, drier styles. Just across the border in France, Alsace makes Riesling in a characteristically dry, structured style.

Austria is another Old World star, producing racy, dry Rieslings, and the grape also has a long history in parts of Eastern Europe. Wherever it grows well, Riesling tends to prefer cooler climates that let it ripen slowly and hold on to its acidity.

In the New World, Australia is the standout: the Clare and Eden Valleys are famous for bone-dry Rieslings bursting with lime and zesty citrus. Washington State and New York’s Finger Lakes in the USA both make excellent examples across the sweetness spectrum, and New Zealand and cooler pockets of Canada and Chile add their own versions. It’s a true globe-trotter that still tastes unmistakably of itself.

Why does Riesling taste the way it does?

Two things explain most of Riesling’s character: it’s an aromatic grape, and it thrives in cool climates. “Aromatic” simply means the grape is naturally packed with fragrant compounds, which is why the wine smells so strongly of flowers and fruit even before you taste it.

Cool climates are the key to that trademark acidity. Grapes build sugar as they ripen and lose acidity in warmth, so growing Riesling in cooler places lets it ripen slowly — keeping the crisp, fresh backbone that makes the wine so refreshing and so good with food.

Riesling is also unusually good at expressing where it’s grown, which is why winemakers often ferment it in neutral tanks rather than oak barrels. Skipping oak keeps the pure fruit and mineral character front and centre, so a Mosel Riesling and a Clare Valley Riesling can taste noticeably different even though they’re the same grape.

How to serve Riesling

Serve Riesling well chilled, roughly 7–10°C — cooler for lighter, sweeter styles and a touch warmer for fuller, dry ones. Over-chilling can mute its lovely aromatics, so if it comes straight from a cold fridge, give it a few minutes in the glass.

A standard white-wine glass works perfectly; you don’t need anything special. Most Riesling is made to enjoy young and fresh, but the best examples — especially finer German and Alsace bottles — can age gracefully for many years, deepening into honeyed, complex flavours.

One practical bonus: many Rieslings, particularly the German ones, are lower in alcohol than the average white. That makes them a lighter, easy-going choice for a long lunch or a warm afternoon — enjoyed responsibly, of course.

Classic Riesling food pairings

Riesling is one of the most food-friendly wines there is, and its secret weapon is spice. That high acidity and (in off-dry styles) a hint of sweetness are brilliant at taming the heat of Thai, Vietnamese, Sichuan and Indian dishes, where many wines struggle.

Dry Riesling loves fresh seafood, sushi and light salads, its citrus zing echoing a squeeze of lemon. Slightly richer or off-dry styles are a classic partner for pork, ham, sausages and duck — the fruit and acidity cut through the fattiness beautifully. It also flatters soft and washed-rind cheeses.

For sweeter Rieslings, lean into fruit-based puddings like apple tart, or serve a dessert-style Riesling with blue cheese for a sweet-and-savoury treat. The guiding idea is simple: match the wine’s sweetness to the dish, and let that fresh acidity do the rest.

  • Spicy food — Thai, Vietnamese, Sichuan, Indian (off-dry shines here)
  • Pork, ham, sausages and duck
  • Sushi, sashimi and fresh seafood
  • Soft and washed-rind cheeses
  • Fruit desserts and blue cheese (with sweeter styles)

FAQ

Questions & answers

Is Riesling a sweet wine?

Not necessarily. Riesling ranges from bone-dry to lusciously sweet — the sweetness is a winemaking choice. Look for the word “dry” or the German “trocken” on the label if you want a crisp, non-sweet style.

What does Riesling taste like?

It’s a light- to medium-bodied white with high, refreshing acidity and no tannin. Expect green apple, lime, lemon, peach and floral notes, often with a stony, mineral character. Aged examples can show honey and a distinctive petrol-like note.

What food goes well with Riesling?

Riesling is superb with spicy Asian food, pork, ham, duck, sushi and fresh seafood. Off-dry styles tame chilli heat, while sweeter Rieslings pair with fruit desserts and blue cheese.

Where does the best Riesling come from?

Germany (especially the Mosel and Rheingau) is the classic home, alongside Alsace in France and Austria. In the New World, Australia’s Clare and Eden Valleys and the USA’s Washington State and Finger Lakes are renowned.

How should I serve Riesling?

Chill it to around 7–10°C and use a standard white-wine glass. Avoid over-chilling, which mutes its aromas. Most Riesling is delicious young, though top bottles can age for years.
