Dry Red Wine: What It Means, the Main Types and How to Cook With It

Why nearly every red is dry, why some still taste sweet, which dry reds to try, and which to pour into the stew.

By Jonas Egeskov · Updated 14 October 2026

A dry red wine is a red wine with little or no sugar left in it, so it does not taste sweet. Almost every red wine on a shop shelf or a restaurant list is dry, from a light, juicy Pinot Noir to a bold Cabernet Sauvignon.

Dry is a word about sugar, not about how a wine feels. The rough, mouth-drying grip some reds have comes from tannin, which is a different thing entirely. This guide covers what dry really means, why some dry reds seem sweet, and which to cook with.

What is a dry red wine?

During fermentation, yeast eats the natural sugar in grape juice and turns it into alcohol. When the yeast finishes the job, almost no sugar is left and the wine is called dry. Whatever sugar remains is called residual sugar, measured in grams per litre (g/L).

Under EU rules, a still wine can be labelled dry if it has no more than 4 g/L of residual sugar, or up to 9 g/L when its acidity is high enough to balance it. Most red wines sit well below that line, often at around 2 g/L or less, a level very few people can taste.

The part that confuses many people: dry is not the same as tannic. Tannins are compounds from grape skins, seeds and oak barrels. They bind to the proteins in your saliva and leave your gums feeling rough, a bit like strong black tea. A red can be very tannic, or soft with almost no tannin, and still be completely dry. Read more in what tannins in wine are.

Key takeaway: dry describes sugar, drying describes tannin. Almost all reds are dry, but only some are drying.

Why almost every red wine is dry

Red wine is made by fermenting the juice together with the dark grape skins, which give it colour and tannin. Winemakers usually let this fermentation run until the yeast has used up the sugar. A fully dry red is also more stable, because no sugar is left for stray yeast to start fermenting again in the bottle.

Sweet reds do exist, but they are a small, specialised group. They are made by stopping fermentation early, for example by adding grape spirit as in Port, or by using very ripe or partly dried grapes. When a red is meant to be sweet, the label or the style name usually tells you.

Red wine from dry to sweet

Think of red wine sweetness as a short scale with most bottles bunched at the dry end. For the full picture, including whites and sparkling wines, see our wine sweetness chart.

  • Dry (at most 4 g/L, or up to 9 g/L with high acidity): the vast majority of red wine, including the everyday versions of every grape listed further down this page.
  • Semi-dry, also called off-dry or medium dry (up to 12 g/L, or 18 g/L with high acidity): a hint of sweetness. Examples include Lambrusco labelled semisecco, some German reds labelled halbtrocken, often from Dornfelder, and some mass-market red blends.
  • Medium sweet (up to 45 g/L): clearly sweet but not syrupy, such as Lambrusco labelled amabile and some bottles sold simply as "sweet red".
  • Sweet (above 45 g/L): dessert reds such as Port, Recioto della Valpolicella, and Banyuls and Maury from southern France.

Tannin sits on a separate scale, which is why two equally dry reds can feel so different.

Why some dry red wines taste sweet

When a dry red seems sweet, your senses are usually reacting to something other than sugar.

  • Ripe fruit. Grapes from warm, sunny places develop jammy flavours of blackberry, plum and fig. Your brain links those smells with sweetness, even when the sugar is tiny.
  • Oak. Ageing in oak barrels, especially new ones, can add vanilla, toast, caramel and coconut. These smell like dessert, so the wine seems sweeter. American oak tends to give more vanilla and coconut than French oak; see French vs American oak.
  • Alcohol. Alcohol itself tastes faintly sweet, and riper grapes make stronger wine, so a red at 14.5% can feel rounder and sweeter than one at 12.5%.
  • Low acidity and soft tannin. Acidity and tannin make a wine feel firm. When both are low, nothing balances the fruit, so the wine reads as sweeter.
  • A little real sugar. Some popular, inexpensive reds, often described as "smooth" or "rich", are made with a few grams of residual sugar to soften them. A few carry enough to fall outside the EU definition of dry, and the front label rarely says so. Our guide to low-sugar wine explains more.

Types of dry red wine, from lighter to fuller

These are normally made dry and run roughly from lightest to fullest body. Body is how heavy a wine feels in your mouth, like the difference between skimmed and whole milk. For a wider tour, see our guide to types of red wine.

Gamay

Taste: red cherry, raspberry, cranberry, light and juicy.

Gamay is the grape of Beaujolais in France. Low tannin and fresh acidity make it one of the easiest reds to enjoy lightly chilled.

Pinot Noir

Taste: strawberry, red cherry, with earthy or mushroom notes as it ages.

Pinot Noir is light to medium in body, with gentle tannin and bright acidity. It is dry; its silky texture simply makes it feel soft.

Sangiovese

Taste: sour cherry, dried herbs, tomato leaf.

Sangiovese is the main grape of Chianti. High acidity and firm tannin make it taste distinctly dry, and very good with tomato-based food.

Grenache

Taste: strawberry, raspberry, sometimes white pepper and dried herbs.

Grenache, called Garnacha in Spain, is medium to full in body, with soft tannin and often high alcohol. That ripe fruit and warmth can make it seem sweet, though it is normally dry.

Merlot

Taste: plum, black cherry, chocolate, soft and round.

Merlot is medium to full in body with smooth, medium tannin. It is dry; its plush fruit and gentle texture are why people sometimes think otherwise.

Tempranillo

Taste: red cherry, dried fig, leather, vanilla from oak.

Tempranillo is Spain's leading red grape, at the heart of Rioja. It has medium to high tannin, and versions aged in American oak can smell of vanilla and coconut.

Zinfandel

Taste: blackberry jam, raspberry, black pepper.

Zinfandel, known as Primitivo in southern Italy, is full-bodied with medium tannin and often 14% alcohol or more, which makes it one of the most sweet-tasting dry reds. White Zinfandel, a pink wine made from the same grape, is usually off-dry to medium sweet.

Malbec

Taste: plum, blackberry, cocoa, violet.

Malbec, best known from Mendoza in Argentina, is full-bodied with ripe fruit and plenty of tannin that usually feels soft.

Syrah / Shiraz

Taste: blackberry, black pepper, smoked meat, dark chocolate.

Syrah, called Shiraz in Australia, is full-bodied with medium to high tannin. Cooler places such as the northern Rhône give peppery, savoury wines; warmer ones give riper, richer ones.

Cabernet Sauvignon

Taste: blackcurrant, cedar, mint.

Cabernet Sauvignon is full-bodied, with high tannin and fresh acidity. It tastes noticeably dry and firm, especially when young.

Nebbiolo

Taste: sour cherry, rose, tar, dried herbs.

Nebbiolo looks pale in the glass but has high tannin and high acidity. It makes Barolo in north-west Italy, one of the most drying reds in the world.

The driest-tasting red wines

Since nearly all reds carry a similarly tiny amount of sugar, the ones that taste driest have the most tannin and acidity, and the least jammy fruit. Tannin dries your mouth, while acidity makes it water and pushes fruit flavours towards tart rather than sweet.

  • Nebbiolo: high tannin and high acidity, with fruit that is more sour cherry than jam.
  • Sangiovese: bright acidity and firm tannin, with savoury, herbal flavours.
  • Cabernet Sauvignon: high tannin, especially when young, with blackcurrant rather than sweet, jammy fruit.
  • Tannat: as the name hints, one of the most tannic grapes of all, grown mainly in south-west France and Uruguay.

Climate and age matter too. Wines from cooler places tend to have more acidity and less ripe fruit, so they taste drier, and years in bottle soften tannin. Protein and fat in food, such as a steak, also soften the grip of tannin.

Tip: if you find red wine "too dry", you probably mean too tannic. Try a low-tannin red such as Gamay or Pinot Noir before you reach for a sweeter wine.

Dry red wine for cooking

A recipe that asks for dry red wine wants acidity and fruit without added sweetness. The wine reduces as it cooks, so everything in it becomes more concentrated.

What to use: a medium-bodied, fruity, dry red with moderate tannin. Merlot, Grenache-based blends such as Côtes du Rhône, Sangiovese and Tempranillo are all reliable. Beef bourguignon and coq au vin are traditionally made with red Burgundy, which is Pinot Noir.

Best dry red wine for beef stew: something you would happily drink with the finished dish, such as a Merlot, a Côtes du Rhône, a Malbec or a Shiraz. They have enough fruit and body for long cooking. A simple, lightly oaked bottle usually beats a prized one, whose subtle flavours are lost in the pot.

What to avoid:

  • Sweet reds such as Port (unless the recipe asks for it), Lambrusco amabile or anything sold as "sweet red". The sugar concentrates and can make a savoury sauce taste jammy.
  • Salted "cooking wine". Supermarket bottles labelled cooking wine usually have salt, and often preservatives, added. They make seasoning hard to control and tend to taste flat.
  • Very tannic or heavily oaked wines, which can turn harsh or bitter when reduced hard.
  • Faulty or tired wine that smells musty or of vinegar. A bottle opened a few days ago is fine if it still smells clean; see how long open wine lasts.

Cooking reduces alcohol but does not always remove all of it, especially with short cooking times, so keep that in mind when cooking for children or anyone avoiding alcohol. Wine itself is for adults of legal drinking age and is best enjoyed in moderation.

Substitute for dry red wine

With no wine to hand, aim to replace both the liquid and the gentle sourness that wine brings.

  • Stock plus vinegar: use beef, chicken or vegetable stock in the same amount as the wine, with about a tablespoon of red wine vinegar per 250 ml. Best for stews and braises.
  • Unsweetened red grape juice or pomegranate juice: gives colour and fruit. Add a splash of vinegar or lemon juice so it does not taste too sweet. Best for sauces and marinades.
  • Non-alcoholic red wine: the closest flavour match, though some are a little sweeter than regular dry red, so taste as you go.
  • Extra tomato and stock: in tomato-based sauces, a little more passata and stock adds body and acidity in place of wine.

Never use red wine vinegar undiluted: it is far too sharp.

FAQ

Questions & answers

What is a dry red wine?

A dry red wine has little or no residual sugar, so it does not taste sweet. Under EU rules a still wine counts as dry with no more than 4 grams of sugar per litre, or up to 9 grams when acidity is high. Almost all reds are dry. Dry is about sugar, not the rough, drying feel of tannin.

Is Merlot dry?

Yes. Merlot is almost always made as a dry red wine. It can seem sweet because of its ripe plum and black cherry flavours, soft tannin and often fairly high alcohol, which make it feel round and smooth. A noticeably sweet Merlot may be a mass-market style made with a little residual sugar, but that is the exception.

Is Pinot Noir dry?

Yes. Pinot Noir is a dry red wine. It is light to medium in body with gentle tannin and bright acidity, so it feels silky rather than drying, which can make people assume it is sweeter than it is. Its strawberry and red cherry flavours come from the grape, not from sugar. Serve it at 12 to 14°C.

What is a semi-dry red wine?

A semi-dry red, also called off-dry or medium dry, has a noticeable hint of sweetness. Under EU rules a still medium dry wine can have up to 12 grams of sugar per litre, or 18 grams when acidity is high. Examples include Lambrusco labelled semisecco, some German halbtrocken reds and some mass-market red blends.

What is the best dry red wine for cooking beef stew?

Choose a medium to full-bodied, fruity dry red you would happily drink with the meal, such as Merlot, Côtes du Rhône, Malbec or Shiraz. These have enough fruit and body for slow cooking. Avoid sweet reds, salted cooking wine and very tannic or heavily oaked wines, which can turn bitter as they reduce.

What can I substitute for dry red wine?

For stews and braises, use the same amount of stock with about a tablespoon of red wine vinegar per 250 ml. For sauces and marinades, try unsweetened red grape or pomegranate juice with a splash of vinegar or lemon juice. Non-alcoholic red wine is the closest match. Never swap in undiluted red wine vinegar one for one.
