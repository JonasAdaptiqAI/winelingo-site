Should Red Wine Be Chilled?

Which reds love a chill, which turn harsh when cold, fridge times for every style, and when wine belongs in the fridge at all.

By Jonas Egeskov · Updated 14 October 2026

Yes, a little. Most red wines taste best slightly below room temperature: around 12–14°C (54–57°F) for light reds and 15–18°C (59–64°F) for medium and full-bodied reds, which in practice means 20 minutes to an hour in the fridge before you pour.

The old advice to serve red wine at “room temperature” is not wrong, it is just out of date. It comes from a time when rooms were a lot cooler than a centrally heated home today. This guide covers which reds love a proper chill, which turn harsh when cold, fridge times for every style, and when wine belongs in the fridge at all.

Should red wine be chilled?

For most bottles, a light chill makes red wine taste better. A typical modern home sits around 20–22°C (68–72°F). At that temperature the alcohol in a red becomes more noticeable, a hot, slightly prickly feeling on the nose, and the fruit can turn soft and jammy. Bring the same wine down a few degrees and it tastes fresher, more defined and less boozy.

The advice to serve red at “room temperature” comes from the French word chambré, meaning a wine brought up to the temperature of the room. That advice dates from homes without central heating, where a dining room was often in the mid teens. Picture a cool old room, not a heated living room.

Chilled does not mean cold, though. A red straight from a 4°C fridge will taste closed and thin. Aim for cool: slightly cold to the touch, not icy. Our wine serving temperature chart has every style in Celsius and Fahrenheit.

Quick answer: light reds 12–14°C, about 45–60 minutes in the fridge. Medium and full reds 15–18°C, about 20–30 minutes in the fridge. Both from a room at around 21°C.

Red wines that taste best with a proper chill

The reds that welcome real chilling share three things: they are light in body, juicy and fruity, and low in tannin. Tannin is the drying, grippy feeling a red leaves on your gums, and the less of it there is, the more happily a wine takes the cold. These reds sit at the bottom of the red range, around 12–14°C.

Beaujolais and other Gamay

Taste: red cherry, raspberry and a soft, juicy feel with very little grip.

Beaujolais is made from the Gamay grape and is the classic chillable red. Simple Beaujolais and Beaujolais Nouveau can go a touch colder still; the fuller cru wines, such as Morgon, are better at the top of the range.

Light Pinot Noir

Taste: strawberry, cherry and a silky, gentle texture.

Everyday Pinot Noir from cooler climates is light and bright, and a short chill keeps its delicate fruit lively. Richer, riper Pinot from warm regions likes a degree or two more.

Lambrusco

Taste: fizzy, fruity and fresh, with dark berry flavours; it ranges from dry to sweet.

Lambrusco is a sparkling red from Emilia-Romagna in northern Italy, and it is meant to be served chilled, closer to a white than a red.

Frappato and other light Italian reds

Taste: floral, with red berries and a light, almost rosé-like weight.

Frappato is a pale, fragrant red grape from Sicily. Schiava from Alto Adige and simple Valpolicella show their perfume best when cool, too.

Zweigelt and Cinsault

Taste: juicy cherry and berry fruit, often with a peppery or herbal edge.

Austria's Zweigelt and the lighter styles of Cinsault are made for easy summer drinking and suit a proper chill. For more light reds to try, see our guide to the types of red wine.

Which red wines should not be served cold?

Big, structured reds with plenty of tannin are a different story. Cold makes tannin taste harder, drier and more bitter, and it mutes the aromas that make these wines interesting. A fridge-cold Cabernet Sauvignon or Nebbiolo can taste astringent and hollow.

That does not mean serving them warm. These wines belong at 15–18°C, still a little below a typical living room. Aim for the warmer end, around 17–18°C, for the most tannic wines, and the cooler end for juicier, rounder reds such as Merlot or young Malbec.

Rule of thumb: the more a red dries your mouth, the less cold it wants.

Which wines go in the fridge, and for how long?

White, rosé, sparkling and sweet wines are all served chilled, and reds benefit from a short spell too. The table gives the serving ranges from our chart and a rough time in a kitchen fridge (about 4°C) for a bottle starting at a room temperature of around 21°C.

Wine styleServe atTime in the fridge from room temperature (rough)
Sparkling (Champagne, Prosecco, Cava)6–10°C (43–50°F)2½–3 hours
Light white and rosé7–10°C (45–50°F)2–3 hours
Full white (for example oaked Chardonnay)10–13°C (50–55°F)1–1½ hours
Sweet and dessert wine6–10°C (43–50°F)2½–3 hours
Light red12–14°C (54–57°F)45–60 minutes
Medium and full red15–18°C (59–64°F)20–30 minutes

These timings are honest estimates, not lab results. A packed fridge, a door shelf, a thick sparkling bottle or a warm kitchen will all slow things down. A white kept in the fridge for days is colder than ideal, so take it out 10–15 minutes before pouring. Crisp whites such as Sauvignon Blanc are happy on the cold side; richer whites show more flavour a little warmer. Our guides to types of white wine and sweet wines for beginners say which is which.

Do you need to refrigerate wine?

It depends on whether the bottle is open and how long you plan to keep it. Unopened wine is stable at room temperature in the short term, so the fridge is about taste and freshness rather than safety.

SituationFridge?Why
Before opening, drinking within days or a few weeksYes for whites, rosé, sparkling and sweet; optional for redsKeeps them ready to pour. A few weeks in a kitchen fridge does no harm.
After opening, red or whiteYesCold slows the oxidation that makes open wine taste flat. Put the cork or a stopper back in first.
Long-term storage (months or years)NoA kitchen fridge is too cold and its air is usually dry, which can dry a cork over time. Store at a steady 12–13°C instead.

For longer keeping, a steady temperature matters more than the exact number. Our guide to wine storage temperature explains the details, and a screw cap removes the cork worry altogether.

Once open, everything goes in the fridge, reds included. Most still wines keep for several days this way and sparkling for one to three; see how long open wine lasts for each style. Take an open red out 30 to 60 minutes before you want to drink it so it is not served fridge-cold.

The simple rule: chill before serving, refrigerate after opening, and store long-term somewhere cool and steady, not in the kitchen fridge.

How to chill wine fast

Forgot to put the bottle in the fridge? These methods work, from fastest to slowest.

  1. Ice bucket with water and salt. Fill a bucket or deep bowl with roughly half ice and half cold water, stir in a good handful of salt and sink the bottle up to its neck. Water touches the whole bottle, which ice alone cannot, and salt lets the mix get colder than plain iced water. Expect around 10–15 minutes for a white, less for a red.
  2. The freezer, with a timer. About 10–15 minutes takes a medium or full red to a cool serving temperature and around 20 minutes a light red; a white or sparkling wine wants 30–40 minutes or so to be properly cold. Some people wrap the bottle in a damp tea towel, but home tests disagree on whether it helps. Always set a timer. A forgotten bottle can freeze after a few hours, push its cork out or crack, and sparkling wine is the riskiest.
  3. Frozen grapes in the glass. A few frozen grapes cool a glass that is already poured without watering it down.

Ice cubes work too, but they water the wine down as they melt.

Wine too cold? How to warm it up

Over-chilling is easy to fix. Cup the bowl of the glass in your hands for a few minutes and your body heat will gently raise the temperature. You will notice the aromas opening up as it warms. For a whole bottle, leave it on the table for 10–20 minutes.

Starting a little too cold beats too warm: a glass warms up quickly, but never cools down on its own. Skip hot water and radiators; patience does the job. And, as always, wine is for adults of legal drinking age and is best enjoyed in moderation.

FAQ

Questions & answers

Is red wine served cold?

Not cold, but cool. Most red wine tastes best a little below room temperature: about 12–14°C (54–57°F) for light reds such as Beaujolais or light Pinot Noir, and 15–18°C (59–64°F) for medium and full-bodied reds such as Cabernet Sauvignon or Syrah. Straight from a 4°C fridge, a red tastes closed and its tannins feel harsh, so give it a short chill rather than a long one.

Does white wine need to be refrigerated?

Before opening, no: unopened white wine keeps well in a cool, dark place, and only needs the fridge for a few hours before serving. Light whites taste best at 7–10°C and fuller whites at 10–13°C. After opening, yes: put the cork or a stopper back in and keep the bottle in the fridge, where most whites stay pleasant for several days.

Do you refrigerate red wine?

You do not need to refrigerate unopened red wine, but 20–30 minutes in the fridge before serving brings a medium or full red to its ideal 15–18°C, and light reds like a little longer. Once a red is open, refrigerate it with the cork or stopper back in. Cold slows the oxidation that makes open wine taste flat. Take it out 30 to 60 minutes before drinking.

How long to chill wine in the fridge?

From a room temperature of about 21°C, allow roughly 2–3 hours for light whites and rosé, 2½–3 hours for sparkling and sweet wines, 1–1½ hours for full-bodied whites, 45–60 minutes for light reds and 20–30 minutes for medium and full reds. These are rough guides: a crowded fridge, a door shelf or a thick sparkling bottle will slow things down.

How long to chill wine in the freezer?

About 10–15 minutes takes a medium or full red to a cool serving temperature and around 20 minutes a light red; whites and sparkling wine need 30–40 minutes or so to be properly cold. Always set a timer, because a forgotten bottle can freeze after a few hours, push its cork out or crack, especially sparkling wine.

Does wine need to be refrigerated after opening?

Yes, for both red and white. Once a bottle is open, oxygen starts to flatten its flavours, and cold slows that process right down. Reseal it with the cork or a stopper and keep it upright in the fridge. Most still wines then last several days and sparkling wine one to three. Let reds warm up for 30 to 60 minutes before you drink them.
