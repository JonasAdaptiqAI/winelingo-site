How Long Does Open Wine Last?

How long wine lasts after opening — realistic timelines for sparkling, white, red and fortified wine, plus how to keep an open bottle tasting fresh.

Half a bottle left, cork balanced on top, and the quiet question: is this still going to be nice tomorrow? The honest answer is that open wine doesn’t suddenly go off — it fades, style by style, on a fairly predictable clock.

Here are the realistic timelines for every kind of wine, why the clock runs at different speeds, and the small habits that genuinely buy you extra days. None of it requires gadgets, and the single most effective trick is one you already own: the fridge.

The quick answer, style by style

These are practical, real-kitchen timelines for a resealed bottle kept in the fridge. Individual bottles vary, so treat them as a window, not a deadline.

WineKeeps well forNotes
Sparkling1–3 daysUse a proper sparkling stopper; the clamp kind holds fizz best
Light white & rosé5–7 daysThe everyday survivors — crisp acidity slows the fade
Full-bodied white3–5 daysRich, oaked styles oxidise faster than crisp ones
Red3–5 daysMore tannin and acidity buy more days; light reds fade first
Fortified (Port, oloroso)Up to ~3 weeksThe extra alcohol shields them — but fino sherry is the exception: treat it like a delicate white

The pattern behind the numbers: acidity, tannin, sugar and alcohol all act as preservatives. The more of them a wine carries, the longer it holds together once air gets in.

Why open wine fades at all

The moment the cork comes out, oxygen starts working on the wine. At first that’s no bad thing — a splash of air is exactly why some young reds taste better an hour after opening. But past that sweet spot, oxidation flattens the fruit, dulls the aromas and eventually turns the wine tired and stale. Given long enough, bacteria that thrive on oxygen begin nudging the wine towards vinegar.

Two things set the pace: how much air is in the bottle, and how warm it is. A nearly full, tightly resealed bottle in the fridge fades slowly. A third of a bottle left open on a warm counter fades overnight. Every trick below works by starving the wine of one of those two things — air or warmth.

How to make an open bottle last longer

  • Reseal it straight away. Cork back in (either end works) or a cheap bottle stopper. The goal is simply to stop fresh air circulating.
  • Refrigerate everything — reds included. Cold slows every reaction that ages an open wine. Just take the red out half an hour before pouring so it isn’t served fridge-cold; our serving temperature guide shows where each style tastes best.
  • Shrink the airspace. If you know you’ll only drink half tonight, pour the rest into a clean half-size bottle and seal it. Less air touching the wine is the closest thing to pressing pause.
  • Keep it upright. Standing the bottle up exposes the smallest possible wine surface to the air trapped inside.
  • For fizz, use a sparkling stopper. The hinged clamp stoppers genuinely keep pressure in for a day or two. The teaspoon-in-the-neck trick, sadly, is a myth — it does nothing.

Vacuum pumps and inert-gas sprays exist and do help at the margins, but the fridge plus a proper reseal gets you most of the benefit for free.

How to tell a wine is past it

Trust your senses — they are exactly the right instrument, and this is good tasting practice in miniature. A wine has gone over when:

  • It smells sharp and vinegary, or of nail-polish remover — the clearest sign the acetic slide has begun.
  • It smells flat and bruised — like a cut apple left out, or an oddly sherry-ish nuttiness in a wine that isn’t sherry. That’s oxidation.
  • The colour has dulled — whites deepening towards amber, reds going brick-brown at the rim faster than their age explains.
  • The fruit has simply vanished — it tastes thin, stale and hollow. Nothing smells wrong; nothing tastes of much either.

And the question everyone quietly wonders: drinking a wine that’s past it won’t hurt you — a faded or even vinegary wine is unpleasant, not unsafe. If it tastes dull but clean, you can happily finish it; you now know precisely what oxidation tastes like, which is a genuinely useful palate lesson.

The special cases: fizz, fortified and bag-in-box

Sparkling wine loses its sparkle before it loses its flavour. With a clamp stopper in the fridge, a good Champagne often still shows gentle fizz on day two — softer, but lovely. Once truly flat, it’s a still white wine on borrowed time.

Fortified wines are the marathon runners. An open Port or oloroso sherry holds for a couple of weeks or more in the fridge, because the higher alcohol (and often sugar) protects it — though richer styles do slowly lose their top notes. The exception is fino and manzanilla sherry: bottled fresh and delicate, they behave like a crisp white, so drink them within days.

Bag-in-box wine quietly wins this whole contest: the bag collapses as you pour, so no air ever reaches the remaining wine, and an opened box stays fresh for weeks in the fridge.

Too faded to drink, too good to pour away

A wine that has lost its charm in the glass still has a second life in the pan. Anything short of fully vinegary is fine for cooking — deglazing, stews, risotto, pan sauces — because the flavours that survive are exactly the ones cooking concentrates. Freeze leftover wine in ice-cube trays and you’ll always have a splash ready for the pot.

And if a bottle keeps outliving your evenings, that’s useful information about your drinking rhythm, not a failing: half bottles, bag-in-box and wines by the glass exist for exactly this. The goal is never to finish a bottle — it’s to enjoy what you pour. Notice how a wine changes on day two and three; tracking those differences is one of the fastest ways to learn your own taste profile.

FAQ

Questions & answers

Can you drink wine that has been open for a week?

It’s safe, but whether it’s pleasant depends on the wine. A crisp white kept sealed in the fridge can still be decent at a week; most reds and rich whites will taste flat by then. If it smells clean rather than vinegary, taste it and decide.

Does open wine need to go in the fridge, even red?

Yes — cold slows the oxidation that makes open wine fade, for reds just as much as whites. Reseal the bottle, refrigerate it, and let the red warm up for about half an hour before you pour the next glass.

Is it safe to drink oxidised wine?

Yes. Oxidised or even vinegary wine is unpleasant rather than harmful — wine that’s past its best won’t make you ill. If the flavour has faded but it still smells clean, it’s fine to drink or cook with.

Does putting a spoon in a Champagne bottle keep it fizzy?

No — the teaspoon trick is a well-tested myth and does nothing to preserve the bubbles. A hinged sparkling-wine stopper and the fridge will genuinely keep fizz for another day or two.
