How to Serve Champagne: Chilling, Opening, Pouring and Keeping It

How cold Champagne should be, how to open it quietly and safely, which glass to use, and how long an open or unopened bottle keeps.

By Jonas Egeskov · Updated 14 October 2026

Yes, you should refrigerate Champagne before you serve it: a few hours in the fridge, or 20 to 30 minutes in a bucket of ice and water, brings it to the ideal 6–10°C. For long-term storage, though, skip the fridge and keep unopened bottles somewhere cool, dark and steady.

The rest is easy once you know the steps. Below: how cold to serve it, how to open a bottle with a gentle sigh rather than a pop, what to do with a stuck cork, how to pour, which glass to use, and how long Champagne keeps before and after opening. It all works for other sparkling wines too.

Do you refrigerate Champagne?

Before serving, yes. Champagne, the sparkling wine made only in the Champagne region of France, tastes best properly chilled. Cold keeps the bubbles fine, tames the foam on opening and keeps the wine tasting fresh. Warm Champagne gushes, because cold liquid holds on to carbon dioxide far better than warm.

  • Fridge: three to four hours. The easiest route.
  • Ice bucket: ice and water, bottle sunk to the neck. Water touches the whole bottle, so it chills in roughly 20 to 30 minutes.
  • Freezer: about 15 minutes in an emergency. Set a timer, because a forgotten bottle can freeze and crack.

What you should not do is keep Champagne in the fridge for months. A home fridge is dry, a little too cold and opened all day. A few days or a couple of weeks is fine; for longer, store bottles in the dark at a steady temperature, ideally around 12–13°C (see wine storage temperature). Keep them out of light too, as it can give sparkling wine a dull, cabbage-like smell.

Is Champagne supposed to be chilled, and how cold?

Yes. The sweet spot for sparkling wine is 6–10°C, the range our wine serving temperature tool uses.

BottleServe atWhy
Non-vintage Brut and everyday fizz6–8°CCrisp and refreshing
Vintage and prestige Champagne8–10°CLets the richer, toasty aromas show
Sweeter styles such as Demi-sec6–8°CKeeps the sweetness lively

Non-vintage is a blend of several years; vintage comes from one stated year. Brut, the most common style, has under 12 grams of sugar per litre and tastes dry or nearly dry; Demi-sec has 32–50 g/L and tastes clearly sweet.

A fridge runs at about 4–5°C, so a bottle straight from it is slightly too cold, but it warms a degree or two in the glass within minutes. The same advice applies to Prosecco, Cava and Crémant; for how they differ, see Prosecco vs Champagne.

How to open a bottle of Champagne safely

A Champagne bottle holds roughly 5 to 6 bar of pressure, about two to three times that of a car tyre. A cork released carelessly can hit hard enough to injure an eye, so the aim is control: a soft sigh, not a loud pop.

  1. Chill the bottle well and dry it if it came from an ice bucket.
  2. Point it away from people and anything breakable, and keep it that way throughout.
  3. Remove the foil using its pull tab.
  4. Put your thumb on the cork and keep it there until the cork is out.
  5. Loosen the wire cage (the little wire basket over the cork) by untwisting its loop, usually six half-turns. Leave the cage on: it gives extra grip.
  6. Drape a tea towel over the cork for grip, if you like.
  7. Hold the bottle at about 45 degrees. A gush is less likely this way.
  8. Grip the cork and twist the bottle, not the cork, turning the base slowly with your other hand.
  9. Hold the cork back as it rises, so the gas escapes with a gentle hiss. Keep the bottle tilted a few seconds, then pour.

The one rule to remember: thumb on the cork, twist the bottle, never point it at anyone.

How to open a Champagne bottle when the cork is stuck

Older bottles especially can have corks that grip the glass tightly. Try these in order:

  • Better grip. Wrap the cork in a tea towel or use a rubber jar opener. Most stuck corks are a grip problem.
  • Rock gently. While turning the bottle, nudge the cork from side to side to break the seal.
  • A Champagne key or cork grip. These cheap tools clamp the cork and give leverage.

Never use a corkscrew, because the pressure can shoot the cork out with the screw still in it. And do not warm the neck under hot water: it raises the pressure and makes the cork harder to control.

Sabrage, opening a bottle by striking the neck with a sabre, is a ceremony best left to trained hands. It sends glass flying and is not something to try at home.

How to pour Champagne and how to hold the glass

Pouring straight into an upright glass sends up a tower of foam. Use a two-stage pour instead: tilt the glass and pour a small splash down the side, wait a few seconds for the foam (the mousse) to settle, then top up slowly to about two-thirds full. Hold the glass by the stem, not the bowl, so your hand does not warm the wine.

Flute, tulip or white wine glass?

  • Flute: tall and narrow, so the bubbles last and look lovely, but the small opening holds back the aroma.
  • Tulip: wider in the middle, narrower at the top. A good compromise between fizz and smell.
  • White wine glass: many Champagne producers now prefer it for good bottles, as the bigger bowl shows off the aromas.
  • Coupe: the shallow saucer looks glamorous, but bubbles and aroma escape quickly.

At a standard 125 ml pour, a 750 ml bottle fills six glasses; see how many glasses of wine in a bottle for party maths. Champagne, like all wine, is for adults of legal drinking age and best enjoyed in moderation.

How to save and close an opened bottle of Champagne

The original cork will not go back in, because it swells into a mushroom shape. Use a Champagne stopper, a cheap clamp-style cap that grips the lip of the bottle and holds the pressure in. Stopper on and bottle in the fridge, Champagne usually stays pleasantly fizzy for one to three days. Without one, the bubbles fade within hours.

And the silver spoon dangling in the neck? It is a popular myth that tests have not supported: the spoon has not kept wine noticeably fizzier than an open bottle simply left in the fridge. The cold is what helps, because chilled wine holds on to its carbon dioxide better, so the spoon gets the credit for the fridge's work. More of these on our wine myths page, and for still wines, see how long open wine lasts.

Does Champagne go bad? How long it keeps

Unopened Champagne has no expiry date, but it does fade. Most non-vintage Champagne is made to drink young and is best within a few years of purchase. Vintage and prestige Champagne is built to age and can keep developing for a decade or more. Some producers print a disgorgement date on the back label, the date the yeast sediment was removed and the bottle corked, which tells you how long it has rested since.

How Champagne keepsRoughly how longWhere
Unopened, non-vintageBest within about 3 years of purchaseCool, dark, steady place
Unopened, vintage or prestigeOften 5–10 years or moreCool, dark, ideally 12–13°C
Opened, with a Champagne stopper1–3 daysFridge
Opened, no stopperA few hours to a dayFridge

Tired Champagne shows it: few bubbles, a deep gold or brownish colour, and a flat, sherry-like smell. That differs from a fault such as cork taint, which smells of damp cardboard; see wine faults.

Rule of thumb: buy non-vintage to drink, not to keep.

What to do with leftover Champagne

Flat Champagne is still good for cooking, since its bright acidity and flavour remain.

  • Risotto: use it in place of white wine in a mushroom risotto.
  • Sauces: reduce it with shallots and finish with butter for fish or scallops.
  • Mussels: steam mussels in it with garlic and parsley.
  • Mimosas: if some fizz remains the next day, top it up with orange juice.

FAQ

Questions & answers

Do you put Champagne in the fridge?

Yes, before serving. Three to four hours in the fridge brings Champagne close to its ideal serving temperature of 6–10°C. You can also use an ice bucket filled with ice and water, which takes around 20 to 30 minutes. For long-term storage over months, a cool, dark place with a steady temperature is better than the fridge, which is dry and opened often. Move the bottle into the fridge the day you plan to open it.

How long is Champagne good for once opened?

With a proper Champagne stopper and kept in the fridge, an opened bottle usually stays pleasantly fizzy for one to three days. Without a stopper, the bubbles fade within hours, though the wine may still have a little life the next day. Once it goes flat, it is still fine for cooking, for example in risotto, sauces or steamed mussels, because the flavour and acidity remain.

Does unopened Champagne go bad?

It does not expire, but it fades. Most non-vintage Champagne is best within about three years of purchase, while vintage and prestige Champagne can age well for a decade or more if stored cool, dark and steady. A bottle past its best usually has few bubbles, a deeper, browner colour and a flat, sherry-like smell. It will taste tired rather than fresh, so drink everyday Champagne fairly soon after buying it.

How do you open Champagne without it popping?

Chill the bottle well, point it away from people, remove the foil and keep your thumb on the cork. Loosen the wire cage with about six half-turns but leave it on. Hold the bottle at around 45 degrees, grip the cork firmly and slowly turn the bottle rather than the cork. As the cork rises, hold it back so the gas escapes gently. The result should be a soft sigh rather than a loud pop.

Does a spoon in the bottle keep Champagne fizzy?

No. The silver spoon in the neck is a popular myth that tests have not supported. A bottle with a spoon in it loses its fizz at about the same rate as an open bottle left in the fridge. The cold is what really helps, because chilled wine holds on to its carbon dioxide better. For real results, use a clamp-style Champagne stopper and keep the bottle in the fridge.

How do you hold a Champagne glass?

Hold it by the stem, between your thumb and first two fingers. Holding the bowl warms the wine with your hand and leaves fingerprints that hide the bubbles. When pouring, tilt the glass slightly and pour a small splash first, let the foam settle, then fill to about two-thirds. A tulip-shaped glass or a white wine glass shows off the aromas better than a narrow flute.
