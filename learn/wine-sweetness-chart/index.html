The Wine Sweetness Chart: Every Style from Dry to Sweet

A plain-English wine sweetness chart — what dry really means, how residual sugar works, and where the classic styles sit from bone-dry to lusciously sweet.

“Is this wine sweet or dry?” is the single most useful question in wine — and the one the label answers worst. Words like dry, brut, sec and demi-sec all describe sweetness, several of them don’t mean what they look like they mean, and a wine that smells of ripe peaches can still contain almost no sugar at all.

This page puts the whole spectrum on one plain-English scale. First the chart, then what the terms actually mean, and further down the labelling numbers and the famous traps — like why “extra dry” sparkling wine is sweeter than brut.

The chart: wine styles from driest to sweetest

Sweetness in wine runs on a spectrum, but five plain bands cover it. Wines can vary by producer, so read the placements below as where each style usually sits.

BandWhat it feels likeTypical styles
Bone-dryNo sweetness at all; often stony or briskMuscadet, Chablis, fino sherry, brut nature sparkling
DryNo obvious sugar; fruit without sweetnessMost Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Grigio and rosé, and nearly all everyday reds
Off-dryA gentle kiss of sugar, often barely noticedMany Gewürztraminer and Vouvray, fruity German Riesling, “extra dry” Prosecco
Medium-sweetClearly sweet, but still refreshing with foodDemi-sec sparkling, sweeter Riesling styles, many supermarket “smooth” whites
SweetHoneyed, luscious, a dessert in the glassSauternes, Tokaji Aszú, ice wine, Moscato d’Asti, Port, PX sherry

Two things surprise most people. Almost every red wine on a shop shelf is technically dry, even the ones that taste jammy and ripe. And the sweet band contains some of the most celebrated (and age-worthy) wines on earth — sweetness done well is a feature, not a flaw.

What “dry” actually means

Grape juice is naturally sweet. During fermentation, yeast eats that sugar and turns it into alcohol. Let the yeast finish its meal and virtually no sugar remains: the wine is dry. Stop the fermentation early — or start with grapes so sugar-rich the yeast can’t finish — and some sugar stays behind. That leftover sugar is called residual sugar, and it is the entire story of sweetness in wine.

So “dry” simply means the sugar is gone. It says nothing about how fruity, ripe or intense a wine tastes — which is where the confusion starts, and why the next section matters more than any chart.

Fruity is not sweet (the mix-up behind most wine confusion)

Smell is not taste. A dry Riesling can smell of peaches and lime; a dry Gewürztraminer positively radiates lychee and rose. Your nose reads ripe fruit and whispers “sweet”, but on the tongue there is no sugar to find — the wine finishes clean and dry.

The reverse happens too: a very sweet wine with racy acidity, like a great German Riesling or Tokaji, can taste far less sugary than it is, because acidity balances sweetness the way lemon juice balances sugar in lemonade. This is why some of the world’s sweetest wines feel refreshing rather than cloying — and why two wines with identical sugar levels can feel completely different.

When you want to know if a wine is genuinely sweet, ask your tongue, not your nose: sweetness registers on the tip of your tongue in the first second or two of a sip.

The numbers: residual sugar and what labels are allowed to say

Residual sugar is measured in grams per litre (g/L). Under EU labelling rules, the familiar words map to bands roughly like this:

  • Dry — up to 4 g/L (or up to 9 g/L where high acidity balances it)
  • Medium-dry — up to 12 g/L (up to 18 g/L with the same acidity allowance)
  • Medium-sweet — up to 45 g/L
  • Sweet — more than 45 g/L

For scale: a famous dessert wine like Sauternes typically carries well over 100 g/L, while a crisp dry white might hold less than 2 g/L. Producers aren’t required to print any of this, which is why the style words — and a little knowledge of which styles run sweet — do most of the practical work.

Sparkling wine: the scale where “extra dry” means sweeter

Sparkling wine uses its own historical sweetness ladder, and it contains the most famous trap in wine labelling: “extra dry” is sweeter than “brut”. From driest to sweetest, the scale runs:

  1. Brut nature / zéro dosage — bone-dry, no sugar added
  2. Extra brut — very dry
  3. Brut — dry; the standard style for most Champagne
  4. Extra dry / extra sec — noticeably rounder and softer than brut
  5. Sec (“dry”) — gently sweet, despite the name
  6. Demi-sec — properly sweet; lovely with dessert
  7. Doux — the sweetest of all, and rare today

The ladder made sense a century ago, when sparkling wine was drunk far sweeter than it is now and the terms described how far a wine had moved from that norm. The terms stuck; the tastes moved. If you want the driest glass on the shelf, look for brut nature or extra brut — and remember most Prosecco is made in the softer brut or “extra dry” styles.

Reading sweetness on real labels

A few practical flags help you predict sweetness before you pull the cork:

  • Alcohol level is a clue. Sugar becomes alcohol, so a white at 8–9% ABV very likely kept some sweetness back, while 13% suggests the yeast finished the job. It’s a rule of thumb, not a law — fortified wines break it by design.
  • German labels have their own ladder — but one word cuts through it: trocken means dry, whatever else the label says.
  • Style names carry the information. Sauternes, Tokaji Aszú, ice wine, Moscato d’Asti and Port are reliably sweet; Chablis, Muscadet and Sancerre are reliably dry. Learning a handful of style names beats decoding any chart.
  • When in doubt, ask or look it up — sweetness is exactly the kind of thing wine shops love being asked about.

Finding your own place on the scale

There is no correct amount of sweetness — only what you enjoy. Plenty of experienced wine lovers drink bone-dry everything; plenty adore a chilled Moscato d’Asti on a summer afternoon. If dry wines feel sharp to you right now, start off-dry and drift drier as your palate adjusts (it genuinely does). If you already love bracing, bone-dry whites, the sweet classics are still worth one evening of your life — a glass of Sauternes with blue cheese is one of wine’s great experiences.

Sweetness is also just one of the dials that make up a wine’s character — body, acidity, tannin and oak are the others, and our guide to understanding a wine’s taste profile puts all five together in the same plain language.

FAQ

Questions & answers

Is Riesling sweet or dry?

Both — Riesling is made across the entire spectrum, from bone-dry to intensely sweet dessert styles. Look for the word “trocken” (dry) on German labels, check the alcohol level (lower often means sweeter), or ask. Alsace and Australian Rieslings are usually dry.

What is the driest type of wine?

Bone-dry styles with virtually no residual sugar include Muscadet, Chablis, fino sherry and brut nature sparkling wine. Almost all everyday red wine is also fully dry, even when ripe fruit flavours make it seem otherwise.

Does dry wine mean no sugar at all?

Very nearly. In a dry wine the yeast has fermented almost all the grape sugar into alcohol, typically leaving only a few grams per litre — too little for most palates to notice as sweetness.

Why does extra dry Prosecco taste sweet?

Because on the sparkling-wine scale, “extra dry” is actually a step sweeter than brut. The historical ladder runs brut nature, extra brut, brut, extra dry, sec, demi-sec, doux — from driest to sweetest. For a genuinely dry glass, choose brut or drier.
