Understanding a Wine’s Taste Profile: Body, Tannin, Acidity, Sweetness, Alcohol & Oak

Six simple building blocks that explain why any wine tastes the way it does — and how to feel each one in your own glass.

Every wine you taste is really a blend of a few simple things working together. Once you can name them, wine stops feeling like a secret code and starts making sense — glass by glass.

A wine’s taste profile is just the balance of six building blocks: body, tannin, acidity, sweetness, alcohol and oak. None of them is good or bad on its own. It’s how they play together that gives a wine its character. There are no wrong answers here — only what you notice and enjoy.

Below we walk through each one in plain words: what it is, what causes it, how it actually feels in your mouth, and easy examples of high and low. By the end you’ll have a simple vocabulary you can use with any bottle.

Body: how heavy the wine feels

Body is the overall weight and fullness of a wine in your mouth. The easiest way to picture it is milk: skimmed milk feels light and watery, whole milk feels rich and coating. Wine works the same way, from light and delicate to full and mouth-filling.

What causes it? Mostly alcohol, plus the concentration of sugars, tannins and other dissolved ‘stuff’ in the wine. Warmer growing regions and riper grapes tend to make fuller-bodied wines; cooler regions often make lighter ones. Body isn’t about flavour strength — it’s about weight and texture.

How it feels: a light-bodied wine feels crisp and easy, almost like water with flavour. A full-bodied wine feels dense and lingers, coating your tongue.

  • Light-bodied examples: Pinot Grigio, Muscadet, many Pinot Noirs
  • Full-bodied examples: Chardonnay (especially oaked), Cabernet Sauvignon, Shiraz

Tannin: the grippy, drying feeling

Tannin is that dry, grippy sensation that makes your mouth pucker slightly — the same feeling you get from strong black tea or biting into a grape skin. It’s a texture more than a flavour, and it mostly lives in red wines.

What causes it? Tannins are natural compounds found in grape skins, seeds and stems, and they can also come from ageing the wine in oak barrels. Because red wines are made in contact with their skins, they carry far more tannin than whites. More skin contact and thicker-skinned grapes mean more grip.

How it feels: run your tongue over your teeth after a sip. If it feels dry, rough or ‘furry’, that’s tannin. Soft tannins feel gentle and velvety; firm tannins feel bold and structured, and they soften as a wine ages.

  • High-tannin examples: Cabernet Sauvignon, Nebbiolo, young Syrah
  • Low-tannin examples: Pinot Noir, Gamay (Beaujolais), most white wines

Acidity: the fresh, mouth-watering zing

Acidity is the crisp, tart, refreshing quality in wine — the thing that makes your mouth water, just like a squeeze of lemon or a green apple does. It’s the backbone of freshness, and it lives in every wine, white and red.

What causes it? Grapes naturally contain acids, and cooler climates preserve more of them because the grapes ripen more slowly. Warmer climates give riper, softer, lower-acid wines. Acidity is also what makes a wine feel ‘food-friendly’ — it cuts through rich, fatty dishes.

How it feels: notice how much your mouth waters a few seconds after you swallow. Lots of watering means high acidity, which reads as zippy and fresh. Low acidity feels rounder, softer, sometimes a little flat or ‘flabby’.

  • High-acidity examples: Sauvignon Blanc, Riesling, Champagne and other sparkling wines
  • Lower-acidity examples: warm-climate reds like Grenache, oaked Chardonnay from hot regions

Sweetness: from bone-dry to dessert-sweet

Sweetness is simply how much sugar you can taste. Wines range from bone-dry (no perceptible sugar) through off-dry (a touch of sweetness) to lusciously sweet dessert wines. A common surprise: many wines that smell fruity are actually completely dry.

What causes it? During fermentation, yeast turns grape sugar into alcohol. If all the sugar is converted, the wine is dry. If the winemaker stops fermentation early, some natural grape sugar remains, and the wine tastes sweet. It’s a deliberate style choice, not a sign of quality.

How it feels: sweetness is sensed on the tip of your tongue and gives a rounder, richer impression. Don’t confuse it with fruitiness — a wine can smell of ripe berries yet finish bone-dry.

  • Dry examples: most Cabernet Sauvignon, Pinot Grigio, Brut Champagne
  • Off-dry to sweet examples: many Rieslings, Moscato, Port and Sauternes (dessert wines)

Alcohol: warmth and weight

Alcohol does more than give wine its kick — it adds body, texture and a gentle sense of warmth. Most table wines land somewhere between roughly 11% and 15% ABV (alcohol by volume), with fortified wines like Port climbing higher.

What causes it? Riper grapes have more sugar, and more sugar means more alcohol after fermentation. That’s why warm, sunny regions tend to produce higher-alcohol wines, while cool regions make lighter, lower-alcohol styles.

How it feels: take a small sip and notice any warming sensation at the back of your throat, a bit like the glow of a warm room. More warmth usually means higher alcohol. High alcohol also makes a wine feel fuller and weightier. As always, savour slowly — a little goes a long way.

  • Lower-alcohol examples: German Riesling, Moscato d’Asti, many sparkling wines
  • Higher-alcohol examples: Zinfandel, Shiraz, Châteauneuf-du-Pape

Oak: vanilla, spice and smooth texture

Oak isn’t a grape thing — it’s a winemaking choice. When wine is aged in oak barrels, it can pick up flavours like vanilla, toast, baking spice, coconut, smoke or a subtle sweetness, along with a rounder, smoother texture. Not every wine sees oak, and that’s perfectly fine.

What causes it? Time spent in oak barrels, especially newer ones, which give the strongest flavour. Winemakers use oak to add complexity and soften a wine. Wines aged in stainless steel or concrete instead stay fresher and more ‘fruit-forward’, letting the grape shine through.

How it feels: look for notes of vanilla, toast, caramel or a creamy, silky texture. If a red tastes smoky and spicy, or a white tastes buttery, oak is often the reason.

  • Oaked examples: many Chardonnays, Rioja, oak-aged Cabernet and Merlot
  • Unoaked examples: Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Grigio, stainless-steel Chardonnay

Putting the profile together

No single building block tells the whole story. A great wine is about balance — the way body, tannin, acidity, sweetness, alcohol and oak support one another so that no single element sticks out awkwardly. A high-tannin red can feel wonderful when there’s enough fruit and body to carry it; bright acidity can make a touch of sweetness taste refreshing rather than cloying.

The best way to learn is simply to taste with these six words in mind. Next time you pour a glass, ask yourself: is it light or full? Grippy or smooth? Zingy or soft? Dry or sweet? Warming? Oaky or fresh? You’ll be reading a taste profile like a pro before you know it.

FAQ

Questions & answers

What is a full-bodied wine?

A full-bodied wine is one that feels heavy, rich and mouth-filling — think of the difference between whole milk and skimmed milk. Fuller body usually comes from higher alcohol and riper, more concentrated grapes. Cabernet Sauvignon, Shiraz and oaked Chardonnay are classic full-bodied examples.

What is tannin in wine?

Tannin is the dry, grippy, slightly puckering feeling you get from red wine — the same sensation as strong black tea. It comes from grape skins, seeds and stems, and sometimes from oak barrels. It’s a texture rather than a flavour, and firm tannins soften as a wine ages.

How can I tell if a wine has high acidity?

Take a sip, swallow, and notice how much your mouth waters a few seconds later. Lots of watering means high acidity, which tastes fresh, crisp and zippy — like a squeeze of lemon. Cool-climate wines such as Sauvignon Blanc, Riesling and Champagne tend to be high in acidity.

Does a fruity wine mean a sweet wine?

Not usually. Fruitiness is an aroma or flavour — notes of berries, citrus or stone fruit — while sweetness is actual sugar you taste on the tip of your tongue. Many wines smell intensely fruity yet finish completely dry, which surprises a lot of people. They’re two separate things.

What does oak do to wine?

Ageing wine in oak barrels can add flavours like vanilla, toast, spice, smoke or a creamy, buttery character, plus a smoother, rounder texture. Newer barrels give stronger flavour. Wines aged in stainless steel skip this and taste fresher and more fruit-forward instead.
