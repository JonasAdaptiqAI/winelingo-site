How to Practise Wine Tasting at Home

Simple wine tasting practice you can do at home — side-by-side comparisons, a three-question note habit and nose training that build your palate fast.

Reading about wine is like reading about swimming: pleasant, occasionally useful, and no substitute for getting in the water. Your palate is not a talent you’re born with — it’s a skill, and it improves embarrassingly fast with a little deliberate practice.

The good news is that practising wine tasting at home needs no kit, no courses and no expensive bottles. It needs two glasses, a bit of attention, and the handful of exercises on this page. Most people notice a real difference within a dozen bottles — not because their tongue changed, but because they finally started paying attention to what it was already telling them.

The golden rule: taste in pairs

The single most effective exercise in wine is also the simplest: taste two contrasting wines side by side. One wine on its own gives your brain nothing to push against; two wines turn vague impressions into obvious differences. Body, acidity, tannin and sweetness stop being abstract words the moment you can feel them change between two glasses.

Classic first pairings, in rough order of how loudly the contrast speaks:

  • Crisp vs creamy white: a Sauvignon Blanc against an oaked Chardonnay. You will never forget what “oak” means again.
  • Light vs bold red: a Pinot Noir against a Shiraz or Malbec. This is body and tannin, live on stage.
  • Dry vs sweet: any dry white against a Moscato. Calibrates your sweetness scale in one sip (our sweetness chart maps the whole spectrum).
  • Same grape, two places: a Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc against a Sancerre — same grape, different country, and suddenly “terroir” isn’t a pretentious word, it’s a taste.

You don’t need to open two full bottles for this: halves, small formats, or simply inviting a friend and sharing both bottles across the evening all work. So does re-tasting yesterday’s open bottle against a fresh one — that’s a lesson in oxidation thrown in for free.

The three-question note that beats a tasting course

Every wine you drink is practice — but only if you collect the rep. After each new wine, answer three questions:

  1. Did I like it? (Gut answer, no justification needed.)
  2. Was it light or heavy? (You’ve just assessed body.)
  3. What did it remind me of? (Lemon? Strawberry jam? Strong tea? A campfire? All valid.)

Grape, one line, done. That’s a real tasting note — the rest is decoration. Keep them anywhere: a notes app, a photo of the label with a caption, or the journal in Winelingo, which files the label scan and your reaction together so patterns surface on their own.

The pattern is the point. A dozen notes in, you’ll see it: everything you loved was light and zippy, or everything was full and smooth. That pattern is your palate — and from then on, wine lists and shop shelves read like a map instead of a lottery.

Train your nose without opening a bottle

Most of what we call taste is actually smell, which means you can practise wine without wine. The aromas people find in wine are the aromas of ordinary life, and your kitchen is a free training library. When you cook or shop this week, pause for two seconds and consciously smell:

  • citrus peel, green apple, ripe peach, blackcurrants, strawberries
  • fresh-cut grass, bell pepper, mint, black pepper, vanilla, cinnamon
  • butter, toast, honey, coffee, dark chocolate, wet stones after rain

This feels faintly ridiculous and works remarkably well. You’re building a reference library, so that when a Sauvignon Blanc throws gooseberry and cut grass at you, your brain has somewhere to file it. Smell is also the fastest sense to fatigue — so in practice sessions, sniff in short visits rather than long inhales, and rest your nose between wines.

A four-week starter plan

If you like a bit of structure, here is a month of practice that costs four to eight modest bottles in total — shared, ideally, because wine practice is better with company.

  • Week 1 — whites, loud contrast: crisp unoaked white vs rich oaked white. Note which side of the line you live on.
  • Week 2 — reds, loud contrast: light silky red vs big grippy red. Meet tannin properly.
  • Week 3 — one grape, two homes: same variety from two countries. This is the week wine gets interesting.
  • Week 4 — revisit and retest: re-buy something you disliked a while ago. Palates genuinely shift, and catching your own taste changing is oddly delightful.

Repeat with new pairs for as long as it stays fun. There is no syllabus and no exam — the only goal is that your answer to “what do you like?” keeps getting more precise.

“Why can’t I taste what others taste?”

Almost everyone asks this, usually quietly. Three reassurances:

Nobody actually tastes fifteen things in a glass. A critic’s flamboyant tasting note is a trained memory reaching for associations, not a checklist the wine objectively contains. If you taste “red fruit” where they wrote “morello cherry and forest floor”, you are both right — you’re just earlier on the same road.

Your setup might be muting the wine. A wine served too cold whispers; a few degrees warmer and it speaks. (Our serving temperature chart is the quickest fix in wine.) An overfilled glass can’t be swirled, and swirling is what pumps aroma up to your nose.

Use the exhale trick. After you swallow (or spit), breathe out slowly through your nose. Flavour blooms on that exhale — it’s where much of tasting actually happens, and most beginners have simply never been told.

The kit you don’t need (and the little that helps)

No tasting flight boards, no aroma wheels, no crystal. Any wine glass that curves slightly inward will do its job. Two of the same glass helps a side-by-side comparison stay fair; pouring small measures keeps a practice session about attention rather than volume, and spitting or pouring away is completely legitimate — professionals do it all day.

Two small habits quietly upgrade every session: taste before you eat strong flavours (toothpaste, chilli and coffee all bulldoze your palate for a while), and keep a bottle’s leftovers properly — resealed and refrigerated — so tomorrow’s glass is still honest practice. Our guide on how long open wine lasts covers exactly that.

Finally, keep it kind to yourself and others: practice means small pours and full attention, never volume — wine tasting is for adults of legal drinking age, and the learning happens in the noticing, not the finishing.

FAQ

Questions & answers

How can I get better at tasting wine?

Taste two contrasting wines side by side and note the differences — body, acidity, tannin and sweetness become obvious with a comparison. Add a three-question note after each wine (Did I like it? Light or heavy? What did it remind me of?) and patterns appear within a dozen bottles.

Why can't I taste the flavours other people describe in wine?

Because those notes are trained associations, not objective contents of the glass. Tasting “red fruit” where a critic writes “morello cherry” means you’re on the same road, just earlier. Serving temperature, glass shape and exhaling through your nose after a sip all noticeably sharpen what you detect.

Do I need to spit when practising wine tasting?

Spitting is completely normal and what professionals do — it lets you stay sharp across several wines. At home, small pours with full attention teach far more than large glasses, and pouring leftovers away is a legitimate part of practice.

How many wines should I compare at once?

Two is the sweet spot for learning — a clear contrast with no confusion. Three or four is a fun evening with friends, but beyond that your nose fatigues and the wines start blurring together.
