Types of Wine: The Complete Plain-English Guide

Six families, one clear map — what each type of wine is, how it tastes, and where to start.

Wine looks like an impossibly long list until you notice it falls into a handful of families. Learn those, and the wall of bottles in a shop turns into a map you can actually read.

There are six main types of wine: red, white, rosé, sparkling, dessert and fortified. This guide explains what each one is, what it tastes like, and which specific styles and grapes belong to it — starting with the short answer and going deeper as you scroll.

The six main types of wine

Every bottle you meet belongs to one of these families. The first three are defined by colour, the last three by how they are made.

Types of red wine

Red wine gets its colour and its grip from grape skins. The most useful way to sort reds is not by grape but by body — how heavy the wine feels in your mouth — because that is what decides when you would drink it and what you would eat with it.

Light-bodied reds are pale, silky and low in tannin, with fresh red-berry fruit. They suit lighter food and can even take a slight chill. Try Pinot Noir or Gamay (the grape behind Beaujolais).

Medium-bodied reds sit in the middle — enough structure for a roast, enough freshness for a Tuesday. Merlot, Sangiovese and Tempranillo live here.

Full-bodied reds are deep, powerful and more tannic, built for rich food. Cabernet Sauvignon, Syrah/Shiraz and Malbec are the classics; Nebbiolo adds high acidity and firm tannin to the mix.

Almost every red on a shop shelf is technically dry — no leftover sugar — even when ripe fruit makes it taste sweet. That confusion is common enough that we gave it its own wine sweetness chart.

Types of white wine

White wines are usually fermented without grape skins, so they carry no tannin and lean on acidity for structure. Three groups cover almost everything you will meet.

Crisp, dry whites — zesty and refreshing, the easiest place to start. Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Grigio, Albariño, and unoaked Chablis.

Aromatic whites — intensely perfumed, sometimes with a touch of sweetness. Riesling, Gewürztraminer, Viognier and Moscato. Be careful here: these often smell sweet while tasting dry.

Full-bodied whites — rounder and richer, often from oak ageing or time on the yeast lees, which can add vanilla, toast and a creamy texture. Oaked Chardonnay is the benchmark; Chenin Blanc spans the whole range from bone-dry to lusciously sweet.

Rosé, and how it is actually made

The most common myth in wine is that rosé is red and white wine mixed together. With one notable exception — rosé Champagne, where blending is permitted — that is not how it works.

Rosé is made from dark grapes given only brief contact with their skins, usually a matter of hours rather than days. Pull the juice off early and you get colour without much tannin: pink wine with the freshness of a white. The longer the contact, the deeper the colour and the more structure.

Styles range from the very pale, delicate Provence rosé to the fuller, fruitier Spanish rosado and the notably robust Tavel. Colour is a clue to intensity, not to sweetness — most serious rosé is dry.

Sparkling wine: where the bubbles come from

Bubbles are carbon dioxide, trapped in the bottle from a second fermentation. How that second fermentation is done shapes the whole style, and it is the single most useful thing to understand about fizz.

  • Traditional method — the second fermentation happens inside the individual bottle, and the wine rests on its spent yeast. This builds fine, persistent bubbles and bready, biscuity flavours. Used for Champagne, Cava and Crémant.
  • Tank method — the second fermentation happens in a large pressurised tank before bottling, which preserves fresh, grapey, floral aromas. This is how most Prosecco is made.

Sparkling wine also has its own sweetness ladder, and it hides the most famous trap in wine labelling: “extra dry” is actually sweeter than “brut”. If you want the driest fizz on the shelf, look for brut nature or extra brut.

Dessert and fortified wines

These two families both taste rich, but they get there by completely different routes.

Dessert wines keep their sweetness because the grapes carried more sugar than the yeast could ferment. Growers concentrate that sugar in several ways: leaving grapes to dry (Vin Santo), letting noble rot shrivel them (Sauternes, Tokaji Aszú), or picking them frozen (ice wine). The best examples pair intense sweetness with high acidity, which stops them cloying.

Fortified wines have neutral grape spirit added. Add it partway through fermentation and the yeast is stopped in its tracks, leaving sweetness behind — that is Port. Add it after fermentation has finished and the wine stays dry, as with fino sherry and oloroso. Madeira takes the idea further by deliberately heating the wine as it ages, which makes it famously long-lived once opened.

How to choose a type of wine

Pick by weight, not by rules. The old “red with meat, white with fish” line is a rough proxy for something more useful: match the weight of the wine to the weight of the food. Delicate dishes suit light, fresh wines; rich dishes stand up to fuller ones.

If you are starting from scratch, work outward from what you already enjoy in other drinks. Like strong black coffee and dark chocolate? Full-bodied reds will feel like home. Prefer sparkling water with lemon? Start with crisp dry whites. Our beginner's guide walks that logic through properly, and the glossary defines any word here you would like pinned down.

The fastest way to learn the types, though, is to taste two side by side — a crisp white against an oaked one, or a light red against a bold one. The contrast teaches in one evening what reading teaches in a month, which is exactly what our guide to practising wine tasting at home is built around.

FAQ

Questions & answers

What are the main types of wine?

There are six main types: red, white and rosé, which are defined by colour and skin contact, plus sparkling, dessert and fortified, which are defined by how they are made. Orange wine — white grapes fermented on their skins — is often counted as a seventh.

What are the different types of red wine?

Reds are most usefully grouped by body. Light-bodied reds like Pinot Noir and Gamay are silky and low in tannin; medium-bodied reds include Merlot, Sangiovese and Tempranillo; full-bodied reds like Cabernet Sauvignon, Syrah and Malbec are deeper and more tannic.

What are the different types of white wine?

White wines fall into three broad groups: crisp and dry, such as Sauvignon Blanc and Pinot Grigio; aromatic and perfumed, such as Riesling and Gewürztraminer; and full-bodied, such as oaked Chardonnay, which is richer and often has vanilla or toast from the barrel.

Is rosé wine a mix of red and white wine?

Almost never. Rosé is made from dark grapes given only brief contact with their skins, which gives colour without much tannin. Blending red and white is permitted for rosé Champagne, but it is the exception rather than the rule.
