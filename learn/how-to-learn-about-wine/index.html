How to Learn About Wine: A Step-by-Step Plan

Taste with attention, then learn a little theory in the right order: styles, a dozen grapes, the places they grow, labels and food. Here is the plan, at home or online.

By Jonas Egeskov · Updated 11 October 2026

Most people who want to learn about wine start in the wrong place: a thick book, a map of Bordeaux, a list of grapes to memorise. It rarely sticks, because nothing connects it to what is in the glass. The fastest way to learn is the opposite: start with your own palate, and add one layer of theory at a time, in an order where each step explains the next.

1. Start with the glass, not the textbook

Every glass you drink is a free lesson if you ask it three questions: is it light or full, is it fresh or soft, and does it dry your mouth (that drying feel is tannin, in red wines). Say the answers out loud or write a line in your phone. After a dozen bottles you will start to notice patterns, and the theory you read later will have something to stick to.

The single most useful habit is to taste in pairs: two wines side by side make differences obvious that you would never notice one at a time. Our guide to practising wine tasting at home has simple exercises.

2. Learn in this order

Wine knowledge is layered. Each step below makes the next one easier, so the order matters more than the speed.

  1. How to taste. Body, acidity, tannin, sweetness and the main families of aromas. This is the vocabulary for everything else. (Wine tasting course, one of the two free units.)
  2. The main styles. Light and full reds, crisp and rich whites, rosé, sparkling and sweet wines. See types of wine.
  3. A dozen grapes. Around ten red and ten white grapes cover a large share of what you meet on a shop shelf or a wine list. Learn what each tastes like, and taste them. (Red grapes · white grapes · grape guides.)
  4. The places those grapes grow. Now regions make sense: Burgundy is Pinot Noir and Chardonnay, Rioja is mostly Tempranillo, Marlborough is Sauvignon Blanc. Go country by country. (France, Italy, Spain and nine more.)
  5. How to read a label. Old World labels name the place, New World labels usually name the grape; once you know steps 3 and 4, most labels decode themselves. (How to read a wine label.)
  6. Food and wine. A handful of principles, such as matching weight with weight, beat any pairing chart. (Food and wine pairing.)
  7. Going deeper. How wine is made, the vineyard, terroir and vintages, ageing and value. This is where the hobby opens up. (How wine is made.)

How to learn about wine at home

You do not need a course, a cellar or special glasses to start. A few habits do most of the work:

  • Compare two wines instead of opening one. Same grape from two countries, or two grapes from one region.
  • Keep one-line notes with the three questions above, plus whether you would buy it again.
  • Share bottles with friends and taste blind now and then. It is more fun and it costs less per wine.
  • Ask in a good wine shop. Tell them what you liked and why; you will learn from their answer.
  • Little and often. Five minutes of theory a day beats an afternoon a month.

Learning about wine online

Online learning works well for wine theory, because the theory is the part you can do without a glass, any time. What separates a good online wine course from a pile of articles:

  • Structure. A path that follows the order above, so you never wonder what to learn next.
  • Short lessons. Five to ten minutes, so it fits into a normal day and you keep going.
  • Testing yourself. Questions after each idea are what move it into long-term memory.
  • A link to real bottles. The best courses send you back to the glass and the label in front of you.

Do you need a wine certificate?

For enjoying and choosing wine, no. A certificate makes sense if you work in restaurants, bars or the wine trade, or if you simply want an exam to aim for. The best-known route for beginners is the Wine & Spirit Education Trust (WSET): its Level 1 Award in Wines is a short introduction that ends with a multiple-choice exam, and Levels 2 and 3 go much deeper into grapes and regions. Restaurant professionals also take the sommelier route through the Court of Master Sommeliers. A self-paced course is a good way to learn the ground before you pay for an exam. For the detail, see WSET levels explained, how to become a sommelier and the best online wine courses.

A realistic timeline

  • First month: taste with the three questions, learn the main styles, and try five or six of the major grapes in pairs.
  • First three months: the dozen grapes you will meet everywhere, and the first two or three countries, so labels start to make sense.
  • First year: the main regions of the classic countries, food and wine, and how winemaking shapes taste. By now you can order from most wine lists with confidence.

Mistakes that slow people down

  • Memorising regions before tasting. Names without flavours fall out of your head.
  • Only drinking favourites. You learn most from the wine you would not have chosen.
  • Chasing scores and prices. An expensive bottle teaches no more than a good everyday one.
  • Waiting to feel ready. There is no wrong answer in tasting; there is only paying attention.

Questions & answers

How long does it take to learn about wine?

You can taste with confidence and read most labels after a few months of steady, light practice: a lesson or two a week and one bottle tasted with attention. Going deeper, into regions, producers and vintages, is a lifelong hobby, and that is part of the fun.

What is the best way to learn about wine for a beginner?

Taste in pairs and ask three questions of every glass, while you learn a little theory in a fixed order: tasting, the main styles, a dozen grapes, then the regions where they grow. A structured course keeps the order for you.

Can I learn about wine without drinking a lot?

Yes. Most of the theory needs no glass at all, small tasting pours teach as much as full glasses, and you can spit or share a bottle between several people. Learning is about attention, not quantity.

Is an online wine course worth it?

It is worth it if it is structured, short enough to keep up with and makes you test yourself. Pair it with real tasting: the course gives you the map, the glass makes it stick.

Do I need a wine certificate?

Only if you work in wine or want a formal qualification. For enjoying and choosing wine, a certificate is optional; a certificate course like WSET Level 1 or 2 suits people who want structure, an exam and a recognised result.
