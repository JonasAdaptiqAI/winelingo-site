Alternatives to Red Burgundy: Six Cheaper Bottles Worth Buying

What to drink when you love Pinot Noir and refuse to pay Burgundy prices — with an honest note on where each substitute stops short.

The best-value swap for red Burgundy is Cru Beaujolais — a Morgon or a Moulin-à-Vent from a named grower — with German Spätburgunder from the Ahr or Baden a close second. Both give the pale colour, mouth-watering acidity and savoury edge that make Burgundy worth chasing, at a fraction of the outlay.

Burgundy is expensive because of arithmetic, not greed. The region — Chablis down to the Mâconnais — covers about 32,300 hectares; Bordeaux, even after pulling out some 8,000 hectares of vines in 2024 alone, still farms nearly three times as much. Add global demand for Pinot Noir, and inheritance law that split the vineyards into rows rather than fields, and the price stops being negotiable. The question is not how to buy red Burgundy cheaply, but which wines do the same job.

The shortlist

Six that genuinely work, ordered by how close they land. Which suits you depends on what you love about Burgundy: the perfume, the acidity, or the savoury note of age.

Start here: Cru Beaujolais

A joke is buried in this recommendation. In July 1395 Philip the Bold, Duke of Burgundy, banished Gamay from his vineyards, denouncing the “disloyal Gaamez” and its “very great and horrible harshness”. The grape moved south to the granite hills of Beaujolais, and now makes the cheapest dependable route to a Burgundian sensation.

Skip Nouveau. Buy one of the ten crus — Morgon and Moulin-à-Vent are firmest and reward keeping, Fleurie is the most perfumed — and serve it at 15–17°C (59–63°F), not fridge-cold. What you do not get: the truffle-and-undergrowth complexity a village Burgundy grows into after a decade. Gamay stays fruit-driven to the end.

The closest match: German Spätburgunder

Germany is the world’s third-largest grower of Pinot Noir, behind France and the United States, and almost nobody outside Germany knows it. Under its local name, Spätburgunder covers about 11,500 hectares — 11% of the national vineyard.

Two addresses matter. The Ahr, a narrow slate valley north of the 50th parallel, gives 64% of its vines to the grape. Baden, warmer and much larger, has around 5,000 hectares and makes the rounder style. The caveat is supply: good estates are small, sell most of it at home, and price their top cuvées like village Burgundy. Buy the tier below the flagship.

Same shape, different grape

If what you love is Burgundy’s build — pale, high-acid, savoury, light on its feet — three southern reds get there by another road.

  • Etna Rosso. At least 80% Nerello Mascalese, grown on a live volcano. Sicily’s first DOC, in 1968, and the least Sicilian-tasting red on the island. Nearest in texture, furthest in flavour.
  • Naoussa. All Xinomavro, a name that translates as “sour black”. Pale as Burgundy, smelling of dried tomato and olive, then gripping like a young Barolo. Serve it with lamb, not turbot.
  • Bierzo. Mencía off slate slopes in north-west Spain; young reds must be at least 70% of it. DNA testing killed the old story that this was a lost Cabernet Franc clone — it is Portugal’s Jaen.

Two bottles to walk past

Cheap négociant Bourgogne Rouge. The bottom rung of the ladder is the worst value in France: thin, sharp, hollow, sold on one word. Move sideways instead: the Côte Chalonnaise has no Grand Cru vineyards and so no Grand Cru pricing, yet Mercurey holds 32 Premier Cru climats and Givry 38.

Bargain-bin Pinot Noir from hot places. Pinot is thin-skinned and sheds acidity early; picked warm and made cheaply it turns soft, jammy and faintly sweet — exactly the wine you are escaping. Nor are the famous New World names an answer to “cheaper”: Central Otago, over 80% planted to Pinot Noir, and Willamette Valley charge Burgundy-adjacent prices.

The verdict

Buy the Morgon this week. Find an Ahr Spätburgunder for the next roast. Keep a Naoussa for anything cooked long with lamb and tomato. And if the craving is for Burgundy the place, not the sensation, spend on one Côte Chalonnaise village bottle rather than three regional ones — Mercurey and Givry are the red-focused pair, both a shade over 80% red by planted area.

FAQ

Questions & answers

What is a good cheaper alternative to red Burgundy?

Cru Beaujolais is the best-value substitute — Morgon and Moulin-à-Vent give you pale colour, high acidity and a silky texture for far less. German Spätburgunder from the Ahr or Baden is the closest match of all, because it is the same grape in a similarly cool climate.

Is Beaujolais the same as Burgundy?

No. Beaujolais sits just south of Burgundy and is made from Gamay, not Pinot Noir — a grape the Duke of Burgundy banished from his vineyards in 1395. The wines share a pale, high-acid, low-tannin build, but Beaujolais stays fruit-driven where aged Burgundy turns earthy and savoury.

Where can I find cheap Pinot Noir that still tastes good?

Germany first: Spätburgunder covers about 11,500 hectares there and rarely carries a fashionable price tag. Chile and Alto Adige are worth a look too. Avoid the cheapest Pinot Noir from warm regions, which tends to taste soft, jammy and faintly sweet.

Why is red Burgundy so expensive?

Supply. The whole of Burgundy covers about 32,300 hectares, roughly a third of Bordeaux, and most of it is planted to white grapes — Chardonnay alone takes 57%. Holdings are tiny because inheritance law divided them across generations, so celebrated vineyards make only a few hundred cases a year against global demand.
