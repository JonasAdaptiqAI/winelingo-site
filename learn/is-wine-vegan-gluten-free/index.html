Is Wine Vegan? Is Wine Gluten-Free? Clear Answers and How to Check

Why some wines use egg, milk or fish in the cellar, why nearly all wine is gluten-free, and what the label can and cannot tell you.

By Jonas Egeskov · Updated 14 October 2026

Not all wine is vegan: it is made from grapes, but many wines are clarified with animal products such as egg white, milk protein, gelatine or a fish product called isinglass. Wine is almost always gluten-free: grapes contain no gluten, and major coeliac charities in the UK and US treat plain wine as safe on a gluten-free diet.

The detail behind both answers sits in one cellar step called fining, plus a few labelling rules that differ between the EU and the US. This guide explains what is actually used, how to spot a vegan bottle, where the small gluten questions come from, and the neutral facts on carbs and sulphites.

What makes wine not vegan?

The grapes are never the problem. The question is what happens in the cellar after fermentation. Young wine is cloudy with tiny particles: dead yeast, grape proteins and some harsher tannins, the compounds from skins and seeds that make a red feel drying.

Many winemakers speed up the clearing with fining. A fining agent is stirred into the wine, it binds to the unwanted particles, and the clumps sink to the bottom. The clear wine is drawn off, and you can see where this step fits in how wine is made.

Several traditional fining agents come from animals. Fresh egg whites have long been used to soften the tannins of fine red wines, Bordeaux being the classic example. Milk protein (casein), gelatine and isinglass, which is made from the dried swim bladders of fish, are also common. Most of the agent leaves with the sediment, but traces can remain, and most vegans avoid these wines either way.

Key takeaway: a non-vegan wine does not usually contain egg or fish as an ingredient. It was clarified with an animal product that is then mostly removed.

Ox blood powder was once used in parts of Europe. It has been banned in the EU since the late 1990s, and it is not on the list of materials US regulations authorise for treating wine.

The vegan options work well, which is why so many modern wines are vegan. Some winemakers skip fining altogether and label the wine unfined; it may look slightly hazy, which is harmless. Unfiltered is a different step, so an unfiltered wine may still have been fined.

How to tell if a wine is vegan

  • Vegan wording or a certification logo. The clearest sign: a vegan wine was made without animal products, and a certifying body's logo adds an independent check.
  • Allergen statements. In the EU, if egg or milk products were used for fining and can still be detected in the finished wine, the label must say so, with words such as "egg" or "milk protein". That tells you the wine is not vegan.
  • No allergen statement is not proof. Gelatine and isinglass never need declaring, and egg or milk traces below the detection limit need no warning either.
  • The producer or importer. Many publish fining details online or answer by email, the most reliable route for unlabelled wines.

If you have an egg, milk or fish allergy, the same applies: ask the producer how the wine was fined, and follow the advice of your own doctor.

Wines made for the EU since December 2023 must also give an ingredient list, often through a QR code on the back label. Fining agents count as processing aids rather than ingredients, so the list will not reveal gelatine or isinglass. In the US, the regulator (the TTB) proposed mandatory allergen labelling for alcoholic drinks in 2025, but for now such statements are voluntary. See how to read a wine label for the rest.

Is wine gluten-free?

Yes. Gluten is a protein found in wheat, barley and rye, and wine is fermented grape juice, so no grain is involved. This holds for red, white, rosé and sparkling wine, including Champagne and prosecco. The bready notes in some sparkling wines come from yeast, not grain.

Two small questions come up, and both turn out to be minor:

  • Wheat paste on barrels. Some coopers once sealed the heads of oak barrels with a flour paste. Wax has largely replaced it, and when two wines aged in wheat-paste-sealed barrels were tested in 2012, both came in under 10 parts per million (ppm) of gluten, below the 20 ppm limit used for gluten-free foods in the UK, EU and US.
  • Gluten as a fining agent. Wheat protein can be used for fining, but this is rare, and studies of wines fined this way found very little or no gluten left behind.

The real exceptions are products that are not just wine. Some wine coolers and flavoured wine drinks contain barley malt or added flavourings and colourings, so read the label on anything ready-mixed.

Key takeaway: plain wine is gluten-free. If you have coeliac disease, the bottles worth checking are flavoured wine drinks and coolers, not ordinary wine.

What "gluten-free" on a wine label means

Most producers never print the claim, so its absence means nothing. Where you do see it, rules apply.

In the US, the TTB lets a wine carry a gluten-free claim if it is made without any gluten-containing ingredients and the producer has taken steps to prevent cross-contact with gluten, including from storage materials such as barrels. Wines under 7% alcohol are regulated by the FDA instead, which uses the 20 ppm limit.

In the UK and EU, "gluten-free" on any food or drink means no more than 20 mg of gluten per kilogram, the same 20 ppm figure.

If you have coeliac disease or a wheat allergy and want certainty about a particular bottle, the producer can tell you exactly how it was made. Follow the guidance of your own doctor or dietitian, and your national coeliac charity, on what is right for you.

Is wine keto or low-carb? Plus sulphites

Carbs and sugar. In a dry red or dry white wine, yeast has turned almost all the grape sugar into alcohol. EU rules allow a still wine labelled dry to hold at most 4 g of sugar per litre, or up to 9 g/L with high acidity. At 4 g/L, a 150 ml glass holds at most 0.6 g of sugar. Nutrition databases list a glass at roughly 3 to 4 g of carbohydrate, because they count other dissolved substances along with sugar.

Many sweet wines hold well over 45 g/L. See low-sugar wine and the wine sweetness chart.

Alcohol itself is not a carbohydrate, but it does carry energy: about 7 kcal per gram. Check a glass with the wine calorie calculator, or read about wine alcohol content.

Sulphites. Sulphites protect wine from spoiling and are also made in small amounts by yeast during fermentation, so almost every wine contains some. A wine with more than 10 mg/L in the EU, or 10 mg/L or more in the US, must declare them on the label. A small minority of people are sensitive to them. They are not an animal product, so they do not affect whether a wine is vegan.

FAQ

Questions & answers

What makes wine not vegan?

Fining, a cellar step that clears the wine before bottling. Some fining agents come from animals: egg white, casein from milk, gelatine from animal skin and bones, and isinglass from fish swim bladders. Most of the agent is removed, but traces can remain. Wines clarified with clay, pea or potato protein, or not fined at all, can be vegan.

Is prosecco vegan?

Some prosecco is vegan and some is not. Like other wines, it may be fined with animal-derived agents such as casein, gelatine or isinglass, or with vegan alternatives such as bentonite clay or pea protein. Look for vegan wording or a certification logo on the label, or check the producer's website.

Is Champagne gluten-free?

Yes. Champagne is made from grapes, usually Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Pinot Meunier, and contains no wheat, barley or rye. Its toasty, bread-like aromas come from ageing on yeast, not from grain. Coeliac charities treat Champagne and other sparkling wines as gluten-free. For certainty about a specific bottle, ask the producer.

Is red wine gluten-free?

Yes. Red wine is fermented grape juice and naturally contains no gluten. The two issues sometimes raised, wheat paste once used to seal oak barrels and rare gluten-based fining, have been tested and leave little or no gluten, well below the 20 ppm limit used for gluten-free food. Flavoured wine drinks and coolers are different and may contain malt, so check their labels.

Is wine keto?

Dry wine contains very little sugar, because yeast has turned almost all of it into alcohol. Nutrition databases list a glass of dry red or white at roughly 3 to 4 g of carbohydrate. Sweet wines, dessert wines and many wine coolers contain far more. Alcohol also carries about 7 kcal per gram. Whether it fits any particular eating plan is a personal question to discuss with your own doctor or dietitian.

Does wine have carbs?

Yes, but dry wine has only a little. A dry still wine in the EU may contain at most 4 g of sugar per litre, or 9 g/L with high acidity, which is under 1.5 g in a 150 ml glass. Nutrition databases list a glass at roughly 3 to 4 g of carbohydrate in total. Sweet styles can contain many times more.

Is there gluten in wine?

Ordinary wine has no gluten, because it is made from grapes rather than grain. Tests on wines aged in barrels sealed with wheat paste found under 10 ppm, below the 20 ppm gluten-free limit, and most barrels are now sealed with wax. Products that are not plain wine, such as some coolers and flavoured wine drinks, can contain malt or other gluten sources.
