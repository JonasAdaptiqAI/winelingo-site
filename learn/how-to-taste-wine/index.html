How to Taste Wine: The 5 S’s (See, Swirl, Sniff, Sip, Savour)

A simple, friendly way to slow down and actually notice what’s in your glass — no special vocabulary required.

Tasting wine isn’t a test, and you don’t need a fancy vocabulary or a trained palate to do it well. It’s really just about slowing down for a minute and paying attention to what you already sense — colour, smell, and flavour. That’s it.

There’s a simple little framework that makes it easy to remember: the 5 S’s. See, Swirl, Sniff, Sip, Savour. Walk through them in order and you’ll notice far more than you would from a quick, distracted gulp. The whole thing takes under a minute, and you can do it with any wine, anywhere.

Here’s the most important part before we start: there are no wrong answers. If you smell strawberry and your friend smells cherry, you’re both right. Tasting is personal. This guide just gives you a gentle path to follow. (Wine is for adults of legal drinking age — enjoy it slowly and in moderation.)

1. See — look at the wine

Start with your eyes. Tip the glass slightly and look at the wine, ideally against something white like a napkin or tablecloth. You’re not judging anything yet — just noticing.

Colour tells a quiet story. In reds, a bright ruby or purple usually means a younger wine, while brick or garnet tones often mean it’s had some age. In whites, pale and almost watery hints at a light, fresh style, while deeper gold can mean more age, more richness, or time in oak. None of this is a pass-or-fail grade; it’s simply your first clue about what’s coming.

  • Tilt the glass over a white background and look at the colour and how deep or pale it is.
  • Notice the shade: purple and ruby reds skew younger; brick and garnet skew older.
  • Pale whites tend to be light and crisp; golden whites tend to be richer or older.
  • That’s all — no need to overthink it. Move on when you’ve had a look.

2. Swirl — wake the wine up

Give the glass a gentle swirl. The easiest trick: keep the base of the glass on the table and move it in small circles. Once that feels natural, you can lift and swirl in the air.

Swirling isn’t for show. It mixes air into the wine, which releases more of its aroma — so the next step, sniffing, gives you much more to work with. You might notice ‘legs’ or ‘tears’ sliding down the inside of the glass afterwards. Fun to watch, but they mostly relate to alcohol and sugar, not quality, so don’t read too much into them.

  • Keep the glass on the table and swirl in small circles a few times.
  • This adds air and lifts the aromas so you can smell more.
  • Swirl gently — a smaller pour and a slow hand keep things tidy.
  • ‘Legs’ running down the glass are normal; they don’t tell you if a wine is good.

3. Sniff — take a smell

Bring the glass to your nose and take a gentle sniff. Then take another. Your first sniff wakes up your senses; the second usually tells you more. Don’t chase the ‘correct’ smell — just ask yourself what it reminds you of.

Aromas often fall into easy families: fruit (berries, citrus, apple, tropical), flowers or herbs, and sometimes warmer notes like vanilla, spice, toast, or oak. Smell is where most of what we call ‘flavour’ actually happens, so this step matters more than people expect. If nothing jumps out at first, that’s completely fine — give it a moment and try again.

  • Take two short sniffs rather than one long one.
  • Ask a simple question: does this remind me of fruit, flowers, herbs, or something warm and spicy?
  • There’s no scorecard — whatever you smell is the right answer.
  • Coming up blank? Swirl again and re-sniff; aromas open up with a little air and time.

4. Sip — taste it properly

Now the good part. Take a small sip and, instead of swallowing straight away, let the wine roll around your whole mouth for a few seconds. Different parts of your mouth pick up different things, so give it a chance to spread out.

As it sits there, notice a few basics. Is it sweet or dry (dry just means ‘not sweet’)? Does it make your mouth water — that mouth-watering tang is acidity, and it’s what makes wine feel fresh. In reds, do your gums or tongue feel a slight grip or dryness? That’s tannin, the gentle grippy feeling that also shows up in strong tea. And is the wine light and delicate or big and full in your mouth? That’s its body.

  • Take a small sip and hold it in your mouth for around 3 to 5 seconds before swallowing.
  • Sweet or dry? Dry simply means the wine isn’t sweet.
  • Mouth-watering freshness is acidity; a grippy, tea-like feel in reds is tannin.
  • Does it feel light, medium, or full? That’s the wine’s body — all of it is normal.

5. Savour — notice the finish

After you swallow, pause. The taste that lingers is called the finish, and noticing it is the whole point of this last step. Count quietly: does the flavour disappear almost at once, or hang around for several seconds?

A longer finish is often a sign of a more complex wine, but a short finish can be lovely and refreshing too — think of an easy weeknight white. Ask yourself the friendliest question of all: did I enjoy that? Would I want another sip? Your honest reaction is the most useful tasting note you’ll ever have.

  • After swallowing, notice how long the flavour stays with you — that’s the finish.
  • Longer finishes often point to more complexity, but short and refreshing is great too.
  • Check whether the taste changes as it fades, or stays the same.
  • Most importantly: did you like it? That answer is always valid.

Putting it all together

Strung together, the whole thing is quick and natural: See the colour, Swirl to release the aromas, Sniff to see what it reminds you of, Sip and let it fill your mouth, then Savour the finish and how much you liked it. Under a minute, start to finish.

You don’t need to do all five every time, either. On a relaxed evening you might just swirl, sniff, and enjoy. The 5 S’s are a helpful structure when you want to pay closer attention — not a ritual you’re obliged to perform. The more you do it, the more automatic it becomes, and the more words you’ll naturally find for what you’re tasting.

You really can’t get this wrong

Here’s the reassuring truth: tasting is personal, and your palate is your own. Two people can taste the same wine and describe it completely differently, and neither is mistaken. If you taste blackcurrant where the label says ‘plum’, trust yourself — you’re building your own vocabulary, and that’s exactly the point.

Beginners often worry about using the ‘wrong’ words or missing something an expert would catch. But even professionals mostly rely on the same simple senses you already have. The only real skill is attention, and you’ve been practising that since your first bite of food.

So pour a glass, take your time, and enjoy the small ritual of noticing. Wine is meant to be a pleasure, not an exam. And, as always, savour it responsibly — a little, slowly, and only if you’re of legal drinking age.

FAQ

Questions & answers

Why do you swirl wine before smelling it?

Swirling mixes air into the wine, which releases more of its aromas. Since most of what we think of as ‘flavour’ comes from smell, a quick swirl gives your nose much more to notice on the next sniff. Keep the glass on the table and swirl in small circles if that feels easier.

How long should you hold wine in your mouth?

About 3 to 5 seconds is plenty. Let the wine spread across your whole mouth rather than swallowing straight away, so you can pick up its sweetness, freshness (acidity), any grippy texture (tannin), and how light or full it feels. There’s no need to swish dramatically — a gentle roll around does the job.

Do I need to spit the wine out?

Not at all, unless you’re tasting many wines in a row and want to stay clear-headed — that’s when professionals spit. For enjoying a glass at home or with dinner, simply swallow and notice the finish. If you’re tasting several wines, spitting or just taking small sips helps you pace yourself sensibly.

What are wine ‘legs’ and do they matter?

Legs, sometimes called tears, are the streaks that run down the inside of the glass after you swirl. They’re mostly related to the wine’s alcohol and sugar, not its quality. They’re interesting to watch, but you can happily ignore them when deciding whether you like a wine.

What if I can’t smell or taste anything specific?

That’s completely normal, especially at first. Try swirling again and taking two short sniffs instead of one long one, and give it a moment — aromas open up with air and time. Start with broad families like ‘fruity’, ‘fresh’, or ‘earthy’ rather than hunting for exact notes. It gets easier every time you practise.

Do I need special glasses or expensive wine to taste properly?

No. Any clean glass works, though a rounded shape that narrows at the top helps gather the aromas. And you can practise the 5 S’s on any wine at any price — an everyday bottle is a perfectly good teacher. The skill is in paying attention, not in what you spend.
