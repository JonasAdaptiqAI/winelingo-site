Dry White Wine: Which Whites Are Dry, and Which to Cook With

A plain guide to dry white wine: what dry means, which whites are dry, how to spot sweeter bottles and what to use in the kitchen.

By Jonas Egeskov · Updated 14 October 2026

A dry white wine is a white wine with little or no sugar left in it, usually no more than about 4 grams per litre, so it tastes crisp rather than sweet. Most everyday whites are dry, including Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Grigio, Chablis, Albariño and Grüner Veltliner, and any of these also works well for cooking.

This guide explains what makes a white wine dry, lists the dry whites worth knowing, flags the ones that are often sweeter than people expect, and shows you how to tell from the label. The last part covers which dry white wine to buy for cooking and what to use if you have none in the house.

What is a dry white wine?

All wine starts as sweet grape juice. During fermentation, yeast eats the grape sugar and turns it into alcohol. When the yeast is allowed to finish the job, almost no sugar is left and the wine is dry. Whatever sugar does remain is called residual sugar.

European wine law puts numbers on this. A still wine counts as dry with up to 4 g/L of residual sugar, or up to 9 g/L if it has high acidity, because acidity makes sugar taste less obvious. Medium dry goes up to 12 g/L (18 g/L with high acidity), medium sweet up to 45 g/L, and anything above that is sweet. Our wine sweetness chart lines these levels up side by side.

One common mix-up: fruity is not the same as sweet. A ripe Sauvignon Blanc can smell of passion fruit and still be completely dry. Sweetness is something you taste on your tongue; fruitiness is mostly smell.

Quick answer: if a white wine has 4 g/L of sugar or less (up to 9 g/L when it is very fresh and acidic), it is dry. Most supermarket whites made from Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Grigio or Chardonnay fall into this group.

List of dry white wines

These are the dry whites you will meet most often.

Sauvignon Blanc

Taste: lime, green apple, gooseberry, cut grass.

Sauvignon Blanc is almost always dry, with sharp acidity. Loire versions such as Sancerre are leaner and more mineral, while New Zealand bottles are more tropical.

Pinot Grigio

Taste: lemon, pear, a light almond note.

Italian Pinot Grigio is usually dry, light and fairly neutral, which makes it easy to drink and easy to cook with. The same grape from Alsace, labelled Pinot Gris, is richer and sometimes off-dry.

Chablis and unoaked Chardonnay

Taste: green apple, lemon, a flinty, chalky edge.

Chablis is Chardonnay from the cool north of Burgundy, and most of it sees little or no new oak. Oaked Chardonnay is also dry, even though vanilla and butter flavours can make it seem sweeter.

Albariño

Taste: peach, lime, a salty sea-breeze finish.

Albariño comes mainly from Rías Baixas in north-west Spain. It is dry, fresh and lightly aromatic, and a natural match for seafood.

Grüner Veltliner

Taste: lime, green pear, a pinch of white pepper.

Grüner Veltliner is Austria's most planted grape. It is usually dry, with a light peppery note that sets it apart.

Muscadet

Taste: lemon, sea salt, a bready hint.

Made from Melon de Bourgogne near Nantes in the Loire Valley, Muscadet is one of the driest, lightest whites around. Bottles marked sur lie have rested on their lees (spent yeast), which adds a soft, bready texture.

Assyrtiko

Taste: lemon, salt, wet stone, very high acidity.

Assyrtiko is the great white grape of Santorini in Greece. It keeps its fresh acidity even in a hot climate and is almost always dry.

Dry Riesling (trocken)

Taste: lime, green apple, white flowers.

Riesling can be anything from bone dry to very sweet, so look for the word trocken, German for dry. Most Alsace and Australian Riesling is also made dry.

Vermentino

Taste: lemon, fresh herbs, a bitter-almond finish.

Vermentino grows in Sardinia, Liguria and along the Tuscan coast, and in Provence under the name Rolle. It is dry, bright and gently herbal.

White Rioja

Taste: lemon and green apple when young; nuts and honeyed notes when oak-aged.

White Rioja is made mainly from Viura, also known as Macabeo. Modern versions are fresh and unoaked, while traditional ones spend years in barrel. Both are dry.

White wines that are often not dry

Some whites are sweet by design; others come in several styles under one name.

  • Moscato. Moscato d'Asti, made from the Moscato grape, is gently fizzy, sweet and low in alcohol, often around 5 to 6%. Dry Muscat exists, notably in Alsace, but most bottles labelled Moscato are sweet.
  • Gewürztraminer. Many Gewürztraminer wines, especially from Alsace, keep some sugar to balance their low acidity and perfumed lychee and rose flavours. Some are fully dry, many are off-dry.
  • Riesling. German Riesling labelled Kabinett, Spätlese or Auslese without the word trocken often has noticeable sweetness.
  • Chenin Blanc and Vouvray. Chenin Blanc from Vouvray in the Loire can be sec (dry), demi-sec (off-dry), moelleux (sweet) or doux (very sweet). South African Chenin is usually dry.
  • Dessert wines. Sauternes, late harvest wines and ice wine are sweet on purpose. If that is what you are after, see our sweet wines for beginners.

How to tell if a white wine is dry from the label

Most dry wines do not say "dry" on the bottle, because dry is the default. Sweeter wines are the ones that usually give clues. Here is what to look for, and our guide on how to read a wine label goes further.

  • Words that mean dry: sec (French, on still wine), secco (Italian), seco (Spanish and Portuguese), trocken (German).
  • Words that mean off-dry or sweet: demi-sec, moelleux, doux, halbtrocken, feinherb, amabile, abboccato, dolce, late harvest, Spätlese, Auslese.
  • Alcohol level: a still white below about 10 or 11% alcohol often has unfermented sugar left in it, because the yeast was stopped before it finished. This is a hint, not a rule.
  • Riesling scale: some Riesling back labels carry a small scale running from dry to sweet, with a marker showing where the wine sits.
  • Sparkling terms: on fizz, Brut means under 12 g/L of sugar and Brut Nature under 3 g/L. Confusingly, Extra Dry (12 to 17 g/L) and Sec (17 to 32 g/L) are both sweeter than Brut, and Demi-sec (32 to 50 g/L) is clearly sweet.

Dry white wine for cooking

When a recipe asks for dry white wine, it wants acidity and a little fruit to lift a sauce, without adding sweetness. It is used to deglaze a pan (dissolving the browned bits stuck to the bottom), steam mussels and build risotto.

What to buy. Choose a crisp, unoaked, clearly dry white. Pinot Grigio, Sauvignon Blanc, unoaked Chardonnay, Muscadet and Vermentino are all reliable. The best dry white wine for cooking is simply one you would happily drink a glass of, because any off flavours will be concentrated as the wine reduces. There is no need for a special bottle: the fine details of an expensive wine are lost in the heat.

What to avoid.

  • Heavily oaked Chardonnay. Strong oak and butter flavours can taste heavy, and sometimes bitter, once reduced.
  • Sweet or off-dry whites. Reduction concentrates sugar as well as flavour, so a sweet wine can make a savoury sauce taste oddly sugary, unless the recipe asks for it.
  • Products labelled "cooking wine". These usually contain added salt, and often preservatives, which makes them hard to season around and unpleasant to taste on their own.

How much does it matter? More in quick dishes than slow ones. In a pan sauce, a risotto or a pot of mussels, the wine's flavour stays front and centre, so a fresh, clean wine pays off. In a long braise with stock, herbs and vegetables, the exact bottle matters far less. Let the wine simmer for at least a few minutes after adding it so the raw, sharp alcohol edge cooks off; some alcohol still remains in the dish, even after long cooking.

Rule of thumb: cook with a crisp, unoaked, dry white you would drink, and drink the rest of the bottle with the meal. Leftover wine can be frozen in an ice cube tray for future sauces.

Dry white wine substitutes

If you have no wine at home, or prefer to cook without it, these swaps are widely used. Replace the wine with the same amount of liquid unless noted, then taste and adjust.

  • Stock plus lemon juice or white wine vinegar. The most common substitute. Use chicken or vegetable stock, and add a teaspoon or two of lemon juice or white wine vinegar for roughly every 250 ml to bring back the acidity. Start small and add more to taste.
  • Dry vermouth. A fortified, herb-flavoured white wine. It has more alcohol and a stronger flavour than table wine, so you may want to use a little less. An opened bottle keeps much longer than wine, especially in the fridge, which is why many cooks keep one for sauces.
  • White wine vinegar diluted with water. Vinegar is far sharper than wine, so use a small splash in water or stock rather than replacing the wine one for one.
  • White grape juice with a squeeze of lemon. Works in a pinch, but grape juice is sweet, so use it only where a little sweetness will not hurt.
  • Non-alcoholic white wine. Closest in flavour to the real thing, though some are sweeter than dry wine, so check the label.

For risotto, extra stock and a squeeze of lemon at the end does the job well.

Choosing a dry white wine to drink

The best dry white wine depends on what you like. For something light and zesty, try Sauvignon Blanc, Albariño or Muscadet. For more texture, look at Chablis, Assyrtiko or an oak-aged white Rioja.

Serve light dry whites well chilled at 7–10°C, and fuller, oaked whites a little warmer at 10–13°C, so their flavours show. Wine is for adults of legal drinking age and is best enjoyed in moderation.

FAQ

Questions & answers

What is considered a dry white wine?

A white wine is considered dry when it has very little residual sugar, the grape sugar left after fermentation. Under EU rules, a still wine is dry with up to 4 grams of sugar per litre, or up to 9 grams per litre if its acidity is high. Most Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Grigio, Chablis, Albariño and Grüner Veltliner sit comfortably in this range and taste crisp rather than sweet.

Is Sauvignon Blanc a dry wine?

Yes. Sauvignon Blanc is almost always made dry, with crisp acidity and flavours of lime, green apple, gooseberry and cut grass. Riper styles, such as many from New Zealand, can smell of passion fruit and taste very fruity, but fruitiness is not the same as sweetness. Sweet wines made with Sauvignon Blanc do exist, notably Sauternes, where it is blended with Sémillon, but they are clearly labelled as dessert wines.

Is Pinot Grigio dry?

Usually, yes. Italian Pinot Grigio is typically dry, light bodied and fairly neutral, with lemon, pear and a hint of almond. That makes it one of the safest choices when a recipe asks for dry white wine. The same grape from Alsace, sold as Pinot Gris, tends to be richer and is sometimes off-dry, so check the label for the grape name and region.

Is Chardonnay dry?

Yes, almost all Chardonnay is dry. Unoaked styles such as Chablis taste of green apple and lemon, while oaked Chardonnay adds vanilla, butter and toast. Those rich flavours can trick your brain into sensing sweetness, but the sugar level is still low. For cooking, unoaked Chardonnay works better, because strong oak flavours can turn heavy or bitter when the wine is reduced in a sauce.

What is the best dry white wine for cooking?

Choose a crisp, unoaked, dry white that you would also enjoy drinking. Pinot Grigio, Sauvignon Blanc, unoaked Chardonnay, Muscadet and Vermentino are all reliable. Avoid heavily oaked wines, sweet or off-dry whites, and supermarket products labelled cooking wine, which usually contain added salt. You do not need an expensive bottle, because the fine details of a wine are lost during cooking.

What can I substitute for dry white wine?

The most common substitute is chicken or vegetable stock with a teaspoon or two of lemon juice or white wine vinegar for roughly every 250 ml, which replaces the wine's acidity. Dry vermouth is another good option and keeps much longer once opened, though it has more flavour and alcohol, so use a little less. Non-alcoholic white wine also works if it is not too sweet.
