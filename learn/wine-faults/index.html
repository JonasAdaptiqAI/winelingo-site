Is My Wine Corked? How to Spot a Faulty Bottle

What corked, oxidised, reduced and heat-damaged wine each smell like — and which one fixes itself in fifteen minutes.

If the fruit has vanished and the wine smells of damp cardboard, a wet dog or a cellar that needs airing, it is corked. Cork taint never blows off. Pour it away and open something else.

Corked is the only fault most people can name, so it gets blamed for all the others. You are as likely to have a bottle that cooked in a delivery van, or one that needs fifteen minutes of air.

Run the thirty-second check

Do these in order; how to taste wine covers step four.

  1. Check the capsule. A cork standing proud, or a sticky trickle under the foil, means the bottle got hot enough to push wine past it.
  2. Smell it before you swirl. Musty, vinegary and rotten-egg notes are loudest in the first sniff.
  3. Swirl hard, then smell again. If a struck-match or drains note fades, that was reduction, and the wine is sound.
  4. Sip, and hunt for the fruit. If there is none — just something short and papery — suspect cork taint, even with nothing on the nose.

Corked wine works by subtraction

Cork taint is 2,4,6-trichloroanisole, or TCA, formed when moulds meet chlorine residues in cork bark, barrel staves or cellar timber. Most people start to notice it somewhere around one or two nanograms per litre — parts per trillion.

Here is the useful part, and it is stranger than the usual explanation. Takeuchi, Kato and Kurahashi, writing in PNAS in 2013, recorded single olfactory receptor cells and found that TCA evokes no odorant response of its own. It suppresses the cyclic nucleotide-gated channels those cells fire through — and it did so at attomolar concentrations, far below anything you could taste. Cork taint is not a smell added to the wine. It is your nose being turned down. The wet-dog stink is the severe case. The common one is a wine that seems oddly quiet and finishes early — you sniff, think “fine, I suppose”, and drink it anyway. It is a packaging failure, not a bad producer: the next bottle is usually lovely.

Light strike is riboflavin attacking methionine under bright shop lighting, throwing off sulphur compounds that taste of cooked cabbage; Champagne and pale rosé in flint glass suffer worst. Brett is a judgement rather than a fault: a trace in an old-school Northern Rhône red is character, but once you cannot find the fruit under the farmyard, send it back.

Four things that are not faults

  • Tartrate crystals. The glitter under the cork is potassium bitartrate, which drops out of solution below roughly 10 °C (50 °F) — in practice, a spell in the fridge or a cold garage, not the bottle ageing. Harmless, and often a sign the wine was not filtered to death.
  • Sediment in an old red. Colour and tannin dropping out with age. Stand it upright a day ahead and decant.
  • A stained, crumbly or two-piece cork. Corks are bark; they stain, and they break on a tired corkscrew. Neither tells you anything about the wine — smell the wine, never the cork.
  • A bottle opened on Sunday and poured on Wednesday. Ordinary oxidation on the usual timetable, not a defect.

Sending it back without a scene

Ask rather than announce. “Would you mind smelling this? I think it might be corked.” That hands the sommelier the decision, and a good one replaces it without debate. Say it at the tasting pour, not two glasses in. That pour checks for faults, not for whether you like the wine: a sound bottle you dislike is still yours, so the brief you give before ordering matters more.

In a shop, keep the cork and most of the wine, and take the receipt. The Consumer Rights Act 2015 gives you 30 days to reject faulty goods outright and take the cash back — but read the clock carefully, because it starts the day the bottle is delivered to you, not the day you pull the cork. Buy a case to lay down and the short-term right is long gone by the time you find the fault. You are not out of luck: for six months after delivery the law presumes the fault was there all along, so the burden is on the shop to show otherwise — it just means a replacement rather than a refund on demand. Finishing it first never works.

FAQ

Questions & answers

What does corked wine smell like?

Damp cardboard, a wet dog, or a mouldy cellar — but that is the heavy end, and it is the version everyone knows. Mild cork taint is far more common and much harder to name, because it has no signature smell at all: the wine simply tastes flat, short and stripped of fruit. TCA works mostly by subtraction, muting what the wine already had rather than laying something new on top of it.

How common is corked wine?

Nobody agrees, which is itself the interesting part. Published estimates have ranged from well under one per cent to several per cent, and they disagree because the counters disagree: cork producers and wine critics are measuring different bottles, and, more importantly, no two panels draw the line at the same concentration. Every estimate is also biased low for the reason above — taint that merely flattens a wine gets recorded as a dull bottle, not a faulty one. Assume roughly one bottle in a hundred and you will not be far out.

Is corked wine safe to drink?

Yes. TCA ruins the aroma at parts per trillion, concentrations far too small to do you any harm. The only reason to reject a corked bottle is that it tastes of very little, so there is nothing to be gained by persevering.

Can wine with a screwcap be corked?

Rarely, but yes. TCA also forms in barrel staves, pallets and cellar timber, so a screwcap closes the main route in rather than every route. Screwcapped wines are instead a little more prone to reduction, which a hard swirl or a decanter usually clears.
